# Chapter 1B — Nuclear And Existential-Risk Responsibility

Status: **draft; v5 objective-evidence questions require controlled rejudgment**

## Chapter Identity

- Chapter: `1B`
- Rating: nuclear and global existential-risk responsibility
- Evidence strategy: local-first structured evidence plus cited manual research
- Rubric version: `chapter_1b_v5`
- Output: one chapter score (`1`–`10`) and one confidence assessment per ruler-period

## Purpose And Non-Goals

This chapter asks whether the ruler used the risk-bearing powers actually available
to them responsibly, reduced catastrophic danger, and left a safer posture. A high
score means prudent restraint, credible safeguards, good-faith risk reduction, and
responsible crisis behavior. A low score means reckless escalation, proliferation,
unsafe control, coercive nuclear shielding, or knowingly increased catastrophic risk.

It does not rank rulers by arsenal size alone, reward non-nuclear rulers automatically,
or penalize a ruler merely because they inherited a dangerous posture. Conventional
military strength, generic environmental policy, prestige, and peaceful rhetoric are
not substitutes for demonstrated existential-risk choices. Non-nuclear cyber,
biological, or AI policy belongs here only where a credible catastrophic pathway and
ruler-level choice are shown. The client matrix is never evidence.

## Researcher Guidance

Follow the local-first researcher guide. Start with the resolved ruler-period and local
D17/FAS facts, then check SIPRI and FAS narrative material. Search by themes rather
than issuing ten repetitive searches: inherited capability and authority; arsenal and
doctrine change; safeguards/command systems; treaties and inspections; threats and
crises; proliferation relationships; expert appointments; catastrophic dual-use policy;
and end-of-period posture.

Prefer IAEA, UNODA/UN treaty records, CTBTO, treaty inspection bodies, declassified or
contemporaneous official records, SIPRI/FAS technical assessments, legislative or
inquiry records, serious scholarship, and reputable reporting. Official claims about
restraint or safety need independent corroboration; official texts remain strong for
formal doctrine, appointments, treaty acts, and dated statements. Preserve contrary
evidence and uncertainty. Do not infer responsible rhetoric from silence or safety from
the absence of reported accidents.

Record reusable evidence once and point it to every materially relevant lens. Separate
target-year evidence, broader ruler-period evidence, inherited baseline, and later
retrospective evidence. The researcher describes evidence and attribution but does not
assign the final chapter score.

## Sparse, Historical, And Closed-Regime Evidence

For non-nuclear rulers with no plausible authority or exposure, document that limited
exposure; do not manufacture activity or award an automatic `10`. For older cases use
archives, treaty records, memoirs cautiously, cabinet/command histories, and later
scholarship, marking retrospective and contested claims. In closed regimes, missing
reporting is weak evidence: triangulate external monitoring, satellite/technical
assessments, defectors only with corroboration, and adversarial-source caveats. A thin
record may still support a cautious chapter score when the known exposure and choices
are clear; otherwise widen the confidence interval or flag review.

Classify exposure before scoring as `material_exposure`,
`limited_exposure_with_choices`, `no_discriminating_opportunity`, or
`unknown_due_to_evidence_gap`. The last category normally produces a null and a
targeted research continuation. `No_discriminating_opportunity` also produces a null:
it documents why the ruler cannot be discriminatingly scored and must not be ranked
against materially exposed rulers. Generic multilateralism,
civil nuclear phase-out, or the absence of nuclear rhetoric is contextual unless tied
to a ruler-attributed catastrophic-risk choice.

## Attribution And Inherited Baseline

Establish what authority the ruler personally held over doctrine, forces, diplomacy,
appointments, and crisis decisions. Quarantine acts of predecessors, military bodies,
coalition partners, or dominant patrons unless adoption, direction, knowing tolerance,
or failure to use available corrective authority is evidenced. Judge change from the
inherited posture alongside absolute risk: inheriting a huge arsenal is not itself
culpability, while expanding or recklessly using it is; prudent reduction deserves
credit even when the remaining stockpile is large.

## Chapter-Level 1–10 Rubric

| Score | Chapter anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | The ruler authorized or caused an actual nuclear detonation against people or a military target, or deliberately caused an equivalent realized nuclear catastrophe. Actual use establishes eligibility for the floor; context, target, scale, alternatives, and restraint still determine whether 1 is warranted. |
| 2 | The ruler moved credibly toward nuclear use or created an acute near-use danger through operational preparation, direct brinkmanship, extremely unsafe command, or sustained nuclear coercion during war, without a realized nuclear detonation. |
| 3 | Severe nuclear risk increase through doctrine expansion, deployment, proliferation, repeated threats, safeguard abandonment, or destabilizing exercises, but without demonstrated near-use. |
| 4–5 | Mixed or weak responsibility: meaningful restraint in some areas but consequential escalation, obstruction, unsafe governance, or deterioration in others. |
| 6–7 | Generally responsible risk management and some tangible risk reduction, with notable omissions, ambiguity, inherited constraints, or inconsistent conduct. |
| 8–9 | Strong, sustained, and consequential restraint, safeguards, diplomacy, expert governance, and crisis prudence that materially improved the posture. |
| 10 | Exceptional, durable, and well-evidenced leadership that substantially reduced catastrophic risk across the ruler's actual sphere of authority while resisting powerful contrary incentives. |

The lenses are qualitatively and non-equally weighted. Crisis escalation, actual
weapon use, proliferation, and command safety can dominate several weaker signals.
No arithmetic average, equal ten-percent weighting, or minimum lens count is required.
Exposure matters: the judge evaluates available choices, consequences, and authority.
Scoring is opportunity-adjusted. Limited exposure or authority is not automatically a
high or low score. A ruler may earn a high score when meaningful risk-bearing choices
arose and the ruler consistently exercised responsible restraint within their actual
authority. When no discriminating opportunity exists, return null with an explicit
exposure assessment; do not manufacture excellence, blame, or a midpoint from mere
non-exposure.

Scores of 6 or above require a direct ruler-attributed risk-bearing choice. Scores of
8 or above additionally require consequential improvement or restraint supported by
multiple independent source types. Country posture or silence alone cannot support a
score.

### Absolute-scale guardrails

The annual cohort does not define the endpoints. The worst ruler in a current batch
must not receive 1 unless the realized-use floor is met. Nuclear threats, coercive
shielding of conventional war, doctrine hardening, exercises, deployments, or arsenal
growth can be profoundly irresponsible while remaining in bands 2 or 3. Classify each
record before scoring as `realized_use_or_catastrophe`,
`credible_near_use_or_operational_preparation`,
`severe_escalation_without_near_use`, `mixed_risk_management`,
`generally_responsible_management`, `strong_consequential_risk_reduction`, or
`exceptional_durable_risk_reduction`.

A rationale may not describe threats, exercises, deployments, or doctrine change as
"maximally dangerous" merely because no worse case appears in the annual cohort. Since
no nuclear weapon has been detonated in war after 1945, a modern annual batch can
properly contain no score of 1.

The general historical-extreme analogy (including references to Nazi-regime-level
conduct) does not replace this chapter-specific test. Regime cruelty, repression, or
aggression alone cannot establish the 1B floor: the ruler must meet the realized nuclear
use or equivalent realized-catastrophe condition above.

## Judge Workflow Across Rulers

### V4 direct-evidence and absolute-floor gate

A numeric score requires a target-period choice, action, restraint, remedy, or
outcome within the ruler's actual nuclear or existential-risk authority. Generic
nuclear posture, inherited arsenals, speeches, and pre- or post-period treaties are
context only. If no such target-period fact exists, return null; do not manufacture
a neutral or favorable score from low exposure.

One Chapter 1B judge applies this rubric across all eligible rulers in the comparison
batch. The judge first inventories exposure and inherited posture, reads every dossier,
identifies common anchors and edge cases, provisionally orders rulers, then assigns one
chapter score and confidence to each. It must explain the decisive evidence, meaningful
counterevidence, attribution, change from baseline, and why adjacent higher/lower bands
were rejected. Revisit the full ordering after scoring; use overlapping anchor cases if
context limits require shards. Preserve citations and flag research gaps without
turning formatting differences into substantive failures.

## Minimum Evidence And Graceful Degradation

A score needs at least one traceable, relevant source and a stated structured prior or
`not_available`, plus an explicit exposure/authority and temporal-fit assessment.
High-confidence extreme scores normally require multiple independent source types and
evidence of consequential conduct. Sparse lenses, unknown appointments, or unavailable
internal control details lower confidence rather than mechanically lower the score.
If identity, core attribution, or citation authenticity is unresolved, withhold the
score for review; ordinary incomplete coverage is not a publication blocker.

## Common Evidence-Item Semantics

Each material evidence item should carry a stable evidence ID; a concise factual claim;
source URL, title, publisher, and publication/access date where available; source type
and confidence with a short reason; a quotation or precise locator when available;
target-year/ruler-period fit; ruler attribution and inherited-baseline relevance; its
positive, negative, mixed, contextual, or contrary role; and the supported lens IDs.
Fields may be normalized later and natural-language equivalents are acceptable. The
substantive minimum is a traceable source, intelligible claim, temporal fit, attribution,
and evidence role. One item may support multiple lenses without duplication.

## Bias And Comparability Checks

Check visibility and secrecy, nuclear/non-nuclear exposure, great-power attention,
survivorship and hindsight, adversarial intelligence claims, rhetoric-versus-capability,
population/state-size irrelevance, inherited arsenals, crisis opportunity, source type,
recency, and consistent cross-ruler calibration. Do not equate report volume with risk,
treat a treaty signature as implementation, or reward a ruler for lacking capacity they
never sought or controlled.

Amnesty International reviewed their responses, which are annexed to this report, and updated its findings accordingly.\nback to top\nBackground to the Carnage\nAFP via Getty Images\nArmed Conflict, Foreign Intervention and Popular Uprising\nThe UN Stabilization Mission in the DRC, MONUSCO, deployed since 1999,\n[4]\nhas often been unable to protect civilians from attacks by armed groups, including M23, and sometimes by the Congolese army itself.\n[5]\nWhen he came to power in 2019, DRC’s President Félix Tshisekedi pledged to improve security and civilian protection in eastern DRC.\n[6]\nOpting for an essentially militaristic approach, he gave a more prominent role to the Congolese army and sought military interventions from countries in the region, while criticizing MONUSCO and pushing for its withdrawal.\n[7]\nHe declared a “State of siege” (similar to a state of emergency) in Ituri and North Kivu provinces in May 2021, granting extraordinary powers to the army and the police, claiming it was a means to rapidly restore security.\n[8]\nThe measure was continually extended and used by the army and police to undermine human rights.\n[9]\nLater that year, the Tshisekedi administration authorized the intervention of the Ugandan army in parts of North Kivu and Ituri,\n[10]\nand, in June 2022, that of the East African Community Regional Force (EACRF).\n[11]\nIn May 2023, after criticizing the failure of the EACRF,\n[12]\nhe requested the intervention of the Southern African Development Community (SADC), which was deployed to North Kivu in December 2023.\n[13]\n\nThe DRC government has openly mobilized local armed groups\n[14]\nand resorted to private military companies\n[15]\nto support the army since 2023, amid persistent war rhetoric between the DRC and Rwanda. Congolese authorities accuse Rwanda of aggression and sponsoring M23 rebels.\n[16]\nThe M23 rebels have occupied parts of North Kivu province since November 2021, despite regional and international efforts for disarmament.\n[17]\nFaced with persistent insecurity despite interventions by the UN and regional forces, the population has regularly protested against the presence of these forces. During anti-MONUSCO protests in Goma and other towns in July 2022, security forces killed at least 32 people, while four UN peacekeepers were killed by protesters, according to the government.\n[18]\nThis is the context in which FNJMN emerged in early 2023. Although the political and religious group’s statements and actions began to be widely publicized around June 2023, it had been legally registered as a non-profit association and operating in Goma since 2019, according to official registration documents seen by Amnesty International and testimonies from members of the group.\n[19]\nIt advocates a return to traditional Congolese and African spiritual practices and “rejection of imperialism in all its guises”.\n[20]\nFollowers of this group are called Wazalendo, which means “patriots” in Swahili.\n[21]\nBefore its protest planned for 30 August 2023, the group held three peaceful protests in Goma on 30 June, 30 July and 2 August 2023.\n[22]\nThese protests, at least two of which were at MONUSCO’s headquarters, took place without violence\n.\n[23]\nPlans for Protest and Authorities’ Reaction\nDuring previous protests in June and July, Efraimu Bisimwa, Wazalendo’s leader, announced a “major protest for 30 August” to demand MONUSCO’s departure. For weeks, he repeated this call via local radio stations, social media, and during the group’s worship services, mobilizing his followers and the public to participate.\n[24]\nOn 19 August, he wrote to the Mayor of Goma to formally give notice about the planned protest as required by Congolese law.\n\n[25]\nHe said: “As usual, it will be a silent and peaceful march. We will depart from our temple and head to the MONUSCO headquarters. Our ancestors will protect us from anyone who might be tempted to attack us.”\n[26]\nMONUSCO’s Request to Prohibit Protest\nOn 21 August, the Head of MONUSCO’s Goma Office wrote to the North Kivu Governor Lieutenant-General Constant Ndima, expressing concerns about the planned protest and urging him to prevent it from taking place.\n[27]\nThe letter stated: “the [Wazalendo’s] call is “a clear incitement to violence against MONUSCO and also against the authorities if they were to interfere with this mobilization. […]. We remain concerned about the motivations of these ‘Wazalendo’ and the FNJMN in particular, as well as the security risks that their actions could pose not only to our bases and staff but also to the Congolese security services and the established authorities.”\n[28]\nIn what appears to be an implicit reference to the conflict with M23 and DRC’s tensions with Rwanda, she went on to reference “a manipulation” which could “create instability and vulnerability that will be difficult to contain at a time when your own efforts are aimed at containing insecurity and anticipating threats in the province”.\n[29]\nThe letter requested the governor’s “firm intervention” with the Wazalendo “to prevent any threats against MONUSCO bases and staff”, and urged him to prohibit the protest “given its hateful and violent nature”.\n[30]\nA letter sent by the head of MONUSCO’s Goma office to the governor of North Kivu on 21 August 2023. Highlights and translations added by Amnesty International.\nOn 28 August, the governor wrote back to MONUSCO’s Head of Goma Office, reassuring her that he had instructed the security forces “to make the necessary arrangements” regarding her request.\n[31]\nIn response to Amnesty International’s findings and questions, the UN Under-Secretary-General for Peace Operations, said that MONUSCO’s request to the authorities to prohibit the protest followed exchanges of information with the authorities which indicated that “the actions planned for 30 August posed significant risks to both peacekeepers and residents in Goma”.\n[32]\nHe stressed that “the Mission’s communications emphasized the need for the Congolese authorities to proactively address these concerns to prevent any loss of life and were delivered within the framework of regular dialogue, stressing the importance of respecting human rights while avoiding undue restrictions on the freedoms of expression and peaceful assembly.”\n[33]\nHe stated: “Notable examples include: a letter dated 30 June from Mr. Bisimwa, leader of the Faith Natural Judaic Messianic towards the Nations (FNJMN), addressed to President Félix Tshisekedi, inciting violence against MONUSCO and other United Nations agencies; a public communication from FNJMN on 18 July to the Mayor of Goma reiterating their intention to erect a statue of Patrice Lumumba on the land occupied by the Mission’s logistics base in Goma; protests organized by FNJMN on 30 and 31 July in front of the Mission’s logistics base; the broadcast of hostile messages targeting MONUSCO disseminated on 2 August on FNJMN’s community radio, Uwezo wa Neno; as well as messages affiliated with Wazalendo and FNJMN circulated on social media, calling for a widespread campaign against MONUSCO starting on 30 August.”\n[34]\nAmnesty International reviewed most of FNJMN’s statements and messages in the lead-up to 30 August 2023, including Bisimwa’s 30 June letter to President Tshisekedi, his 18 July letter to the Mayor of Goma, as well as several social media posts, but could not find evidence of incitement to violence. On 11 October 2024, Amnesty International sent a follow up letter to the UN Under-Secretary-General for Peace Operations, requesting additional information to support his claims.\n[35]\nThe letter reiterated unanswered questions regarding the assessments undertaken by MONUSCO, if any, to determine whether and how the risks identified might have been mitigated without resorting to an outright prohibition of the planned protest, and in relation to the possible consequences of requesting the Congolese authorities to prohibit the protest. Amnesty International further reiterated its question about the reasons why the UN did not publish the findings of its own investigations into these events. At the time of publication, the UN had not responded to our follow up requests.\nPreemptive Ban\n\nOn 23 August, the Mayor of Goma released a statement announcing that the 30 August protest planned by the Wazalendo was banned because of the group’s registration status.\n[36]\nSeveral letters followed between him and the Wazalendo leader, with copies sent to other authorities, including the governor. In his letters, the mayor claimed that the protest was banned because Wazalendo was not legally registered as a non-profit association and because of the “State of siege”.\n[37]\nHowever, Efraimu Bisimwa provided evidence that FNJMN had been legally registered as a non-profit association since 2019 and insisted that the protest go on as planned.\n[38]\nIn any event, authorities were not justified in banning the protest on the basis of Wazalendo’s registration status.\n[39]\nIn an interview with Amnesty International, Efraimu Bisimwa stated that they reached a compromise after a police official went to see them and urged them to call off the protest.\n[40]\nHe said:\n“On the evening of 28 August, Colonel Thierry arrived at our temple at around 4pm. He had reached us thanks to our follower, Mama Deborah, who is close to him. We had a three-hour meeting with him as he tried to convince us not to hold the protest on 30 August. I told him that the followers were largely mobilized and that it was downright difficult for nothing to be done on that day. In the end, I suggested that, as a police commander, he sends a few police officers to escort a small delegation of ours that would go and deliver our message to the authorities on 30 August. That was our final compromise. Colonel Thierry even sat down with us around the fire as we left the temple. He took part in some of our rituals, saying that he was also the son of a traditional chief. He even drank the Kasiksi [a local beer made from bananas] with us, which is a sign of the covenant. For us, this meant that the agreement had been reached and sealed by all parties.”\n[41]\nOperational Plan\nMeanwhile, at the request of the governor, the army was in the process of planning and mounting an operation against the Wazalendo. In an official message (“télégramme”) sent to the army and police units under his jurisdiction on 25 August, in his capacity as Military Operations Commander, Governor Lieutenant-General Ndima characterized the Wazalendo as “a pressure group which supports M23 and the Rwanda Defence Force terrorists”, and whose aim was to “sow unrest in the town in order to facilitate enemy infiltration.”\n[42]\nHe ordered them to take all necessary steps to protect MONUSCO facilities and Goma.\n[43]\nAmong those copied in the message were the defence minister, the army chief of staff and the head of the President’s military household.\nA message signed by the governor of Nord-Kivu addressed to the 34th Military Region. Highlights and translations added by Amnesty International.\nFollowing the governor’s message, the 34th Military Region issued an operational order on 28 August to all army units in Goma, including the Republican Guard’s Combined Armed Brigade (BIA).\n[44]\nThe order stated that “according to information in our possession, the so-called Wazalendo pressure group, which is increasingly acting as an auxiliary of the M23-RDF movement, is planning a march on 30 August 2023 in the town of Goma to demand the departure of MONUSCO, while sowing unrest in order to facilitate enemy infiltration into the town” (emphasis added).\n[45]\nThe operational order characterized the Wazalendo as a pressure group engaging in a march. The instructions were to “secure and permanently defend the city of Goma by reinforcing the CltZ [military area control] apparatus, protect MONUSCO facilities and disperse protesters”.\n\n[46]\nWhile the order recalled that rules of engagement and behaviour are strict, including the ban on the use of firearms “except in self-defence”,\n[47]\nits instructions did not recognize or refer to the right of peaceful assembly or reflect the obligation of law enforcement authorities to protect and facilitate the exercise of this right.\nA document containing orders of operation for the 34th Military Region. Highlights and translations added by Amnesty International.\nIn the order’s annex, each unit involved, including the Republican Guard/BIA, is given precise instructions in three phases corresponding to the response on 28, 29 and 30 August.\n[48]\nIn the final phase (30 August), their mission was to: “pursue the enemy”, “destroy isolated enemy elements”, “consolidate the area”, and “reorganize”.\n[49]\nIn response to the operational order, on 29 August, the Commander of the Republican Guard’s Combined Arms Brigade, Colonel Mike Kalamba Mikombe, issued a detailed “Motorised Deterrence Patrol Order” code-named “Keba”,\n[50]\nto units under his direct command, including the elite Special Forces (FS)\n[51]\nThe document set out the same allegation of collusion between the Wazalendo and the M23 and the Rwanda Defence Force and specified that it had been drawn up on the “orders/authorisation of the higher level.”\n[52]\nBIA’s objective, as stated in the document, was to “carry out a motorised deterrence patrol in the main roads around the city of Goma” on 30 August between 4am and 11.30am and to “nip in the bud the attempt [to sow chaos] by the Messianic pressure group.” While the order recalled the ban on the use of firearms, it included broader exceptions: “except upon order or in case of self-defence.”\n[53]\nA document containing orders of operation for the Republican Guard. This should include ensuring adequate accountability for those alleged to have engaged in attempts to obstruct or interfere with judicial processes, regardless of their official position.\nFacilitate victims’ access to justice and ensure respect for fair trial rights, including by guaranteeing legal aid for accused who are indigent.\nRelease detainees who have not been convicted of any crime or are awaiting trial in preventive detention without charge beyond time limits prescribed by law.\nTake concrete steps to address corruption, including by ensuring clear procedures to detect, prevent, and investigate incidents of corruption, bribery, and embezzlement of public funds and to bring those responsible for corruption-related offenses to justice.\nEnsure legal protections for whistleblowers and support the passage of a freedom of information act.\nRevise the law on the protection of human rights defenders to remove provisions that criminalize their work.\n4.\nPrioritize accountability for serious crimes\nDuring Tshisekedi’s first term in office,\nnational consultations\nwere held on a potential transitional justice process to address past and present serious crimes under international law.
