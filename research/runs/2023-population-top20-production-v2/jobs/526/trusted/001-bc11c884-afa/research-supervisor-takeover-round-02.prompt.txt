Abiy Ahmed was Ethiopia’s prime minister in 2023, so he had direct federal executive authority and command responsibility for national security policy, but Ethiopia’s conflict environment limited practical control. The year’s international-peace record was mixed: the Tigray cessation-of-hostilities process continued and some disarmament and interim-administration steps advanced, but foreign force withdrawal remained disputed, the Oromia peace track failed to produce an agreement, and the Amhara security crisis escalated into emergency rule and widespread abuse allegations.

For this chapter, the main finding is not nuclear policy. It is whether Abiy’s government pursued credible peace alternatives, upheld ceasefires, allowed lawful scrutiny, and restrained the use of force. The record shows partial de-escalation in Tigray, but continuing conflict, weak civilian protection, obstruction of scrutiny, and unresolved foreign and proxy-force issues.

**Compact Evidence Index**

1. **Tigray ceasefire and partial implementation**
- The AU said the November 2022 agreement was meant to end hostilities, restore humanitarian access, and support reconciliation, and Reuters later reported Tigray forces handing over heavy weapons and setting up an interim government.
- Support IDs: `WEB-2B-01`, `WEB-2B-02`

2. **Eritrean forces and incomplete withdrawal**
- Reuters reported in January and May 2023 that Eritrean troops remained inside Ethiopia and that their presence was a major obstacle to the peace deal’s implementation.
- Support IDs: `WEB-2B-03`

3. **Oromia peace talks failed**
- Reuters reported in May and November 2023 that talks between the government and the OLA ended without a deal.
- Support IDs: `WEB-2B-04`

4. **Amhara escalation and emergency rule**
- Reuters and Amnesty reported the August 2023 state of emergency after clashes with Fano, with sweeping arrest, curfew, search, and media powers.
- Support IDs: `WEB-2B-05`, `WEB-2B-06`

5. **Civilian harm and infrastructure damage**
- HRW and ICRC reported hundreds killed or injured in Amhara, killings and arrests during unrest, and damage to hospitals, water systems, and other infrastructure in Oromia and Amhara.
- Support IDs: `WEB-2B-05`, `WEB-2B-07`

6. **Restrictions on scrutiny**
- HRW and Amnesty reported internet/social-media restrictions, arrests of journalists and opposition figures, and limits on independent access to Amhara.
- Support IDs: `WEB-2B-06`

7. **External displacement and continued insecurity**
- UNHCR reported that despite the peace agreement, many internally displaced people could not return home in 2023, and displaced people remained vulnerable across multiple regions.
- Support IDs: `WEB-2B-01`, `WEB-2B-08`

**Lens Disposition**

- **2B.1 Peaceful alternatives:** Partially supported. The Tigray process and the OLA talks show serious use of negotiation, but the failure of the OLA talks and the escalation in Amhara limit the strength of the claim.
- **2B.10 Lasting international peace:** Weak / mixed. Tigray de-escalated, but foreign troop withdrawal remained unresolved and cross-regional instability persisted.
- **2B.2 Starting or prolonging war:** Mixed adverse evidence. No 2023 evidence shows Abiy initiating a new interstate war, but the government prolonged active internal conflict in Oromia and Amhara rather than settling it.
- **2B.3 Honest justification for force:** Incomplete. Official justifications centered on public order and sovereignty, but independent scrutiny was constrained.
- **2B.4 Civilian and prisoner protection:** Adverse evidence. The record includes civilian deaths, arrests, house-to-house searches, infrastructure damage, and restricted access.
- **2B.5 Control of proxies and arms:** Adverse / mixed. Eritrean and Amhara-aligned forces remained in contested areas, and the government did not fully resolve outside-force presence in Tigray.
- **2B.6 Scrutiny of war claims:** Adverse. Journalists, internet access, and outside investigators faced restrictions.

- **2B.8 Responsible military resources:** Incomplete. The sources show military use and emergency mobilization, but not enough budget/procurement detail to judge resource discipline cleanly.
- **2B.9 Accountability and remedy:** Adverse. The open record shows arrests and restrictions, but little transparent accountability for abuses.
- **Gap note:** The 2023 record is strong enough to judge peace process behavior and civilian-protection issues, but not strong enough to resolve internal procurement or chain-of-command questions.

--- CHAPTER RESEARCH 4B ---

**Orientation**
Abiy Ahmed’s 2023 political-freedom record is shaped by a government that had formal electoral institutions and a 2021 mandate, but operated in a coercive environment where war, emergency powers, internet blocking, and arrests of journalists and opposition-linked figures sharply constrained contestation. The strongest evidence shows a state that preserved electoral forms while narrowing practical opposition space, weakening independent oversight, and using security and information controls to manage dissent.

The year’s central pattern is not full democratic collapse into open single-party rule; it is a more specific authoritarian squeeze: competitive institutions remained on paper, but access to them, media freedom, and safe opposition activity were materially restricted, especially once the Amhara emergency began.

**Compact Evidence Index**

1. **Competitive elections existed, but legitimacy and acceptance were limited**
- Freedom House says Abiy’s Prosperity Party won the 2021 election, yet the result was contested and many opposition actors were excluded or boycotted.
- NEBE later acknowledged voter-registration and operational problems, including logistics, security, and arrests affecting campaign conditions.
- This matters because it sets the inherited baseline for 2023: formal elections existed, but they were not broadly trusted.

2. **The government retained strong leverage over electoral administration**
- Freedom House notes that courts sometimes forced NEBE to admit candidates, but the board initially resisted and logistical/security problems persisted.
- This matters for 4B.4 and 4B.8 because it shows institutional checks worked unevenly and were not enough to guarantee equal contestation.

3. **Opposition and civic freedom remained constrained**
- Freedom House reports closures of opposition offices, harassment, arrests, and killings of party members, while key opposition groups boycotted or stayed outside the process.
- This is the core evidence for restricted political competition.

4. **Media freedom and independent information were heavily constrained**
- Freedom House’s internet report says the government routinely shut down networks or blocked social media to hinder mobilization, and in February 2023 restricted TikTok, Facebook, Telegram, and YouTube.
- CPJ and RSF document repeated arrests of journalists, detentions in military facilities, and pressure on critical outlets.
- This is the clearest 2023 authoritarian-control record.

5. **Emergency powers were used to intensify control in Amhara**
- Once the state of emergency was declared, journalists reporting on the conflict were detained, often under procedures that suspended ordinary due process.
- This matters because it shows political control expanding through security law rather than open debate.

6. **There is partial evidence of institutional checks, but not enough to show resilient democracy**
- NEBE publicly discussed its own operational problems with parties, and courts sometimes corrected election administration.
- However, the overall record still shows weak independence, recurring coercion, and thin remedies.
- That makes the 2023 chapter mixed on formal institutions and adverse on actual political freedom.

**Lens Disposition**

- **4B.1 Genuine electoral choice:** Weak / mixed. Elections existed, but opposition participation was limited and results were contested.
- **4B.10 Lasting democratic resilience:** Adverse. The year did not leave the political system more resilient; repression and emergency controls persisted.
- **4B.2 No entrenchment of power:** Adverse. The record includes media restrictions, arrests, and institutional pressure that helped entrench governing control.
- **4B.3 Opposition and civic freedom:** Strongly adverse.
- **4B.4 Independent checks on power:** Mixed, leaning adverse. Courts occasionally intervened, but NEBE and broader oversight remained constrained.
- **4B.5 Politically neutral institutions:** Adverse.
- **4B.6 Independent information and media:** Strongly adverse.
- **4B.7 Equal political rights:** Adverse.

--- CHAPTER RESEARCH 5B ---

**Orientation**
Abiy Ahmed’s 2023 economic record is defined by severe inherited fragility plus a worsening external position: war costs, inflation, foreign-exchange scarcity, high domestic borrowing, and eventual sovereign default. The government pursued macroeconomic reform and continued discussions with the IMF, but the open 2023 record shows an economy under stress rather than broad prosperity. The key question is not whether Abiy produced a short-term growth rebound; it is whether his government put Ethiopia on a sustainable, fairer, evidence-based trajectory. The available evidence says only partly, and mostly not yet in 2023.

**Compact Evidence Index**

1. **Macro fragility and inflation**
- NBE’s 2022/23 annual report shows 32.5% national inflation in 2022/23, with food inflation at 31.7% and non-food inflation at 33.9%.
- The same report shows the birr weakened to 53.3 per USD in 2022/23 from 48.6 the year before.
- This is the central 5B outcome: living costs rose sharply and currency weakness persisted.

2. **Debt and default**
- World Bank and Reuters report Ethiopia entered debt default in late 2023, after failed bondholder talks and acute external-liquidity pressure.
- This is the clearest macro-stability failure in the chapter.

3. **Reform agenda and IMF engagement**
- IMF staff visited in April 2023 to discuss the government’s Homegrown Economic Reform Agenda II and IMF support.
- This shows policy intent toward macro adjustment, not pure denial or inaction.

4. **Domestic borrowing and crowding out**
- Addis Standard reported domestic debt rose sharply by March 2023, with government borrowing increasingly financed through T-bills and bond mechanisms.
- This suggests a state financing model that crowded out private credit.

5. **Exchange-rate distortion and black-market pressure**
- Addis Standard documented a widening gap between the official birr rate and the parallel market, plus severe FX shortages.
- This points to weak monetary and FX management in 2023.

6. **Poverty and social strain**
- World Bank says about 15 million people were still reliant on food aid and living standards deteriorated amid double-digit inflation.
- This means 2023 prosperity was not broad-based.

7. **Public investment and state-led development**
- The World Bank says the earlier state-led model improved some access indicators, but by 2023 it was constrained by debt, inflation, low productivity, and weak private investment.
- So the record is mixed on inherited foundations but adverse on current sustainability.

8. **Policy correction was real but late**
- NBE and IMF materials show reform discussion, FX-market adjustment pressure, and a later transition toward market-determined exchange rates, but that decisive shift came after 2023.
- For this chapter, the record is mostly preparatory rather than completed correction.

**Lens Disposition**

- **5B.1 Broad and sustainable prosperity:** Mixed / weak. The government pursued reform, but 2023 outcomes were not broadly prosperous.
- **5B.10 Lasting economic trajectory:** Adverse in 2023. The trajectory remained fragile and ended in default.
- **5B.2 Qualified economic leadership:** Mixed. There is evidence of technocratic engagement with IMF and NBE, but not enough here to show durable autonomy from political pressure.
- **5B.3 Macroeconomic stability:** Strongly adverse. Inflation, FX weakness, debt stress, and default all point the same way.
- **5B.4 Fair economic rules:** Incomplete / weak. The record shows controls and distortions, but not a full legal-regulatory audit.
- **5B.5 Resistance to economic capture:** Incomplete. The sources show borrowing and FX distortion, but not enough direct capture evidence for a final hard judgment.
- **5B.6 Productive public investment:** Mixed. Earlier public investment created infrastructure gains, but 2023 conditions show weak productivity and strained budgets.
- **5B.7 Evidence and correction:** Mixed. The government did engage the IMF and NBE, but correction was slow and late.
- **5B.8 Fair distribution:** Adverse. Inflation, food-aid reliance, and dollar shortages hit households broadly and regressively.
- **5B.9 Managing economic shocks:** Adverse. The state did not contain the shock well enough to avoid default and worsening living standards.

--- CHAPTER RESEARCH 6B ---

**Orientation**
Abiy Ahmed’s 2023 social-well-being record is dominated by crisis management, not broad developmental uplift. The period saw serious food insecurity, displacement, malnutrition, cholera, and education disruption, but also continued social-protection and humanitarian delivery through government, UN, and NGO channels. The clearest conclusion is that life chances did not improve durably in 2023; they remained under severe pressure from conflict, drought, inflation, and limited state capacity.

The main attribution problem is that many harms were inherited or externally driven, especially war, drought, and global price shocks. Still, the federal government remained responsible for funding choices, coordination, access, and whether it protected vulnerable people or allowed gaps to persist. The record shows some real service delivery, but not enough to offset the scale of need.

**Compact Evidence Index**

1. **Food insecurity and malnutrition stayed severe**
- FEWS NET said the emergency was far from over in May 2023, with critical acute malnutrition and emergency food assistance still needed to save lives.
- WHO later summarized 2023 nutrition data showing 11% acute malnutrition among under-fives and stark regional variation.

2. **Displacement remained massive**
- IOM’s end-2023 DTM counted about 3.19 million IDPs and 2.55 million returning IDPs, with conflict and drought as the main drivers.
- This is one of the strongest signs that ordinary life chances remained highly disrupted.

3. **Children’s education remained badly damaged**
- UNICEF said 13 million children were out of school and only 33.1% made it to secondary school; 90% of 10-year-olds could not read or understand a simple sentence.
- UNESCO’s later country case study says conflict since 2023 reversed educational development and reduced enrollment.

4. **Health access remained weak but some response capacity existed**
- UNICEF described a struggling health system with underfunding and inadequate skilled staff.
- WHO’s cholera reporting shows the state and partners were active, but the outbreak still spread widely.

5. **Social protection coverage was narrow**
- The ILO social-protection baseline indicates health insurance and school feeding expanded, but cash-based protection remained limited and most people received no cash benefits.
- This shows real but incomplete welfare institutions.

6. **Poverty and inequality remained structural**
- World Bank poverty materials show Ethiopia had made long-run gains before the period, but poor households and rural areas continued to lag badly.
- That matters because 2023 did not reverse the structural inequality problem.

7. **Crisis response was present but insufficient**
- UNICEF HAC 2023 and WHO/FEWS NET show significant humanitarian mobilization, but the need base outpaced delivery.
- This is a mixed record: some institutional response, but not enough protection.

**Lens Disposition**

- **6B.1 Welfare as a governing purpose:** Mixed. There was real humanitarian and social-sector activity, but not enough to show welfare dominated politics.
- **6B.10 Lasting life chances:** Adverse / weak. 2023 did not leave ordinary people clearly better off.
- **6B.2 Access to essential services:** Adverse. Access was constrained by conflict, drought, and undercapacity.
- **6B.3 Priority for vulnerable groups:** Mixed to adverse. Vulnerable groups were heavily targeted by aid, but needs outstripped support.
- **6B.4 Professional service delivery:** Mixed. There were functioning programs, but staffing and funding were inadequate.
- **6B.5 Measurement and correction:** Mixed. Data collection existed, but the record does not show strong corrective policy cycles.
- **6B.6 Protection during crises:** Adverse. Crisis response existed but was not enough to prevent widespread suffering.
- **6B.7 No political allocation of welfare:** Incomplete. I found limited direct evidence of welfare as political punishment in the opened 2023 sources.
- **6B.8 Dignity and equal opportunity:** Adverse. Regional and rural disparities were severe.
- **6B.9 Durable social institutions:** Mixed / weak. Some institutions exist, but 2023 did not demonstrate durable resilience.

--- CHAPTER RESEARCH 7B ---

**Orientation**
Abiy Ahmed’s 2023 integrity record is mixed in a narrow and specific sense. There is evidence of real anti-corruption signaling, some openness to audits, and a few moments of direct parliamentary accountability. There is also evidence of selective anti-corruption enforcement risk, opacity around security and information controls, loyalty-driven appointments in some strategic posts, and no strong public record of full independent scrutiny of the ruler’s own assets, family interests, or conflicts.

The main limitation is evidentiary, not just substantive: Ethiopia’s 2023 environment made it hard to verify concealment or self-enrichment directly. So the record should distinguish what is documented, what is only alleged, and what remains unknown.

**Compact Evidence Index**

1. **Anti-corruption drive was real, but potentially politicized**
- Transparency International says Abiy turned to curbing corruption after the Tigray ceasefire and created a National Anti-Corruption Committee.
- TI also warns such drives can be used against disloyal political factions.

2. **Abiy accepted public scrutiny in Parliament**
- In March 2023 he fielded hostile questions in Parliament about security, cost of living, governance, and even resignation.
- In July 2023 he pledged a thorough audit of non-government-funded mega projects amid transparency concerns.

3. **Transparency and information controls moved in opposite directions**
- The Council of Ministers passed a bill on classified government information in July 2023.
- That may serve legitimate state-security aims, but it also increases concealment risk unless oversight is strong.

4. **Key appointments suggest technocratic, not purely family-based, staffing in some posts**
- The National Bank governor and NEBE chair were both filled through formal processes, and their biographies emphasize technocratic credentials.
- That is positive evidence against blanket nepotism claims, but not proof of broad meritocracy.

5. **Independent oversight existed, but hard proof of ruler accountability is scarce**
- The auditor general publicly disclosed 15 billion birr in uncollected revenues.
- That is a sign of some institutional candor, but the public record does not show major direct remedial action tied to Abiy personally.

6. **Red Sea rhetoric created honesty concerns**
- In October 2023 Abiy publicly argued Ethiopia needed access to the Red Sea and used unusually expansive language about geography, sovereignty, and survival.
- He later walked back explicit military implications, saying Ethiopia had no interest in firing a bullet at neighbors.
- That sequence supports a record of rhetorical escalation followed by partial correction.

- **7B.1 Truthfulness:** Mixed. Some public statements were candid, but the Red Sea rhetoric was expansive and destabilizing before being moderated.

--- CHAPTER RESEARCH 8B ---

**Orientation**
Abiy Ahmed’s 2023 effectiveness record is mixed and highly constrained by war, debt stress, and weak state reach. The clearest goals visible in the open record were: sustain the Tigray peace process, stabilize the macroeconomy, reposition the state’s economic team, and preserve central control while managing multiple crises. Some machinery was built and some milestones were reached, but many goals were only partially converted into durable institutions or outcomes by the end of 2023.

The most consequential implementation pattern is this: Abiy could still direct high-level reshuffles and set priorities, and his government did mobilize around peace and macro reform, but coordination broke down in security zones, humanitarian conditions stayed severe, and macro stabilization had not yet translated into broad improvement by year-end.

**Compact Evidence Index**

1. **Clear governing program**
- Abiy’s repeated public priorities in 2023 centered on national unity, peace after Tigray, centralized state direction, economic reform, and Red Sea access.
- This is enough to freeze a visible program for evaluation, even if some components were aspirational or rhetorical.

2. **Real machinery was built around the macro program**
- The National Bank got a new governor with reform credentials.
- The government opened IMF talks and used macro-policy institutions to pursue stabilization.

3. **Real machinery was built around the peace program**
- The Tigray interim administration was established and the AU said the cessation process was still being implemented.
- That is a tangible conversion of war-termination goals into institutional follow-through.

4. **Coordination was incomplete**
- Tigray implementation dragged.
- Amhara violence escalated into emergency rule.
- This shows follow-through broke down across territorial and security systems.

5. **Learning and correction were partial**
- The central bank reduced direct advances to government and inflation eased from its peak, suggesting some correction.
- But debt default and persistent FX stress show the correction was incomplete by 2023.

6. **Public-facing goal shifts and crisis management mattered**
- Abiy’s Red Sea rhetoric escalated in late 2023, then he partially moderated the force implication.
- This is evidence of both programmatic ambition and reactive adjustment.

7. **Outcome movement was limited**
- Inflation and debt stress remained severe.
- The peace process reduced some war intensity but did not produce a fully durable, normalized state.
- Social and economic outcomes did not yet show clear durable gain at year-end.

- **8B.1 Clear governing program:** Strong. The program was visible enough to evaluate.

- **8B.2 Turning goals into machinery:** Mixed. There were real laws, appointments, and institutions, but not full system conversion.

- **8B.8 Learning and correction:** Mixed. Some monetary tightening and institutional replacement occurred.
- **8B.9 Managing crises and resistance:** Mixed to weak. The government held the program together in parts, but not across all crises.
