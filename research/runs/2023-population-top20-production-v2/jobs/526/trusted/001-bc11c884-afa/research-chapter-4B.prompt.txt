Earlier reconnaissance:

**Overview**
Abiy Ahmed was Ethiopia’s prime minister in 2023, so he held the top federal executive office and command authority over the armed forces, but he operated inside a federal parliamentary system with autonomous regional states and a legislature that could authorize emergency measures. In practice, his authority was strong at the center and contested in the regions, especially where armed conflict, regional militias, and federal-regional disputes limited direct control.

The 2023 period was shaped by three overlapping realities: the aftermath of the Tigray war, renewed large-scale violence in Amhara and parts of Oromia, and severe economic strain. Abiy’s government presented peace, national unity, centralization, and macroeconomic reform as core goals, but the year also saw repression, communications restrictions, a debt default, and major humanitarian pressure.

**Evidence Records**

1. **Office and power structure**
- **Fact:** Abiy Ahmed was Ethiopia’s prime minister in 2023, and the constitution placed the highest federal executive power with the prime minister and council of ministers, while the office also carried command of the armed forces. This matters because it anchors attribution for executive decisions, security policy, and national crisis response.
- **Best source:** [FDRE Office of the Prime Minister, “Our Government”](https://www.pmo.gov.et/government/) (official government site, accessed 2026-08-01)
- **Support locator:** lines 21-25 on the page state Ethiopia is a federal parliamentary republic, that executive power rests with the prime minister and council, and that the prime minister is commander-in-chief.
- **Period:** Inherited institutional structure, active in 2023.
- **Connection to ruler:** Direct and strong.
- **Complicating evidence:** Reuters notes Abiy also sought to centralize power in a country where regions retain autonomy, showing formal powers did not translate into full territorial control. - **Independent support:** Reuters, constitutional text, and HRW’s 2023 country reporting all align.

## Workplan

1. **Orient.** Establish the ruler's formal and practical authority, the inherited situation, major external shocks or constraints, and the information environment.
2. **Plan coverage.** For every selected lens, identify the important favorable, adverse, disputed, and exculpatory possibilities that research should test.
3. **Find the main record.** Begin with strong overviews and syntheses. Identify the consequential decisions, laws, budgets, appointments, statements, implementation, corrections, and outcomes.
4. **Verify material claims.** Open the underlying sources. Use precise laws, official records, audits, judgments, datasets, scholarship, monitoring, and credible reporting where they establish or challenge an important claim. Assess each source’s authority, independence, method, proximity, and date.
5. **Test the account.** Look for contrary evidence, independent corroboration, relevant local-language material, source dependencies, and alternative explanations. Distinguish inherited conditions and country context from conduct attributable to the ruler.
6. **Deepen where useful.** Repeat steps 3–5 while new searches add a material fact, a stronger source, a missing perspective, or a necessary correction. Finish when further searching mainly repeats the established record or reaches a concrete access barrier.
7. **Prepare the handoff.** Organize the evidence by underlying fact and map each developed claim to every selected lens it supports.
