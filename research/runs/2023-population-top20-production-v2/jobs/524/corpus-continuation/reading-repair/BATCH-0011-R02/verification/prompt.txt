Not available\n\nThe Dublin Regulation is explicitly referred to as a ground for inadmissibility of an asylum application in\nthe Asylum Act.301 The examination of whether another state is responsible for carrying out the asylum\nprocedure (either based on the Dublin Regulation or, if Germany is the responsible Member State per the\nDublin regulation, on the German ‘safe third country’ rule) is an admissibility assessment and as such a\npart of the regular procedure. Thus, in the legal sense, the term ‘Dublin procedure’ does not refer to a\nseparate procedure in the German context, but merely to the shifting of responsibility for an asylum\napplication within the administration (i.e., takeover of responsibility by the ‘Dublin Units’ of the BAMF).\n\nFingerprints are to be taken from all asylum seekers aged six years or older302 on the day that the\napplication is registered and are systematically subjected to a Eurodac query for applicants aged 14 years\nand older, in line with the Eurodac regulation. Eurodac queries are the major ground for the initiation of\nDublin procedures. No cases of asylum seekers refusing to be fingerprinted have been reported, only\nseveral cases where “manipulation” of fingerprints took place, i.e., persons scraping off or etching their\nfingertips, making fingerprints unrecognisable.\n\nIn principle, only the BAMF is responsible for conducting the Dublin procedure. A possible forced return to the responsible\nMember State is carried out by the federal states (Länder) or the Federal Police.303 The Federal Police\nmay also ask a court to issue a detention order if there is a considerable risk of ‘absconding’. If a person has to be sent to hospital and therefore cannot be accommodated on the\nairport premises, the person is accompanied to the hospital by the Federal police and will be returned to\nthe airport facilities once released from hospital. Within the broader police border procedure, the BAMF\noffice is responsible for processing the claim for asylum. The necessary (detention) facilities exist in the\nairports of Berlin, Düsseldorf, Frankfurt/Main, Hamburg and Munich, although the BAMF does not\nhave a branch office assigned to all of those places.430\n\nThe German Asylum Act foresees the applicability of the airport procedure where the asylum seeker\narriving at the airport:431\n     v Comes from a ‘safe country of origin’; or\n     v Is unable to prove their identity with a valid passport or passport replacement.\n\nThe second ground merits particular consideration. German law triggers the airport procedure as soon as\nit is established that the asylum seeker is unable to prove identity by means of a passport or passport\nreplacement. It does not condition the applicability of the procedure upon requirements of misleading the\nauthorities by withholding relevant information on identity or nationality or destroying or disposing of an\nidentity or travel document in bad faith.432 The scope of the airport procedure in Germany is therefore not\nconsistent with the boundaries set by the recast APD according to the opinion of a lawyer from 2014.433\n\nYet, practice suggests that the second ground is the one most often used for activating the airport\nprocedure. As demonstrated by the countries of origin of applicants, many applicants in the airport\nprocedure in 2022 came from Syria, Iran, and Afghanistan (see table below). These are all countries which\nare not considered as ‘safe’ and which have a relatively high chance of recognition at national level. A\nfortiori, this means that the airport procedure is necessarily mostly activated on the second legal ground,\nwhen a person is unable to present proof of identity.\n\nThe applicability of the Dublin procedure is also examined, and this is done prior to the processing of the\nasylum claim in the airport procedure.
