# Chapter 1B — Nuclear And Existential-Risk Responsibility

Status: **draft; v5 objective-evidence questions require controlled rejudgment**

## Chapter Identity

- Chapter: `1B`
- Rating: nuclear and global existential-risk responsibility
- Evidence strategy: local-first structured evidence plus cited manual research
- Rubric version: `chapter_1b_v5`
- Output: one chapter score (`1`–`10`) and one confidence assessment per ruler-period

## Purpose And Non-Goals

This chapter asks whether the ruler used the risk-bearing powers actually available
to them responsibly, reduced catastrophic danger, and left a safer posture. A high
score means prudent restraint, credible safeguards, good-faith risk reduction, and
responsible crisis behavior. A low score means reckless escalation, proliferation,
unsafe control, coercive nuclear shielding, or knowingly increased catastrophic risk.

It does not rank rulers by arsenal size alone, reward non-nuclear rulers automatically,
or penalize a ruler merely because they inherited a dangerous posture. Conventional
military strength, generic environmental policy, prestige, and peaceful rhetoric are
not substitutes for demonstrated existential-risk choices. Non-nuclear cyber,
biological, or AI policy belongs here only where a credible catastrophic pathway and
ruler-level choice are shown. The client matrix is never evidence.

## Ten Evidence Lenses

| Lens | Simple question | Detailed research question | Priority evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1B.1 — Reducing existential risk** | Did the ruler try to make catastrophic conflict less likely? | Did the ruler use formal authority, strategy, directives, and resource choices to reduce nuclear or other existential risk rather than increase prestige, leverage, or personal power through escalation? | **Formal acts and law**; **Resources**; **Implementation and operational conduct** |
| **1B.2 — Responsible nuclear rhetoric** | Did the ruler speak about nuclear weapons responsibly? | Did the ruler avoid reckless threats, brinkmanship, apocalyptic language, and normalization of nuclear use, and did formal doctrine, orders, and operational posture corroborate rather than contradict responsible rhetoric? | **Rhetoric and representations**; **Formal acts and law**; **Implementation and operational conduct** |
| **1B.3 — Safe nuclear control** | Did the ruler keep nuclear weapons and decisions safe and controlled? | Did the ruler enact, fund, staff, implement, and enforce effective command-and-control, custody, safety, inspection, and accident-prevention safeguards, and correct identified failures? | **Resources**; **Personnel**; **Implementation and operational conduct** |
| **1B.4 — Arms control and inspections** | Did the ruler support and follow agreements that reduce nuclear danger? | Did the ruler support, ratify, implement, fund, and comply with arms-control, inspection, nonproliferation, disarmament, and de-escalation agreements, or obstruct and weaken them? | **Formal acts and law**; **Resources**; **Implementation and operational conduct** |
| **1B.5 — Nuclear cover for aggression** | Did the ruler avoid using nuclear power to protect aggression or repression? | Did the ruler avoid formally or operationally using nuclear capability to authorize, shield, or intensify conventional aggression, territorial coercion, or domestic repression? | **Formal acts and law**; **Rhetoric and representations**; **Implementation and operational conduct** |
| **1B.6 — Preventing proliferation** | Did the ruler stop allies, clients, and domestic actors from spreading nuclear weapons? | Did the ruler establish and enforce proliferation controls against allies, proxies, clients, firms, and domestic factions, and respond when monitoring exposed evasion or assistance? | **Formal acts and law**; **Resources**; **Implementation and operational conduct** |
| **1B.7 — Independent risk expertise** | Did the ruler empower qualified experts who could warn about catastrophic risks? | Did the ruler appoint qualified, independent experts, protect their access and dissent, and resource risk-reducing institutions rather than replace expertise with loyalty or ideology? | **Personnel**; **Resources**; **Implementation and operational conduct** |

Priority evidence categories indicate promising evidence, not required coverage or an
exclusive source list. Missing categories do not invalidate a lens.

## Researcher Guidance

Follow the local-first researcher guide. Start with the resolved ruler-period and local
D17/FAS facts, then check SIPRI and FAS narrative material. Search by themes rather
than issuing ten repetitive searches: inherited capability and authority; arsenal and
doctrine change; safeguards/command systems; treaties and inspections; threats and
crises; proliferation relationships; expert appointments; catastrophic dual-use policy;
and end-of-period posture.

Prefer IAEA, UNODA/UN treaty records, CTBTO, treaty inspection bodies, declassified or
contemporaneous official records, SIPRI/FAS technical assessments, legislative or
inquiry records, serious scholarship, and reputable reporting. Official claims about
restraint or safety need independent corroboration; official texts remain strong for
formal doctrine, appointments, treaty acts, and dated statements. Preserve contrary
evidence and uncertainty. Do not infer responsible rhetoric from silence or safety from
the absence of reported accidents.

Record reusable evidence once and point it to every materially relevant lens. Separate
target-year evidence, broader ruler-period evidence, inherited baseline, and later
retrospective evidence. The researcher describes evidence and attribution but does not
assign the final chapter score.

## Sparse, Historical, And Closed-Regime Evidence

For non-nuclear rulers with no plausible authority or exposure, document that limited
exposure; do not manufacture activity or award an automatic `10`. For older cases use
archives, treaty records, memoirs cautiously, cabinet/command histories, and later
scholarship, marking retrospective and contested claims. In closed regimes, missing
reporting is weak evidence: triangulate external monitoring, satellite/technical
assessments, defectors only with corroboration, and adversarial-source caveats. A thin
record may still support a cautious chapter score when the known exposure and choices
are clear; otherwise widen the confidence interval or flag review.

Classify exposure before scoring as `material_exposure`,
`limited_exposure_with_choices`, `no_discriminating_opportunity`, or
`unknown_due_to_evidence_gap`. The last category normally produces a null and a
targeted research continuation. `No_discriminating_opportunity` also produces a null:
it documents why the ruler cannot be discriminatingly scored and must not be ranked
against materially exposed rulers. Generic multilateralism,
civil nuclear phase-out, or the absence of nuclear rhetoric is contextual unless tied
to a ruler-attributed catastrophic-risk choice.

## Attribution And Inherited Baseline

Establish what authority the ruler personally held over doctrine, forces, diplomacy,
appointments, and crisis decisions. Quarantine acts of predecessors, military bodies,
coalition partners, or dominant patrons unless adoption, direction, knowing tolerance,
or failure to use available corrective authority is evidenced. Judge change from the
inherited posture alongside absolute risk: inheriting a huge arsenal is not itself
culpability, while expanding or recklessly using it is; prudent reduction deserves
credit even when the remaining stockpile is large.

## Chapter-Level 1–10 Rubric

| Score | Chapter anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | The ruler authorized or caused an actual nuclear detonation against people or a military target, or deliberately caused an equivalent realized nuclear catastrophe. Actual use establishes eligibility for the floor; context, target, scale, alternatives, and restraint still determine whether 1 is warranted. |
| 2 | The ruler moved credibly toward nuclear use or created an acute near-use danger through operational preparation, direct brinkmanship, extremely unsafe command, or sustained nuclear coercion during war, without a realized nuclear detonation. |
| 3 | Severe nuclear risk increase through doctrine expansion, deployment, proliferation, repeated threats, safeguard abandonment, or destabilizing exercises, but without demonstrated near-use. |
| 4–5 | Mixed or weak responsibility: meaningful restraint in some areas but consequential escalation, obstruction, unsafe governance, or deterioration in others. |
| 6–7 | Generally responsible risk management and some tangible risk reduction, with notable omissions, ambiguity, inherited constraints, or inconsistent conduct. |
| 8–9 | Strong, sustained, and consequential restraint, safeguards, diplomacy, expert governance, and crisis prudence that materially improved the posture. |
| 10 | Exceptional, durable, and well-evidenced leadership that substantially reduced catastrophic risk across the ruler's actual sphere of authority while resisting powerful contrary incentives. |

The lenses are qualitatively and non-equally weighted. Crisis escalation, actual
weapon use, proliferation, and command safety can dominate several weaker signals.
No arithmetic average, equal ten-percent weighting, or minimum lens count is required.
Exposure matters: the judge evaluates available choices, consequences, and authority.
Scoring is opportunity-adjusted. Limited exposure or authority is not automatically a
high or low score. A ruler may earn a high score when meaningful risk-bearing choices
arose and the ruler consistently exercised responsible restraint within their actual
authority. When no discriminating opportunity exists, return null with an explicit
exposure assessment; do not manufacture excellence, blame, or a midpoint from mere
non-exposure.

Scores of 6 or above require a direct ruler-attributed risk-bearing choice. Scores of
8 or above additionally require consequential improvement or restraint supported by
multiple independent source types. Country posture or silence alone cannot support a
score.

### Absolute-scale guardrails

The annual cohort does not define the endpoints. The worst ruler in a current batch
must not receive 1 unless the realized-use floor is met. Nuclear threats, coercive
shielding of conventional war, doctrine hardening, exercises, deployments, or arsenal
growth can be profoundly irresponsible while remaining in bands 2 or 3. Classify each
record before scoring as `realized_use_or_catastrophe`,
`credible_near_use_or_operational_preparation`,
`severe_escalation_without_near_use`, `mixed_risk_management`,
`generally_responsible_management`, `strong_consequential_risk_reduction`, or
`exceptional_durable_risk_reduction`.

A rationale may not describe threats, exercises, deployments, or doctrine change as
"maximally dangerous" merely because no worse case appears in the annual cohort. Since
no nuclear weapon has been detonated in war after 1945, a modern annual batch can
properly contain no score of 1.

The general historical-extreme analogy (including references to Nazi-regime-level
conduct) does not replace this chapter-specific test. Regime cruelty, repression, or
aggression alone cannot establish the 1B floor: the ruler must meet the realized nuclear
use or equivalent realized-catastrophe condition above.

## Judge Workflow Across Rulers

### V4 direct-evidence and absolute-floor gate

A numeric score requires a target-period choice, action, restraint, remedy, or
outcome within the ruler's actual nuclear or existential-risk authority. Generic
nuclear posture, inherited arsenals, speeches, and pre- or post-period treaties are
context only. If no such target-period fact exists, return null; do not manufacture
a neutral or favorable score from low exposure.

One Chapter 1B judge applies this rubric across all eligible rulers in the comparison
batch. The judge first inventories exposure and inherited posture, reads every dossier,
identifies common anchors and edge cases, provisionally orders rulers, then assigns one
chapter score and confidence to each. It must explain the decisive evidence, meaningful
counterevidence, attribution, change from baseline, and why adjacent higher/lower bands
were rejected. Revisit the full ordering after scoring; use overlapping anchor cases if
context limits require shards. Preserve citations and flag research gaps without
turning formatting differences into substantive failures.

## Minimum Evidence And Graceful Degradation

A score needs at least one traceable, relevant source and a stated structured prior or
`not_available`, plus an explicit exposure/authority and temporal-fit assessment.
High-confidence extreme scores normally require multiple independent source types and
evidence of consequential conduct. Sparse lenses, unknown appointments, or unavailable
internal control details lower confidence rather than mechanically lower the score.
If identity, core attribution, or citation authenticity is unresolved, withhold the
score for review; ordinary incomplete coverage is not a publication blocker.

## Common Evidence-Item Semantics

Each material evidence item should carry a stable evidence ID; a concise factual claim;
source URL, title, publisher, and publication/access date where available; source type
and confidence with a short reason; a quotation or precise locator when available;
target-year/ruler-period fit; ruler attribution and inherited-baseline relevance; its
positive, negative, mixed, contextual, or contrary role; and the supported lens IDs.
Fields may be normalized later and natural-language equivalents are acceptable. The
substantive minimum is a traceable source, intelligible claim, temporal fit, attribution,
and evidence role. One item may support multiple lenses without duplication.

## Bias And Comparability Checks

Check visibility and secrecy, nuclear/non-nuclear exposure, great-power attention,
survivorship and hindsight, adversarial intelligence claims, rhetoric-versus-capability,
population/state-size irrelevance, inherited arsenals, crisis opportunity, source type,
recency, and consistent cross-ruler calibration. Do not equate report volume with risk,
treat a treaty signature as implementation, or reward a ruler for lacking capacity they
never sought or controlled.
