INTRODUCTION\n1. This Performance and Learning Review (PLR) assesses the implementation of the World Bank\nGroup FY20-25 Country Partnership Framework (CPF) for the United Mexican States.1 The CPF was\ndiscussed by the Board on February 27, 2020, at the outset of the COVID-19 pandemic. Covering six years, it\naims to support three interlinked goals: (a) more rapid, more inclusive growth; (b) stronger institutions for\npublic finance, service delivery, and economic inclusion; and (c) sustainable infrastructure and climate action.\n2. The objectives of this PLR are to assess progress in CPF implementation and propose adjustments\nfor the remainder of the CPF period. The PLR has been prepared in 2023 at the mid-point of the CPF period,\nand benefits from updated knowledge about country context, program implementation, and lessons learned.\nIt also suggests forward-looking changes to the results framework, implementation, and risk management.\nThe PLR will inform the engagement with the new government to be elected in June 2024, the Completion\nand Learning Review (CLR) in FY25, and a new CPF planned for FY26.\n3. Immediately after CPF discussion, global and domestic crises hit Mexico, shifting demand for World\nBank collaboration, and affecting CPF implementation and graduation prospects. The COVID-19 pandemic\nresulted in a dramatic economic contraction of 8 percent of Gross Domestic Product (GDP) in 2020,2\ncompounded by a global recession and the economic fallout from Russia’s invasion of Ukraine. Although\nMexico continued to access external capital markets on reasonable terms, crisis management altered\npriorities and affected reforms. This undermined CPF implementation and Mexico’s graduation from the\nInternational Bank for Reconstruction and Development (IBRD).3 A shift in client demand for World Bank\nsupport led to a large increase in countercyclical IBRD policy financing versus the previous CPF period at the\nexpense of a fall in demand for investment lending. Several Investment Policy Financing (IPF) operations were\ncanceled or restructured at client request. The World Bank’s analytical engagement helped maintain\ncollaboration through several high-impact reports, which supported the policy content of the Development\nPolicy Financing (DPF) program. Nevertheless, as a result of these two fundamental global and domestic\nshifts, out of the 24 CPF objective indicators, eight have been achieved, one partially achieved, five are on\ntrack, and ten are off track, five of which are unlikely to be achieved.4\n4. This PLR confirms the original strategic direction and the duration of the 2020 CPF but proposes\nadjustments to its objectives and results framework in light of these dual shifts. The PLR confirms the three\nCPF Focus Areas5 but reduces the number of objectives from seven to six. The changes to the results\nframework reflect current reform space, client demand, and implementation capacity. The PLR also raises\nthe overall risk rating from “Moderate” to “Substantial.” The adjustments proposed in the PLR are aligned\nwith World Bank reforms currently under discussion, notably the Evolution Roadmap, which includes an\nelevated strategic role for middle-income countries on the global challenges agenda.6 The PLR thus\nsummarizes what the World Bank is doing in selected global challenges, particularly those addressed more\ndirectly in Focus Area C (Climate Change Adaptation and Mitigation; Energy Access; and Water Security and\nAccess) and identifies further opportunities for one World Bank Group (WBG) collaboration, through\ncoordination and deployment of collective knowledge and complementary instruments for greater impact.\n1 Country Partnership Framework, World Bank Report No. 137429-MX.\n2 National Statistics Institute (INEGI)’s monthly employment survey (ENOE) by July 2020.\n3 During preparation, the Graduation Discussion Income (GDI) provided a strong filter for the selection of the activities in the CPF.\n4 These terms are defined in Section III. MAIN CHANGES IN COUNTRY CONTEXT\n5. The CPF was prepared shortly after the current administration assumed power and is aligned with\nthe National Development Plan. President Andrés Manuel López Obrador assumed power in December 2018\nand CPF preparation was completed in 2019. The government consolidated its support in the mid-term\nelections held in June 2021 and local elections in June 2023. Ahead of the next presidential elections in June\n2024, the World Bank is preparing its transition engagement,7 which the PLR will inform.\n6. Immediately after the CPF discussion in 2020, the global COVID-19 pandemic hit. Latin America and\nthe Caribbean (LAC) was the hardest hit region of the world in both health and economic terms. In Mexico,\nthe pandemic resulted in 600,000 estimated excess deaths in 2020, followed by about 800,000 in 2021.8 In\n2020, output suffered its largest collapse since the 1930s. This contraction was driven by social distancing\nmeasures, supply chain disruptions, and risk aversion on the part of consumers and tourists.9 Labor poverty\nincreased from 39.0 percent at end-2019 to 46.0 percent in the same quarter of 2020 (a historical peak due\nto the pandemic), falling back to 37.3 percent by third quarter of 2023. Monetary poverty rates (using the\nofficial poverty line - línea de pobreza por ingresos) showed a similar inverted-U trend going from 49.9\npercent in 2018 to 52.8 in 2020 followed by a rapid decline to 43.5 in 2020.10\n7. Other structural challenges persist. There are significant disparities across states and population\ngroups in Mexico,11 including gender, ethnicity and migration disparities. 12 Income inequality is high. Mexico\nhas a higher Gini coefficient than countries with similar GDP/head13 but the Gini coefficient for household\nincome has declined from 48.6 to 43.1 between 2016 and 2022 due to solid household income growth among\npoorer households. The digital divide risks further entrenching historical disparities in economic\nopportunity.14 Increased violence and insecurity driven by organized crime activities has deterred\ninvestment, reducing the scope to further address economic disparities.15\n8. The government focused on ensuring a solid macroeconomic framework during the pandemic. The\nadministration re-prioritized expenditures to focus on universal cash transfers to the elderly and public\ninvestment in hallmark infrastructure projects. CPF design needs to adjust to the policy framework and operating environment, taking a\npragmatic, flexible, and creative approach to opportunities and risks. A realistic assessment of country\ncontext – policy priorities, financing constraints, political economy, and implementation capacity and\nlikelihood – is crucial. While the pandemic was impossible to predict, other governance, institutional, and\nsectoral risks could have been assessed more realistically and the program designed accordingly. The PLR\nbuilds on these lessons, with greater program selectivity and a more realistic risk assessment, seeking\nopportunities and balancing what is desirable with what is politically and technically possible at the federal,\nsubnational and sector levels. The Evolution Roadmap, with an enhanced value proposition for middle-\nincome countries, opens opportunities for a more integrated one World Bank Group approach at the project\nlevel and in joint knowledge engagements, such as on nearshoring, climate finance, financial inclusion, digital,\nenergy supply reliability and water. A disorderly tightening in global liquidity\ncould weaken activity and drive system-wide liquidity stress. These risks could be accentuated by\nrisks from cyber events and structural transitions, i.e., climate change.\n\n3. Banxico’s mid-corridor operating framework fully supports the efficient pricing and\ndistribution of liquidity. Banks have certainty about day-to-day liquidity conditions and can access\na collateralized intraday facility and an overdraft as backstops. The collateral policy provides a\nsufficient volume of securities to efficiently implement monetary policy, while the high quality and\nliquid nature of the securities minimizes the risks to Banxico’s balance sheet.\n\n59. Liquidity management demonstrated flexibility and resilience during the COVID-19\nfinancial market turmoil. The functioning of financial markets was restored after a short period of\nstress. Most measures were not fully utilized but were effective in restoring market functioning due\nto their strong signaling effects and Banxico’s liquidity support was essential in enabling local\nmarket participants to absorb the large amounts of government bonds sold by foreign investors.\nThe COVID support measures (e.g., funding for lending facility) helped resolve the practical\nchallenges of accepting credit claims as collateral. They had several design features that provide\nvaluable lessons for future market support programs: (i) the programs were price-based facilities\nand served as effective backstops; (ii) Banxico deployed targeted operations to support market\nparticipants in the key securities markets by providing them funding liquidity in well-calibrated\namounts and against good collateral with appropriate haircuts; (iii) risk transfer to Banxico remained\ncompletely controlled with very limited direct intervention; and (iv) exit strategies were provided by\nconstruction as most facilities were term repo or swap transactions. Banxico’s ELA framework would benefit from some enhancements. Banxico’s ELA\nframework has a sound legal foundation and a comprehensive internal policy. The framework\nprovides broad flexibility to Banxico’s Governing Board; it can adjust ELA parameters (e.g., collateral,\nterm, and rate) if credit institutions could pose systemic risks.
