Adicionalmente, en al menos\n       uno de los casos mencionados en esta comunicación, las autoridades militares\n       serían quienes habrían iniciado las investigaciones criminales, cuando las\n       autoridades competentes para investigar y procesar los hechos serían las\n       autoridades civiles.\n\n       Organizaciones de la sociedad civil reportaron 287,254 carpetas de\n       investigación abiertas en México por el delito de homicidio doloso y\n       feminicidio entre 2011 y 2022. Almost 2,000,000 searches of the\ndatabase have been made by members of the public, and around 30 percent of those searches revealed that the\nperson was in detention. Main Findings\nThe Working Group notes that arbitrary detention remains a widespread problem in Mexico due to a variety\nof causes and aggravating factors, both in the regulatory framework and in its implementation.\n2.1 Criminal Justice\n2.1.1. Mandatory Pre-trial Detention\nAlthough pre-trial detention should be regarded as an exceptional measure to ensure the availability of the\naccused during criminal proceedings, Article 19 of the Mexican Constitution sets out a list of felonies that\ntrigger mandatory pre-trial detention. In 2019, mandatory pre-trial detention was extended to 16 categories of\ncrimes, which are vaguely framed and susceptible to extension to an indeterminate range of offences. In 2021,\nmandatory pre-trial detention was introduced for other crimes.\nOf the approximately 90,000 people in pre-trial detention (“en proceso”) in 2022, around 50 percent are subject\nto mandatory pre-trial detention. Many of the detainees had been subjected to lengthy mandatory pre-trial\ndetention, including some who were still in mandatory pre-trial detention over 5 years after arrest. Worryingly,\nexcessively lengthy pre-trial detention was common amongst all detainees (noting that there was a total prison\npopulation of around 230,000 in Mexico in July 2023), not just those in mandatory pre-trial detention.\nThe Working Group is mindful that mandatory pre-trial detention has been identified as a violation of the right\nto personal liberty, as well as potentially undermining others, such as the presumption of innocence, the right\nto personal integrity and judicial independence. Mandatory pre-trial detention has been considered a violation\nof human rights in several opinions of the Working Group, including the Mexican context,4 and was declared\nto be a violation of Mexico’s obligations by the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, which ordered it to\nadjust its laws in this respect to meet international standards.\nThe Mexican authorities should reconcile its approach to preventive detention with international human rights\nlaw, by removing mandatory pre-trial detention from the Constitution and requiring that pre-trial detention\nmay only be applied based on an individualized assessment in which it is shown that the defendant is either a\nflight risk, at risk of serious re-offending, or at risk of tampering with evidence or witnesses.\n2.1.2. Arraigo (precautionary detention without charge)\nThe procedure known as arraigo was incorporated into the Constitution in 2008. It permits the authorities to\ndetain a person for up to 80 days for criminal investigation purposes in organized crime, without indicting that\nperson for a crime. The use of arraigo peaked between 2009 and 2012, but has decreased since 2013, and is\nrarely used these days. According to the information received by the Working Group, in 2022 it was used in\n25 cases. An attempt to remove arraigo from the Constitution in 2019 was unsuccessful.\nThe Inter-American Court of Human Rights and other international bodies have called for Mexico to eliminate\narraigo, as it violates the right to personal liberty and due process. Although authorities explained that arraigo\nis currently mainly used to protect witnesses in organised crimes cases, the Working Group notes that its\nongoing existence creates the risk of arbitrary detention. It encourages the Mexican authorities to remove the\nprocedure of arraigo from the Constitution and end its use as a basis for detention.\n3 UNGA, A/RES/53/144 8 March 1999 (“Declaration on the Right and Responsibility of Individuals, Groups and Organs of Society to Promote\nand Protect Universally Recognized Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms”).\n4 See, e.g. Opinion Nos. 32/2023; 14/2021; 1/2018.\n3\n\n2.1.3. The militarization of public security\nIn Mexico, the use of military forces to undertake public policing activities at the federal, state and municipal\nlevel is frequent. There are reportedly over one hundred thousand soldiers currently carrying out policing and\npublic security tasks, as well as migratory control. This has been closely linked to rises in violence against\ndetainees, disappearances, and homicides. Detainees arrested by military personnel frequently were subjected\nto serious violence and torture, including beatings with foreign objects.\nSymptomatic of the extensive use of military elements in policing is the 2019 formation of the National Guard.\nAccording to the Constitution, the National Guard falls under the Ministry of Security and Citizen’s Protection.\nAlthough the incorporation of military personnel into the National Guard has a transitory nature, the armed\nforces have been authorised to continue public security functions until 2028.\nWhereas the National Guard took over the functions of the Federal Police, its personnel are predominantly\nmilitary by background. Of its 128,000 members, around 48 percent come from the Army and Navy. Although\nmembers of the National Guard are trained inter alia in human rights and in gathering evidence, the Working\nGroup notes that there are ongoing reports of violence in arrests and arbitrary detention by military personnel.\nAlthough arbitrary arrests committed by personnel of the Ministry of Defence and Navy have reportedly\ndecreased, 60 of 240 complaints of arbitrary detention from 2018-2023 remain unresolved.\n2.1.4. Excessive use of force during arrests\nDuring its country visit, the Working Group learned that a significant number of detainees suffer serious and\nexcessive violence during their arrests, such as a detainee being shot in the stomach by police, resulting parts\nof his intestine being removed. International human rights mechanisms, including the Working Group in its\nopinions, have also referred to ill-treatment and torture committed by security forces in Mexico.\nOften beatings and torture are inflicted to extract confessions. Detainees are sometimes forced to give\ninformation about other alleged suspects or potential evidence and ill-treatment is also used as a matter of\nhumiliation and punishment. For example, indigenous persons are sometimes beaten to force them to accept\nresponsibility and called “indio” during arrests. A woman who was pregnant at the time of arrest and informed\nofficers of this was hit in the stomach area and then driven around for hours by police to various locations to\nlook for evidence to incriminate her and her boyfriend. Others were “taken for a drive”, during which officers\nor military members did not deliver them promptly to the nearest civil authority, prosecutor, or judge, but\ninstead took them to remote locations and inflicted violence against them with a view to forcing them to\nprovide incriminating evidence.\nThese risks are exacerbated by the fact that a violative practice has emerged whereby the 48 hours within\nwhich a detainee must be brought before a judge is only counted beginning from when the detainee is presented\nto the prosecutor. This means that the time spent in police (or other security force) custody is not included in\nthe counting of the 48 hours, as was also accepted by authorities. Given that the initial period after arrest is a\ncritical time when a detainee is in most jeopardy of unduly self-incriminating or otherwise prejudicing their\nrights, it is critical that the 48 hours is counted from the time of the initial physical deprivation of liberty, rather\nthan from some later point in time. The Working Group also notes with concern that the 48 hr period is\nfrequently doubled to 96 hours in cases of organized crime. Moreover, the Working Group found deficiencies\nin the recording of apprehensions, with a variety of books, spreadsheets and other lists used across various\ninstitutions without a consistent methodology. Regarding accountability, although the authorities did refer to\ninstances of security agents being investigated for violence against detainees, they did not provide details of\nany significant number of convictions for such activities.\n2.1.5. Abuse of flagrante delicto\nMany arrests in Mexico are carried out without a pre-existing warrant, under the justification of flagrante\ndelicto. Since 2016, the rules governing the accusatory criminal process require that people detained in\nflagrante delicto without an arrest warrant must be brought before a judge to carry out a control of the\ndetention. However, the Working Group notes that expanded interpretations of flagrante delicto continue to\n4\n\nbe used. These arrests under expanded interpretations violate human rights, as set out in several opinions by\nthe Group on Arbitrary Detention.\nThe 2021 national survey of persons deprived of liberty indicated that 23 percent of detainees were arrested\nin the streets without an arrest warrant and that over 40 percent claimed that they were falsely accused of\ncommitting a crime. The Working Group recalls that, where a warrant is not obtained in advance, the scope of\njudicial control over detention is restricted to an ex post facto measure, by which time a violation may have\noccurred. There are circumstances, such as flagrante delicto or being \"caught in the act\", in which there is no\nopportunity to obtain a warrant ex ante. However, these must be interpreted restrictively to avoid undermining\njudicial control of detention.\nIn this respect, the Working Group was concerned to learn that an extended version of flagrante delicto, known\nas flagrante por señalamiento, or flagrancy by report, is applied in Mexico, whereby a person can be detained\nwithout a warrant at a later point in time despite not having been caught in the act by an official with policing\npowers, so long as a witness, who may be a private person, reports having seen them committing a crime.\nAnother extended application of this notion relates to the situation when a person who allegedly committed a\ncrime is not arrested immediately but is purportedly followed for a certain amount of time via street cameras,\nand then only arrested at a later stage. These extended interpretations of flagrante delicto are not consistent\nwith human rights law and must be prohibited.\n2.1.6. Deprivation of liberty under drug policy\nFrom 2006, Mexico has taken a far more punitive approach towards the use and dealing of drugs. The\npossession of drugs is still criminalised and even possession at relatively low levels can be sufficient to meet\nthe threshold to qualify as dealing drugs. As a result, there has been an increase in the imprisonment of drug\nusers. The Working Group emphasises the importance of looking for alternatives to detention in this context.\nConcerningly, the Working Group was informed of instances in which security forces fabricated charges\nagainst persons by placing drugs in their homes, vehicles, bags, or clothes. Reportedly, this practice continues\nto be rife among security forces. Allegations of such manufacturing of crimes must be taken extremely\nseriously, thoroughly investigated, and appropriately sanctioned where established. Where security forces can\nbe equipped with body-cams or other recording devices, this can assist to a degree in reducing the possibility\nfor such abuses of power. Additionally, the Working Group learned that detainees are often pre-textually\ncharged for specific offences only to have them changed to different crimes once they are detained, which can\nundermine their rights to liberty, against self-incrimination and to an effective defence.\nThe Amnesty Law includes crimes related to drug offending, provided that they fall under federal jurisdiction.\nIt covers vulnerable persons such as those in extreme poverty or indigenous people. However, the Working\nGroup was told that only around 320 people had been released via amnesty, as the parameters of the law have\nbeen restrictively interpreted and the Amnesty Committee has only met five times in three years. The Working\nGroup recalls that detaining a person despite there being an amnesty law applicable to them constitutes\narbitrary detention under Category I. It strongly encourages the Amnesty Committee to avoid an overly\nrestrictive approach to Amnesty Law, and to ensure that it implements amnesty-based releases expeditiously.\nOn a related note, the Working Group observed that photos of detainees posed in front of tables displaying\ndrugs and weapons often appear in the media, sometimes with the names of detainees or other identifying\ninformation. Such images have the potential to undermine the presumption of innocence in any subsequent\ncriminal proceedings. Although authorities have reportedly prohibited their own forces from disseminating\nsuch images, the posing of arrested detainees and illicit materials for the purpose of taking photos cannot be\ndone without at least the complicity of the arresting forces. Whilst media freedom must be respected, the\nWorking Group implores the Government of Mexico to ensure that its forces are not complicit in disseminating\nsuch images to the public prior to or during the criminal trials of any such detainees.\n2.1.7. Abbreviated trial procedure\nSince 2016, an abbreviated trial procedure has been incorporated into Mexican legislation by the reform of\nthe criminal process. Although the purpose is to reduce the strain on the criminal justice system, the Working\n5\n\nGroup learned that detainees were pressured, often by their own lawyers (frequently public defenders), to\naccept responsibility in order to avoid a trial and possible greater sanction. Whereas it should always be open\nto guilty persons to acknowledge culpability, and whereas this can expedite proceedings and avoid re-\ntraumatizing victims, caution must be taken to ensure that such abbreviated procedures are not used as a means\nof dissuading detainees from exercising their legitimate fair trial rights, including the right to silence, the\npresumption of innocence, and the right to adequate time and facilities to conduct their defence.\n2.1.8. Bureaucratisation of the judicial appeal (amparo trial-habeas corpus)\nThe procedure known as amparo, a judicial remedy available in Mexico, is similar to a habeas corpus writ. It\nis provided for in the Constitution and the Amparo Act of 2013. This writ can protect human rights, including\nto liberty and life. Under Mexican law, the writ of amparo is federal remedy, irrespective of whether a case\nconcerns crimes falling under a State entity’s jurisdiction (“común” crimes).\nWhile this procedure allows for release, the Working Group found out that this rarely occurs. For example, in\nCDMX, only 16.5% of cases in which arbitrary detention is found to have occurred result in the immediate\nrelease of the detainee. Similarly, the Committee on Enforced Disappearance noted, as a result of its country\nvisit in 2021, that few judges make use the amparo procedure (“amparo buscador”).5 As a matter of procedure,\nthe Working Group was assured that judges can order additional information from the prosecution office or\nother authorities in order to address amparo requests. However, of the detainees who exercised the right, the\nvast majority had seen their amparo rejected without any further inquiry. Others had seen their amparo requests\nremain undecided for years, including one case in which an individual had remained in detention for 9 years\nwithout trial. Of the few who had successful amparo proceedings, they had nonetheless not been immediately\nreleased but some remained in detention awaiting a re-trial or reactivation of charges which had been held in\nabeyance while they served their existing sentence.\nThe Working Group considers that the amparo procedure has considerable potential to ensure that human\nrights are respected and that individuals are released where violations have occurred. However, the Mexican\njudiciary must ensure that judges vigorously exercise their powers under the amparo procedure. For its part,\nthe Government and the judicial branch must ensure that all judges are aware of their powers to permit release\npursuant to amparo requests, and that they are not retaliated against for doing so.\n2.1.9. Judicial independence\nAn independent and impartial judiciary is essential to combatting arbitrary detention, as reflected inter alia by\nArticle 14(1) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. The Working Group welcomes\npositive changes in this respect introduced with the reform of the criminal justice system. It was however\nconcerned to learn that members of the executive authorities have pressured judges, particularly when those\njudges have ruled that executive initiatives contravene the law. In some cases, executive members name\nindividual judges who issued decisions or opinions impugning government policies. More broadly, the federal\njudiciary is facing scathing budgetary cuts, reportedly up to 20 percent. Such measures undermine the ability\nof the judiciary to independently conduct its work without fear nor favour. The Working Group is concerned\nthat this has contributed to the ongoing persistence of high numbers of cases of arbitrary detention at both the\nfederal and state levels.\n2.1.10 Right to legal assistance\nThe Working Group welcomes the information that approximately 95 percent of individuals charged with\nfelonies are assigned public defenders to represent them free of charge. It also appreciates the efforts to provide\na wide range of important legal services, including legal assistance to migrants. Additionally, it notes that the\ntransition to an accusatory system has placed public defence lawyers on a more equal footing with the\nprosecution than previously. However, public defenders face heavy caseloads which compromise the quality\nof their services. Many detainees receive poor quality legal services. Moreover, the caseload results in a\nsignificant burden for the Public Defender’s Offices (PDOs), which has financial and staffing constraints.\n5 Para.59.\n6\n\nThe result is a heightened risk of arbitrary detention particularly for persons lacking socio-economic means to\nobtain private legal assistance. Additionally, many detainees lack regular access to public defence counsel\nonce detained; they are unable to contact lawyers by phone, as reportedly such phone calls are often not\nfacilitated free of charge and public defenders often ignore calls from a detention facility. The Working Group\nreiterates its call to strengthen the PDOs (building on its recommendation (d) of its 2002 country visit report).\nIt calls on the authorities to improve mechanisms for access to legal assistance (including through public\ndefenders), particularly for detained persons, in a regular and timely manner.\n2.2 Protections in detention\n2.2.1. Conditions of detention\nSafeguards for detained persons help to prevent arbitrary detention. Under human rights law, safeguards\ninclude facilitating prompt and continuing access to a defence lawyer, conducting a post-arrest medical\nexamination, and communications with third parties as well as visits by external oversight mechanisms.\nThe Working Group learned that these safeguards vary from one facility to another and are frequently not fully\nobserved by the authorities in Mexico. Detainees struggled to obtain access to a lawyer once detained. Many\ndetainees were only allowed one ten-minute phone call per 8 days, which they could use either to talk to their\nfamily or lawyer. Given that they usually choose to talk to family and given that lawyers rarely visit the\ndetention facilities which are often located in remote areas, this restriction has considerable potential to\nprejudice pre-trial detainees' exercise of fair trial rights.\nIll-treatment and violence during detention can also impact on a detainee’s ability to exercise their rights,\nparticularly if they suffer injuries as a result thereof. At the federal level, many detainees suffered excessive\nviolence during their arrests but that the Working Group heard that penitentiary staff generally did not use\nviolence against them. However, there were instances of violence by prison guards, indicating that this\nviolative practice has not been eliminated. There is also corruption in some facilities. Moreover, pre-trial\ndetainees (not subject to any conviction) were often housed together with convicted detainees. Several\ndetainees had serious wounds and illnesses and complained that they were unable to access effective medical\ntreatment. A particularly prevalent concern was the lack of medication available to unwell detainees. Many\ncould only get medication if their families obtained it for them. The Working Group emphasizes that where it\nis medically necessary, the authorities must ensure its availability to detainees. Detainees also complained\nabout improper food and the Working Group observed overcrowded conditions in some facilities, with\ndetainees sometimes sleeping on concrete floors without blankets being provided, which are not compatible\nwith human rights protections.\nIn some prisons at the federal and state levels, detainees are kept locked in their cells most of the day and\nrarely, if ever, leave the cell block in which they were housed. Prisons are hampered by chronic staff shortages,\nincluding in key roles such as security guards and prison medical staff. Those shortages impact on their ability\nto take detainees out of their cell blocks for exercise and activities. This was particularly noticeable at the\nmen's federal facilities visited, where many detainees had spent months without leaving their closed-in cell\nblocks.\nRegarding sanctions in detention, the Working Group noted that detainees found to violate prison rules were\noften placed in deplorable cells, with no bedding and only hard concrete floors to sleep on. Such conditions\nare not compatible with human rights guarantees as reflected in the Nelson Mandela Rules. Moreover, solitary\nconfinement was available for use as a punishment for up to 15 days for misconduct. In reality, however, the\nWorking Group found out that confinement for 30 days and more continues, albeit usually in small groups.\nThe Working Group recalls that Rule 45 of the Nelson Mandela Rules provides that solitary confinement shall\nbe used only in exceptional cases as a last resort, for as short a time as possible and subject to independent\nreview, and only pursuant to the authorization by a competent authority. It should be prohibited in the case of\nprisoners with psycho-social disabilities or physical disabilities.\nA systemic safeguard for detainees is prison registries. However, the Working Group found that registries in\nprisons were non-effective, as they were not consistently utilised and updated by the authorities. There were\n7\n\nconcerns raised about poor internet connectivity, old equipment, and a lack of trained human resources to\nupdate the registry. These shortcomings must be addressed to ensure this tool is available.\nWhile some prisons were under capacity, the Working Group also discovered that severe over-crowding in\nsome male prisons visited by the Group is of particular concern. In one of them, for example, 16 pre-trial\ndetainees are kept locked in small rooms. The Working Group calls upon the authorities to set minimum\nrequirements in respect of the floor space and cubic content of air, per inmate to respect human rights including\nthe right to adequate time and facilities to prepare a defence. It also reiterates the link between overcrowding\nand an excessive length of pre-trial detention, which has been found by this Group to violate the ICCPR, and\nencourages the authorities to resort to alternative preventive measures.\n2.3 People in vulnerable situations\n2.3.1. Migrants\nThe Working Group notes that Mexico is a transitory step for migrants seeking to access the United States of\nAmerica from Central and South America as well as a growing array of other states. Many of those people are\nasylum seekers. In recent years, the situation in Central America, Ecuador, and Venezuela has led to a dramatic\nincrease in the numbers of migrants passing through Mexico, or in some cases using it as destination country.\nFlamed by the migration policies of the United States of America, the situation has resulted in migrants often\nbeing subjected to conditions in contravention of Mexico's human rights commitments.\nLarge numbers of migrants and asylum seekers are detained in Mexico, amounting to over 240,000 in the first\nhalf of 2023. Under the Constitution, such administrative detentions should be limited to 36 hours and the\nSupreme Court has held that detention of migrants for 15-60 days under the Migration Law is unconstitutional.\nHowever, the Working Group discovered that a significant number of detained migrants are held beyond the\n36-hour time limit, which raises the risk of arbitrary detention. Moreover, the Working Group learned that\nwhen migrants who are detained for crimes seek to exercise legal rights, such as amparo, then they are often\ntold they will be kept for months instead of being let go in 36 hours. There should be no punishment for\nexercising legal rights, particularly in context of migration.\nA large number of children are detained in the context of migration. In 2022 alone, over 126,000 were\nchannelled into centres run by the National Agency for Family Development (“DIF”). Under Article 99 of the\nMigration Act, children are not permitted to be held in migrant detention. However, in practice the Working\nGroup found that children migrants were typically deprived of liberty, either in shelters solely for\nunaccompanied minors run by DIF or in facilities co-located with migrant facilities under the authority of the\nInstitute for Migration of the Ministry of the Interior.\nHaving visited children deprived of liberty under the authority of the DIF, the Working Group noted that,\nalthough they were fed and sheltered, it is important that the Mexican authorities provide them with sufficient\nopportunities to ensure their development is not harmed and to ensure that any such detention is exceptional,\nwhen alternatives to detention are manifestly unfeasible, and that any such detention is limited in time.\nHaving also visited children deprived of liberty with family members in facilities co-located with migration\ndetention facilities, the Working Group was concerned at the conditions. Children and their family members\nwere sleeping in courtyard areas outside the facility buildings, in the heat. Moreover, whilst law reforms had\nreportedly prohibited the detention of child migrants, the Working Group saw children who were manifestly\nbelow 18 (and in some cases clearly below 10 years of age) deprived of liberty in these areas co-located with\nmigration detention facilities.\nInside the migrant detention facility buildings themselves, the detained persons were not free to circulate\nwithin the facilities. Concerningly, the Working Group saw that metal gates with locks separated the rooms\nwhere migrants were housed from exits to the outside courtyards and guards prevented migrants from freely\naccessing those open-air locations. This also prevented migrants from accessing the complaints boxes of the\nrelevant human rights commissions. Given the fire at the Ciudad Juárez facilities in March 2023, in which 40\n8\n\npeople died and many were injured, the Working Group emphasizes that migrants should not be kept behind\nlocked doors and should have access to outdoor areas freely.\nMigrants also are subjected to frequent extortion of bribes by Mexican officials, often from the security forces.\nThose unable to pay were consequently detained, whereas others who were able to pay were allowed to go on\ntheir way. The widespread occurrence of such extortion risks creating an underground economy, which may\nbecome entrenched if security forces grow accustomed to extracting additional income in this unlawful\nmanner. Any such extortion must be prohibited and stamped out in law and in practice.\n2.3.2 Adolescents\nThe detention of adolescents from age 12 up to 17 in Mexico is subject to a special legal regime. Adolescents\nwho are detained are kept in separate facilities from adults. While the facilities were largely clean and the\nWorking Group did not learn of violence by staff or any other shortcomings within the course of the\nproceedings against them or in the detention, opportunities for external activities, such as visits to sporting\nand cultural events, were relatively limited and only available to small groups among the youths in the\nfacilities. Given the developmental stage of their lives, it is important that all the detained youths should have\nopportunities to participate in stimulating activities.\nMore concerningly, many adolescents experience considerable violence in their initial arrests and are not\nnotified of the reasons for the arrest. This is reflected in a national survey which revealed that 66 percent of\nyouths complained of suffering some kind of violent attack during their arrests. Youths were sometimes beaten\nby arresting forces who placed bags over their heads, and taken for drives during which they were pressured\nto confess to crimes. Moreover, adolescents are sometimes told by the defence lawyers to plead guilty to the\ncrimes in order to avoid going into the adult criminal justice system. The Working Group emphasizes the need\nfor adolescents to be given access to appropriately trained lawyers who rigorously defend their interests, given\nthe vulnerable age they are at and the detrimental impact that being incarcerated will typically have on their\nfuture lives and physical and psychological well-being.\n2.3.3 Indigenous people\nThe Working Group learned of instances in which indigenous people faced discrimination leading to their\narrest and subjection to considerable violence by security forces. These included cases where the indigenous\npersons were advocating for the rights of their people, including on governmental premises and projects, and\nwere charged with trespass and criminal damage. In this respect, the Working Group emphasizes that persons\nshould not be subjected to detention merely due to exercising their rights, and that this is particularly important\nfor indigenous groups engaging in non-violent protest actions. The Working Group notes that many indigenous\npersons advocate to protect the environment, and thus have a dual-faceted role, seeking to uphold the rights\nof their fellow indigenous people while also defending nature. Such activities should not be met by criminal\nsanction unless they involve serious violent offending or other grave crimes which cannot be addressed\nthrough alternative means. At the same time, if indigenous groups dispense traditional justice involving\ndetention, this must accord with Mexico’s human rights obligations including the prohibition of arbitrary\ndetention and the prohibition of torture, cruel, inhuman, and degrading treatment.\nFor indigenous persons in detention, which constitutes a significant proportion of detainees particularly in\nChiapas, the Working Group learned that culturally appropriate food was not consistently available and that\nthey were not consistently able to observe traditional practices. Regarding language, the Working Group notes\nthe efforts of the National Institute of Indigenous Peoples, which assisted over 500,000 people in 2022 and an\nincreasing number in 2023. This is important from the outset of deprivation of liberty, when the jeopardy of\nundermining legal rights is highest. The Working Group encourages the authorities to be proactive in training\nprosecutors, judges and other officials in the needs of indigenous persons in detention, which extend beyond\nlanguage and also encompass socio-economic challenges, remoteness, and alienation from their peoples.\n9\n\n2.3.4 Detention of persons with psycho-social disabilities\nAccording to the Ministry of Health, approximately 24 million persons in Mexico have psycho-social\ndisabilities, 3.5 million of which have chronic symptoms that seriously impact their lives. Many of these\npersons face detention. The Working Group emphasizes that persons with psycho-social disabilities should be\naddressed by the authorities under the framework of health treatment and consistently with the right to liberty.6\nUntil recently, individuals deemed incompetent to participate in criminal proceedings were nonetheless able\nto be sanctioned for crimes through detention for a period corresponding to the crime. In 2019, the General\nHealth Law was amended to introduce a new model for the treatment of those with psychiatric and/or\nsubstance abuse disorders and place more emphasis on the patients’ autonomy and freedom of choice of\ntreatment. If individuals are diagnosed as having psycho-social disabilities that preclude their fitness to stand\ntrial for a crime, they can be detained via an involuntary internment as a safety measure, and the period of\ninternment relates to the disorder of the individual rather than the nature of the crime committed.\nDespite these changes, the Working Group was concerned to discover that individuals with serious psycho-\nsocial disabilities detained in penitentiaries, along with persons convicted for serious crimes. Although those\nwith disorders were sometimes housed in separate blocks, they were otherwise subject to the same carceral\nconditions as the general population. Only negligible psycho-social disabilities treatment was available, with\nno psychiatrists among the prisons’ medical staff on site. It also emerged that persons deemed incapable of\nparticipating in trial proceedings are detained at prison facilities in some cases. The Working Group\nemphasizes that persons with serious psycho-social disabilities which preclude their competence to be tried\nunder criminal law should never be detained at criminal penitentiaries. Moreover, any person detained,\nincluding persons detained under the “safe-guarding” mechanism for their personal health, should be\nsystematically recorded in the National Register of Detentions, with appropriate care paid to protect their\nprivate personal data and health-related information.\n2.3.5 LGBTI+ persons\nWith respect to LGBTI+ persons, they were generally housed together to insulate against any potential\nharassment. They were reportedly not subjected to any notably different treatment to other detainees or other\ndiscrimination. However, prison officials noted that there were no particular protocols on how to address the\nparticular needs of members of this community in detention. The Working Group considers that the authorities\nshould undertake consultations to determine whether specific protocols are required beyond the existing laws\nand to identify best practices which could be followed.\n3. However, others whom the Working\nGroup has found to be arbitrarily detained remain in detention. Main Findings\nThe Working Group notes that arbitrary detention remains a widespread problem in Mexico due to a variety\nof causes and aggravating factors, both in the regulatory framework and in its implementation.\n\n2.1 Criminal Justice\n2.1.1. Mandatory Pre-trial Detention\nAlthough pre-trial detention should be regarded as an exceptional measure to ensure the availability of the\naccused during criminal proceedings, Article 19 of the Mexican Constitution sets out a list of felonies that\ntrigger mandatory pre-trial detention. In 2019, mandatory pre-trial detention was extended to 16 categories of\ncrimes, which are vaguely framed and susceptible to extension to an indeterminate range of offences. In 2021,\nmandatory pre-trial detention was introduced for other crimes.\nOf the approximately 90,000 people in pre-trial detention (“en proceso”) in 2022, around 50 percent are subject\nto mandatory pre-trial detention. Many of the detainees had been subjected to lengthy mandatory pre-trial\ndetention, including some who were still in mandatory pre-trial detention over 5 years after arrest. Worryingly,\nexcessively lengthy pre-trial detention was common amongst all detainees (noting that there was a total prison\npopulation of around 230,000 in Mexico in July 2023), not just those in mandatory pre-trial detention.\nThe Working Group is mindful that mandatory pre-trial detention has been identified as a violation of the right\nto personal liberty, as well as potentially undermining others, such as the presumption of innocence, the right\nto personal integrity and judicial independence. Mandatory pre-trial detention has been considered a violation\nof human rights in several opinions of the Working Group, including the Mexican context,4 and was declared\nto be a violation of Mexico’s obligations by the Inter-American Court of Human Rights, which ordered it to\nadjust its laws in this respect to meet international standards.\nThe Mexican authorities should reconcile its approach to preventive detention with international human rights\nlaw, by removing mandatory pre-trial detention from the Constitution and requiring that pre-trial detention\nmay only be applied based on an individualized assessment in which it is shown that the defendant is either a\nflight risk, at risk of serious re-offending, or at risk of tampering with evidence or witnesses.\n2.1.2. Arraigo (precautionary detention without charge)\nThe procedure known as arraigo was incorporated into the Constitution in 2008. It permits the authorities to\ndetain a person for up to 80 days for criminal investigation purposes in organized crime, without indicting that\nperson for a crime. The use of arraigo peaked between 2009 and 2012, but has decreased since 2013, and is\nrarely used these days. According to the information received by the Working Group, in 2022 it was used in\n25 cases. An attempt to remove arraigo from the Constitution in 2019 was unsuccessful.\nThe Inter-American Court of Human Rights and other international bodies have called for Mexico to eliminate\narraigo, as it violates the right to personal liberty and due process. Although authorities explained that arraigo\nis currently mainly used to protect witnesses in organised crimes cases, the Working Group notes that its\nongoing existence creates the risk of arbitrary detention. It encourages the Mexican authorities to remove the\nprocedure of arraigo from the Constitution and end its use as a basis for detention.\n\n\n\n\n3 UNGA, A/RES/53/144 8 March 1999 (“Declaration on the Right and Responsibility of Individuals, Groups and Organs of Society to Promote\n\nand Protect Universally Recognized Human Rights and Fundamental Freedoms”).\n4 See, e.g. Opinion Nos. 32/2023; 14/2021; 1/2018.\n\n                                                                                                                                          3\n\n2.1.3. The militarization of public security\nIn Mexico, the use of military forces to undertake public policing activities at the federal, state and municipal\nlevel is frequent. There are reportedly over one hundred thousand soldiers currently carrying out policing and\npublic security tasks, as well as migratory control. This has been closely linked to rises in violence against\ndetainees, disappearances, and homicides. Detainees arrested by military personnel frequently were subjected\nto serious violence and torture, including beatings with foreign objects.\nSymptomatic of the extensive use of military elements in policing is the 2019 formation of the National Guard.\nAccording to the Constitution, the National Guard falls under the Ministry of Security and Citizen’s Protection.\nAlthough the incorporation of military personnel into the National Guard has a transitory nature, the armed\nforces have been authorised to continue public security functions until 2028.\nWhereas the National Guard took over the functions of the Federal Police, its personnel are predominantly\nmilitary by background. Of its 128,000 members, around 48 percent come from the Army and Navy. Although\nmembers of the National Guard are trained inter alia in human rights and in gathering evidence, the Working\nGroup notes that there are ongoing reports of violence in arrests and arbitrary detention by military personnel.\nAlthough arbitrary arrests committed by personnel of the Ministry of Defence and Navy have reportedly\ndecreased, 60 of 240 complaints of arbitrary detention from 2018-2023 remain unresolved.\n2.1.4. Excessive use of force during arrests\nDuring its country visit, the Working Group learned that a significant number of detainees suffer serious and\nexcessive violence during their arrests, such as a detainee being shot in the stomach by police, resulting parts\nof his intestine being removed. International human rights mechanisms, including the Working Group in its\nopinions, have also referred to ill-treatment and torture committed by security forces in Mexico.\nOften beatings and torture are inflicted to extract confessions. Detainees are sometimes forced to give\ninformation about other alleged suspects or potential evidence and ill-treatment is also used as a matter of\nhumiliation and punishment. For example, indigenous persons are sometimes beaten to force them to accept\nresponsibility and called “indio” during arrests. A woman who was pregnant at the time of arrest and informed\nofficers of this was hit in the stomach area and then driven around for hours by police to various locations to\nlook for evidence to incriminate her and her boyfriend. Others were “taken for a drive”, during which officers\nor military members did not deliver them promptly to the nearest civil authority, prosecutor, or judge, but\ninstead took them to remote locations and inflicted violence against them with a view to forcing them to\nprovide incriminating evidence.\nThese risks are exacerbated by the fact that a violative practice has emerged whereby the 48 hours within\nwhich a detainee must be brought before a judge is only counted beginning from when the detainee is presented\nto the prosecutor. This means that the time spent in police (or other security force) custody is not included in\nthe counting of the 48 hours, as was also accepted by authorities. Given that the initial period after arrest is a\ncritical time when a detainee is in most jeopardy of unduly self-incriminating or otherwise prejudicing their\nrights, it is critical that the 48 hours is counted from the time of the initial physical deprivation of liberty, rather\nthan from some later point in time. The Working Group also notes with concern that the 48 hr period is\nfrequently doubled to 96 hours in cases of organized crime. Moreover, the Working Group found deficiencies\nin the recording of apprehensions, with a variety of books, spreadsheets and other lists used across various\ninstitutions without a consistent methodology. Regarding accountability, although the authorities did refer to\ninstances of security agents being investigated for violence against detainees, they did not provide details of\nany significant number of convictions for such activities.\n2.1.5. Abuse of flagrante delicto\nMany arrests in Mexico are carried out without a pre-existing warrant, under the justification of flagrante\ndelicto. Since 2016, the rules governing the accusatory criminal process require that people detained in\nflagrante delicto without an arrest warrant must be brought before a judge to carry out a control of the\ndetention. However, the Working Group notes that expanded interpretations of flagrante delicto continue to\n\n                                                                                                                      4\n\nbe used. These arrests under expanded interpretations violate human rights, as set out in several opinions by\nthe Group on Arbitrary Detention.\nThe 2021 national survey of persons deprived of liberty indicated that 23 percent of detainees were arrested\nin the streets without an arrest warrant and that over 40 percent claimed that they were falsely accused of\ncommitting a crime. The Working Group recalls that, where a warrant is not obtained in advance, the scope of\njudicial control over detention is restricted to an ex post facto measure, by which time a violation may have\noccurred. There are circumstances, such as flagrante delicto or being \"caught in the act\", in which there is no\nopportunity to obtain a warrant ex ante. However, these must be interpreted restrictively to avoid undermining\njudicial control of detention.\nIn this respect, the Working Group was concerned to learn that an extended version of flagrante delicto, known\nas flagrante por señalamiento, or flagrancy by report, is applied in Mexico, whereby a person can be detained\nwithout a warrant at a later point in time despite not having been caught in the act by an official with policing\npowers, so long as a witness, who may be a private person, reports having seen them committing a crime.\nAnother extended application of this notion relates to the situation when a person who allegedly committed a\ncrime is not arrested immediately but is purportedly followed for a certain amount of time via street cameras,\nand then only arrested at a later stage. These extended interpretations of flagrante delicto are not consistent\nwith human rights law and must be prohibited.\n2.1.6. Deprivation of liberty under drug policy\nFrom 2006, Mexico has taken a far more punitive approach towards the use and dealing of drugs. The\npossession of drugs is still criminalised and even possession at relatively low levels can be sufficient to meet\nthe threshold to qualify as dealing drugs. As a result, there has been an increase in the imprisonment of drug\nusers. The Working Group emphasises the importance of looking for alternatives to detention in this context.\nConcerningly, the Working Group was informed of instances in which security forces fabricated charges\nagainst persons by placing drugs in their homes, vehicles, bags, or clothes. Reportedly, this practice continues\nto be rife among security forces. Allegations of such manufacturing of crimes must be taken extremely\nseriously, thoroughly investigated, and appropriately sanctioned where established. Where security forces can\nbe equipped with body-cams or other recording devices, this can assist to a degree in reducing the possibility\nfor such abuses of power. Additionally, the Working Group learned that detainees are often pre-textually\ncharged for specific offences only to have them changed to different crimes once they are detained, which can\nundermine their rights to liberty, against self-incrimination and to an effective defence.\nThe Amnesty Law includes crimes related to drug offending, provided that they fall under federal jurisdiction.\nIt covers vulnerable persons such as those in extreme poverty or indigenous people. However, the Working\nGroup was told that only around 320 people had been released via amnesty, as the parameters of the law have\nbeen restrictively interpreted and the Amnesty Committee has only met five times in three years. The Working\nGroup recalls that detaining a person despite there being an amnesty law applicable to them constitutes\narbitrary detention under Category I. It strongly encourages the Amnesty Committee to avoid an overly\nrestrictive approach to Amnesty Law, and to ensure that it implements amnesty-based releases expeditiously.\nOn a related note, the Working Group observed that photos of detainees posed in front of tables displaying\ndrugs and weapons often appear in the media, sometimes with the names of detainees or other identifying\ninformation. Such images have the potential to undermine the presumption of innocence in any subsequent\ncriminal proceedings. Although authorities have reportedly prohibited their own forces from disseminating\nsuch images, the posing of arrested detainees and illicit materials for the purpose of taking photos cannot be\ndone without at least the complicity of the arresting forces. Whilst media freedom must be respected, the\nWorking Group implores the Government of Mexico to ensure that its forces are not complicit in disseminating\nsuch images to the public prior to or during the criminal trials of any such detainees.\n2.1.7. Abbreviated trial procedure\nSince 2016, an abbreviated trial procedure has been incorporated into Mexican legislation by the reform of\nthe criminal process. Although the purpose is to reduce the strain on the criminal justice system, the Working\n                                                                                                               5\n\nGroup learned that detainees were pressured, often by their own lawyers (frequently public defenders), to\naccept responsibility in order to avoid a trial and possible greater sanction. Whereas it should always be open\nto guilty persons to acknowledge culpability, and whereas this can expedite proceedings and avoid re-\ntraumatizing victims, caution must be taken to ensure that such abbreviated procedures are not used as a means\nof dissuading detainees from exercising their legitimate fair trial rights, including the right to silence, the\npresumption of innocence, and the right to adequate time and facilities to conduct their defence.\n2.1.8. Bureaucratisation of the judicial appeal (amparo trial-habeas corpus)\nThe procedure known as amparo, a judicial remedy available in Mexico, is similar to a habeas corpus writ. It\nis provided for in the Constitution and the Amparo Act of 2013. This writ can protect human rights, including\nto liberty and life. Under Mexican law, the writ of amparo is federal remedy, irrespective of whether a case\nconcerns crimes falling under a State entity’s jurisdiction (“común” crimes).\nWhile this procedure allows for release, the Working Group found out that this rarely occurs. For example, in\nCDMX, only 16.5% of cases in which arbitrary detention is found to have occurred result in the immediate\nrelease of the detainee. Similarly, the Committee on Enforced Disappearance noted, as a result of its country\nvisit in 2021, that few judges make use the amparo procedure (“amparo buscador”).5 As a matter of procedure,\nthe Working Group was assured that judges can order additional information from the prosecution office or\nother authorities in order to address amparo requests. However, of the detainees who exercised the right, the\nvast majority had seen their amparo rejected without any further inquiry. Others had seen their amparo requests\nremain undecided for years, including one case in which an individual had remained in detention for 9 years\nwithout trial. Of the few who had successful amparo proceedings, they had nonetheless not been immediately\nreleased but some remained in detention awaiting a re-trial or reactivation of charges which had been held in\nabeyance while they served their existing sentence.\nThe Working Group considers that the amparo procedure has considerable potential to ensure that human\nrights are respected and that individuals are released where violations have occurred. However, the Mexican\njudiciary must ensure that judges vigorously exercise their powers under the amparo procedure. For its part,\nthe Government and the judicial branch must ensure that all judges are aware of their powers to permit release\npursuant to amparo requests, and that they are not retaliated against for doing so.\n2.1.9. Judicial independence\nAn independent and impartial judiciary is essential to combatting arbitrary detention, as reflected inter alia by\nArticle 14(1) of the International Covenant on Civil and Political Rights. The Working Group welcomes\npositive changes in this respect introduced with the reform of the criminal justice system. It was however\nconcerned to learn that members of the executive authorities have pressured judges, particularly when those\njudges have ruled that executive initiatives contravene the law. In some cases, executive members name\nindividual judges who issued decisions or opinions impugning government policies. More broadly, the federal\njudiciary is facing scathing budgetary cuts, reportedly up to 20 percent. Such measures undermine the ability\nof the judiciary to independently conduct its work without fear nor favour. The Working Group is concerned\nthat this has contributed to the ongoing persistence of high numbers of cases of arbitrary detention at both the\nfederal and state levels.\n2.1.10 Right to legal assistance\nThe Working Group welcomes the information that approximately 95 percent of individuals charged with\nfelonies are assigned public defenders to represent them free of charge. It also appreciates the efforts to provide\na wide range of important legal services, including legal assistance to migrants. Additionally, it notes that the\ntransition to an accusatory system has placed public defence lawyers on a more equal footing with the\nprosecution than previously. However, public defenders face heavy caseloads which compromise the quality\nof their services. Many detainees receive poor quality legal services. Moreover, the caseload results in a\nsignificant burden for the Public Defender’s Offices (PDOs), which has financial and staffing constraints.\n\n\n5 Para.59.\n\n                                                                                                                 6\n\nThe result is a heightened risk of arbitrary detention particularly for persons lacking socio-economic means to\nobtain private legal assistance. Additionally, many detainees lack regular access to public defence counsel\nonce detained; they are unable to contact lawyers by phone, as reportedly such phone calls are often not\nfacilitated free of charge and public defenders often ignore calls from a detention facility. The Working Group\nreiterates its call to strengthen the PDOs (building on its recommendation (d) of its 2002 country visit report).\nIt calls on the authorities to improve mechanisms for access to legal assistance (including through public\ndefenders), particularly for detained persons, in a regular and timely manner.\n\n2.2 Protections in detention\n2.2.1. Conditions of detention\nSafeguards for detained persons help to prevent arbitrary detention. Under human rights law, safeguards\ninclude facilitating prompt and continuing access to a defence lawyer, conducting a post-arrest medical\nexamination, and communications with third parties as well as visits by external oversight mechanisms.\nThe Working Group learned that these safeguards vary from one facility to another and are frequently not fully\nobserved by the authorities in Mexico. Detainees struggled to obtain access to a lawyer once detained. Many\ndetainees were only allowed one ten-minute phone call per 8 days, which they could use either to talk to their\nfamily or lawyer. Given that they usually choose to talk to family and given that lawyers rarely visit the\ndetention facilities which are often located in remote areas, this restriction has considerable potential to\nprejudice pre-trial detainees' exercise of fair trial rights.\nIll-treatment and violence during detention can also impact on a detainee’s ability to exercise their rights,\nparticularly if they suffer injuries as a result thereof. At the federal level, many detainees suffered excessive\nviolence during their arrests but that the Working Group heard that penitentiary staff generally did not use\nviolence against them. However, there were instances of violence by prison guards, indicating that this\nviolative practice has not been eliminated. There is also corruption in some facilities. Moreover, pre-trial\ndetainees (not subject to any conviction) were often housed together with convicted detainees. Several\ndetainees had serious wounds and illnesses and complained that they were unable to access effective medical\ntreatment. A particularly prevalent concern was the lack of medication available to unwell detainees. Many\ncould only get medication if their families obtained it for them. The Working Group emphasizes that where it\nis medically necessary, the authorities must ensure its availability to detainees. Detainees also complained\nabout improper food and the Working Group observed overcrowded conditions in some facilities, with\ndetainees sometimes sleeping on concrete floors without blankets being provided, which are not compatible\nwith human rights protections.\nIn some prisons at the federal and state levels, detainees are kept locked in their cells most of the day and\nrarely, if ever, leave the cell block in which they were housed. Prisons are hampered by chronic staff shortages,\nincluding in key roles such as security guards and prison medical staff. Those shortages impact on their ability\nto take detainees out of their cell blocks for exercise and activities. This was particularly noticeable at the\nmen's federal facilities visited, where many detainees had spent months without leaving their closed-in cell\nblocks.\nRegarding sanctions in detention, the Working Group noted that detainees found to violate prison rules were\noften placed in deplorable cells, with no bedding and only hard concrete floors to sleep on. Such conditions\nare not compatible with human rights guarantees as reflected in the Nelson Mandela Rules. Moreover, solitary\nconfinement was available for use as a punishment for up to 15 days for misconduct. In reality, however, the\nWorking Group found out that confinement for 30 days and more continues, albeit usually in small groups.\nThe Working Group recalls that Rule 45 of the Nelson Mandela Rules provides that solitary confinement shall\nbe used only in exceptional cases as a last resort, for as short a time as possible and subject to independent\nreview, and only pursuant to the authorization by a competent authority. It should be prohibited in the case of\nprisoners with psycho-social disabilities or physical disabilities.\nA systemic safeguard for detainees is prison registries. However, the Working Group found that registries in\nprisons were non-effective, as they were not consistently utilised and updated by the authorities. There were\n                                                                                                               7\n\nconcerns raised about poor internet connectivity, old equipment, and a lack of trained human resources to\nupdate the registry. These shortcomings must be addressed to ensure this tool is available.\nWhile some prisons were under capacity, the Working Group also discovered that severe over-crowding in\nsome male prisons visited by the Group is of particular concern. In one of them, for example, 16 pre-trial\ndetainees are kept locked in small rooms. The Working Group calls upon the authorities to set minimum\nrequirements in respect of the floor space and cubic content of air, per inmate to respect human rights including\nthe right to adequate time and facilities to prepare a defence. It also reiterates the link between overcrowding\nand an excessive length of pre-trial detention, which has been found by this Group to violate the ICCPR, and\nencourages the authorities to resort to alternative preventive measures.\n\n2.3 People in vulnerable situations\n2.3.1. Migrants\nThe Working Group notes that Mexico is a transitory step for migrants seeking to access the United States of\nAmerica from Central and South America as well as a growing array of other states. Many of those people are\nasylum seekers. In recent years, the situation in Central America, Ecuador, and Venezuela has led to a dramatic\nincrease in the numbers of migrants passing through Mexico, or in some cases using it as destination country.\nFlamed by the migration policies of the United States of America, the situation has resulted in migrants often\nbeing subjected to conditions in contravention of Mexico's human rights commitments.\nLarge numbers of migrants and asylum seekers are detained in Mexico, amounting to over 240,000 in the first\nhalf of 2023. Under the Constitution, such administrative detentions should be limited to 36 hours and the\nSupreme Court has held that detention of migrants for 15-60 days under the Migration Law is unconstitutional.\nHowever, the Working Group discovered that a significant number of detained migrants are held beyond the\n36-hour time limit, which raises the risk of arbitrary detention. Moreover, the Working Group learned that\nwhen migrants who are detained for crimes seek to exercise legal rights, such as amparo, then they are often\ntold they will be kept for months instead of being let go in 36 hours. There should be no punishment for\nexercising legal rights, particularly in context of migration.\nA large number of children are detained in the context of migration. In 2022 alone, over 126,000 were\nchannelled into centres run by the National Agency for Family Development (“DIF”). Under Article 99 of the\nMigration Act, children are not permitted to be held in migrant detention. However, in practice the Working\nGroup found that children migrants were typically deprived of liberty, either in shelters solely for\nunaccompanied minors run by DIF or in facilities co-located with migrant facilities under the authority of the\nInstitute for Migration of the Ministry of the Interior.\nHaving visited children deprived of liberty under the authority of the DIF, the Working Group noted that,\nalthough they were fed and sheltered, it is important that the Mexican authorities provide them with sufficient\nopportunities to ensure their development is not harmed and to ensure that any such detention is exceptional,\nwhen alternatives to detention are manifestly unfeasible, and that any such detention is limited in time.\nHaving also visited children deprived of liberty with family members in facilities co-located with migration\ndetention facilities, the Working Group was concerned at the conditions. Children and their family members\nwere sleeping in courtyard areas outside the facility buildings, in the heat. Moreover, whilst law reforms had\nreportedly prohibited the detention of child migrants, the Working Group saw children who were manifestly\nbelow 18 (and in some cases clearly below 10 years of age) deprived of liberty in these areas co-located with\nmigration detention facilities.\nInside the migrant detention facility buildings themselves, the detained persons were not free to circulate\nwithin the facilities. Concerningly, the Working Group saw that metal gates with locks separated the rooms\nwhere migrants were housed from exits to the outside courtyards and guards prevented migrants from freely\naccessing those open-air locations. This also prevented migrants from accessing the complaints boxes of the\nrelevant human rights commissions. Given the fire at the Ciudad Juárez facilities in March 2023, in which 40\n\n\n                                                                                                               8\n\npeople died and many were injured, the Working Group emphasizes that migrants should not be kept behind\nlocked doors and should have access to outdoor areas freely.\nMigrants also are subjected to frequent extortion of bribes by Mexican officials, often from the security forces.\nThose unable to pay were consequently detained, whereas others who were able to pay were allowed to go on\ntheir way. The widespread occurrence of such extortion risks creating an underground economy, which may\nbecome entrenched if security forces grow accustomed to extracting additional income in this unlawful\nmanner. Any such extortion must be prohibited and stamped out in law and in practice.\n2.3.2 Adolescents\nThe detention of adolescents from age 12 up to 17 in Mexico is subject to a special legal regime. Adolescents\nwho are detained are kept in separate facilities from adults. While the facilities were largely clean and the\nWorking Group did not learn of violence by staff or any other shortcomings within the course of the\nproceedings against them or in the detention, opportunities for external activities, such as visits to sporting\nand cultural events, were relatively limited and only available to small groups among the youths in the\nfacilities. Given the developmental stage of their lives, it is important that all the detained youths should have\nopportunities to participate in stimulating activities.\nMore concerningly, many adolescents experience considerable violence in their initial arrests and are not\nnotified of the reasons for the arrest. This is reflected in a national survey which revealed that 66 percent of\nyouths complained of suffering some kind of violent attack during their arrests. Youths were sometimes beaten\nby arresting forces who placed bags over their heads, and taken for drives during which they were pressured\nto confess to crimes. Moreover, adolescents are sometimes told by the defence lawyers to plead guilty to the\ncrimes in order to avoid going into the adult criminal justice system. The Working Group emphasizes the need\nfor adolescents to be given access to appropriately trained lawyers who rigorously defend their interests, given\nthe vulnerable age they are at and the detrimental impact that being incarcerated will typically have on their\nfuture lives and physical and psychological well-being.\n2.3.3 Indigenous people\nThe Working Group learned of instances in which indigenous people faced discrimination leading to their\narrest and subjection to considerable violence by security forces. These included cases where the indigenous\npersons were advocating for the rights of their people, including on governmental premises and projects, and\nwere charged with trespass and criminal damage. In this respect, the Working Group emphasizes that persons\nshould not be subjected to detention merely due to exercising their rights, and that this is particularly important\nfor indigenous groups engaging in non-violent protest actions. The Working Group notes that many indigenous\npersons advocate to protect the environment, and thus have a dual-faceted role, seeking to uphold the rights\nof their fellow indigenous people while also defending nature. Such activities should not be met by criminal\nsanction unless they involve serious violent offending or other grave crimes which cannot be addressed\nthrough alternative means. At the same time, if indigenous groups dispense traditional justice involving\ndetention, this must accord with Mexico’s human rights obligations including the prohibition of arbitrary\ndetention and the prohibition of torture, cruel, inhuman, and degrading treatment.\nFor indigenous persons in detention, which constitutes a significant proportion of detainees particularly in\nChiapas, the Working Group learned that culturally appropriate food was not consistently available and that\nthey were not consistently able to observe traditional practices. Regarding language, the Working Group notes\nthe efforts of the National Institute of Indigenous Peoples, which assisted over 500,000 people in 2022 and an\nincreasing number in 2023. This is important from the outset of deprivation of liberty, when the jeopardy of\nundermining legal rights is highest. The Working Group encourages the authorities to be proactive in training\nprosecutors, judges and other officials in the needs of indigenous persons in detention, which extend beyond\nlanguage and also encompass socio-economic challenges, remoteness, and alienation from their peoples.\n\n\n\n\n                                                                                                                 9\n\n2.3.4 Detention of persons with psycho-social disabilities\nAccording to the Ministry of Health, approximately 24 million persons in Mexico have psycho-social\ndisabilities, 3.5 million of which have chronic symptoms that seriously impact their lives. Many of these\npersons face detention. The Working Group emphasizes that persons with psycho-social disabilities should be\naddressed by the authorities under the framework of health treatment and consistently with the right to liberty.6\nUntil recently, individuals deemed incompetent to participate in criminal proceedings were nonetheless able\nto be sanctioned for crimes through detention for a period corresponding to the crime. In 2019, the General\nHealth Law was amended to introduce a new model for the treatment of those with psychiatric and/or\nsubstance abuse disorders and place more emphasis on the patients’ autonomy and freedom of choice of\ntreatment. If individuals are diagnosed as having psycho-social disabilities that preclude their fitness to stand\ntrial for a crime, they can be detained via an involuntary internment as a safety measure, and the period of\ninternment relates to the disorder of the individual rather than the nature of the crime committed.\nDespite these changes, the Working Group was concerned to discover that individuals with serious psycho-\nsocial disabilities detained in penitentiaries, along with persons convicted for serious crimes. Although those\nwith disorders were sometimes housed in separate blocks, they were otherwise subject to the same carceral\nconditions as the general population. Only negligible psycho-social disabilities treatment was available, with\nno psychiatrists among the prisons’ medical staff on site. It also emerged that persons deemed incapable of\nparticipating in trial proceedings are detained at prison facilities in some cases. The Working Group\nemphasizes that persons with serious psycho-social disabilities which preclude their competence to be tried\nunder criminal law should never be detained at criminal penitentiaries. Moreover, any person detained,\nincluding persons detained under the “safe-guarding” mechanism for their personal health, should be\nsystematically recorded in the National Register of Detentions, with appropriate care paid to protect their\nprivate personal data and health-related information.\n2.3.5 LGBTI+ persons\nWith respect to LGBTI+ persons, they were generally housed together to insulate against any potential\nharassment. They were reportedly not subjected to any notably different treatment to other detainees or other\ndiscrimination. However, prison officials noted that there were no particular protocols on how to address the\nparticular needs of members of this community in detention. The Working Group considers that the authorities\nshould undertake consultations to determine whether specific protocols are required beyond the existing laws\nand to identify best practices which could be followed.\n3. Implementation and follow-up of the Working Group’s opinions\nDuring the visit, the Working Group met multiple individuals who had been released from detention as a result\nof being subjects of its opinions. This step demonstrates a commitment of the authorities to improving rights\nprotection and compliance with the international human rights norms. However, others whom the Working\nGroup has found to be arbitrarily detained remain in detention. The Working Group calls on the Mexican\nauthorities to systematically implement all opinions which find that arbitrary detention has occurred. The\nGroup further encourages to close gaps in domestic accountability by investigating cases of arbitrary detention\nand paying compensation to the victims, as well as following other recommendations set up in its opinions. It\ncalls on the authorities to conduct public outreach campaigns to raise public awareness of human rights,\nincluding the prohibition of arbitrary detention.\n                                                  Conclusion\nThese are the preliminary findings of the Working Group.
