# 8B. Ruler Effectiveness and Competence

Status: draft; v4 objective-evidence questions require controlled rejudgment

## Chapter Identity

- Chapter: `8B`
- Category: `ruler_effectiveness_and_competence`
- Evidence strategy: one ruler-period dossier, researched across ten overlapping lenses
- Judge strategy: one chapter judge applies one comparative meter across all eligible rulers
- Rubric version: `chapter_8b_v4`

## Chapter Purpose

Assess how effectively the ruler defines a governing program, converts it into
state or ruling-network action, coordinates implementation, adapts, and advances
their own stated or reliably revealed objectives. The ten questions are evidence
lenses feeding one final 1-10 chapter score, not equal sub-scores.

This chapter is ideology-neutral. Moral worth and execution competence are
separate. A ruler may execute a cruel, aggressive, corrupt, or authoritarian
program competently and score highly in 8B while scoring very poorly in other
chapters. Never smuggle moral approval or condemnation into the competence score.

## Ten Evidence Lenses

| Lens | Simple question | Detailed research question | Priority evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| **8B.1 — Clear governing program** | Were the ruler's real goals clear enough to evaluate? | Did the ruler state or reliably reveal a sufficiently clear program in dated speeches, manifestos, strategies, directives, or formal acts to freeze and test its policy, ideological, power, and international goals? | **Rhetoric and representations**; **Formal acts and law** |
| **8B.2 — Turning goals into machinery** | Did the ruler turn goals into concrete plans, rules, resources, people, and institutions? | Did the ruler translate that program into enacted laws, budgets, appointments, timelines, institutions, regulations, and enforcement mechanisms within actual authority? | **Formal acts and law**; **Personnel**; **Implementation and operational conduct** |
| **8B.3 — Mobilizing the ruling system** | Did the ruler effectively mobilize the state and ruling network toward those goals? | Did executed resources and administrative records show effective mobilization of the state, party, military, coalition, or ruling network toward the ruler's chosen program? | **Resources**; **Personnel**; **Implementation and operational conduct** |
| **8B.4 — Capable implementers** | Did the ruler choose and manage people capable of carrying out the program? | Did the ruler appoint, empower, retain, and when necessary replace people capable of executing the program, whether professionals, technocrats, organizers, loyal operators, or coercive administrators? | **Personnel**; **Implementation and operational conduct**; **Outcomes** |
| **8B.5 — Coordination and control** | Did the ruler maintain coordination and follow-through across the system? | Did the ruler maintain documented coordination, territorial reach, milestone completion, and compliance across ministries, regions, institutions, security forces, and implementing agencies? | **Implementation and operational conduct**; **Outcomes** |
| **8B.6 — From plans to practice** | Did the ruler turn plans and announcements into real government action? | Did legislation, budgets, and directives become observable enforcement, services, projects, and institutional practice rather than remain slogans, plans, or symbolic acts? | **Formal acts and law**; **Implementation and operational conduct**; **Outcomes** |
| **8B.7 — Progress toward chosen goals** | Did results move toward the ruler's own goals? | Did outcome and distribution indicators move toward the ruler's frozen goals after accounting for baseline, realistic lag, authority, external shocks, and plausible causal alternatives? | **Outcomes**; **Implementation and operational conduct** |
| **8B.8 — Learning and correction** | Did the ruler adapt and correct course when methods failed? | Did audits, evaluations, and implementation failures lead the ruler to adapt methods, replace implementers, reallocate resources, and correct course? | **Personnel**; **Implementation and operational conduct**; **Outcomes** |
| **8B.9 — Managing crises and resistance** | Did the ruler handle crises and resistance without losing the chosen program? | Did formal decisions and implemented responses to crises, opposition, international relations, and institutional resistance preserve or advance the ruler's chosen objectives and durable control? | **Formal acts and law**; **Implementation and operational conduct**; **Outcomes** |
| **8B.10 — Durable goal achievement** | By the end, had the ruler achieved more of the program in a lasting way? | By the end of the period, had the ruler converted more of the frozen program into durable law, institutions, capacity, state practice, and achieved outcomes than at the start, accounting for failures and long-term fragility? | **Outcomes**; **Formal acts and law**; **Implementation and operational conduct** |

exclusive source list. Missing categories do not invalidate a lens.

## Scope and Non-Goals

- Establish an explicit or reliably revealed program before judging execution.
  Repeated discriminating choices may reveal goals; hindsight speculation may not.
- Evaluate strategic clarity, operationalization, mobilization, personnel fit,
  coordination, implementation, outcomes, adaptation, crisis management, and
  durable progress as mutually informing evidence.
- Do not judge whether goals are democratic, peaceful, lawful, prosperous, humane,
  or personally honest. Those judgments belong in 1B-7B.
- Do not reward repression, propaganda, longevity, survival, economic growth,
  administrative size, or institutional quality by itself. Ask whether it
  effectively served the ruler's identified program.
- Do not assume failure from an outcome the ruler did not seek, except where
  maintaining basic government function was necessarily part of governing.
- Do not use the client matrix as evidence.

Before reading outcomes or assigning a score, freeze a portfolio of the ruler's major
stated or reliably revealed programs plus necessary core-government functions. Apply
the same breadth rule across regime types. Do not cherry-pick the easiest achieved
goal, reduce a democratic government to its broadest welfare promises, or reduce an
authoritarian government to survival, weapons, or power consolidation. Survival and
consolidation may be one material program component, never the entire portfolio by
default.

## Researcher Workflow and Evidence Themes

Follow the local-first researcher guide and source-confidence registry. Query the
exact case locally. WGI, V-Dem, BTI, budgets, and outcome indicators are useful
context or corroboration, but generic state capacity is not ruler effectiveness.

Research chronologically across themes rather than issuing one search per lens:

1. Identify stated programs from platforms, manifestos, coalition agreements,
   speeches, strategies, directives, budgets, and plans. Identify revealed goals
   only from repeated choices that distinguish them from rhetoric or speculation.
2. Trace conversion into priorities, resources, laws, institutions, appointments,
   timelines, enforcement, and measurable milestones.
3. Examine whether the state, party, military, coalition, bureaucracy, territorial
   network, or informal machinery actually mobilized, coordinated, and followed
   through; include bottlenecks and implementation gaps.
4. Assess whether personnel were capable for the assigned program. Ideological or
   coercive operators may be effective implementers in this narrow chapter even
   when their conduct is reprehensible elsewhere.
5. Compare promised/revealed objectives with implementation and outcomes after
   realistic lags, inherited trends, peer conditions, shocks, and resources.
6. Trace learning, replacement, reallocation, crisis response, resistance
   management, short-term gains, reversals, durability, and end-of-period position.

Prefer primary program and budget documents, legislation, administrative and
audit records, implementation data, credible outcome series, contemporaneous
records, serious academic histories, independent evaluations, and reputable
reporting. Official sources establish declared goals and formal actions but not
successful execution by themselves. Preserve failures, contradictions, abandoned
priorities, propaganda-only claims, alternative causal explanations, and adverse
side effects; record the latter for cross-chapter use without morally deducting
them in 8B.

Reuse each stable evidence item across relevant lenses. Natural-language coverage
and gaps are acceptable; publication must not depend on exact status terms,
sequential IDs, or exhaustive mapping syntax.

## Common Evidence-Item Semantics

Each material evidence item should carry a stable evidence ID; a concise factual
claim; source URL, title, publisher, and publication/access date where available;
source type and confidence with a short reason; a quotation or precise locator
when available; target-year/ruler-period fit; ruler attribution and
inherited-baseline relevance; its positive, negative, mixed, contextual, or
contrary role; and the supported lens IDs. Fields may be normalized later and
natural-language equivalents are acceptable. The substantive minimum is a
traceable source, intelligible claim, temporal fit, attribution, and evidence
role. One item may support multiple lenses without duplication.

## Attribution, Sparse Evidence, and Historical Cases

Separate ruler capability from inherited state capacity, predecessor programs,
subordinate initiative, favorable trends, external patrons, and luck. Assess
formal/informal control, tenure, coalition and institutional constraints,
resources, shocks, lag, ownership of appointments and priorities, response to
implementation information, and plausible counterfactual trajectory.

For obscure or historical rulers, later scholarship, archives, diplomatic
records, budgets, institutional histories, and observable implementation may be
more informative than missing modern indicators. Oral or informal programs can
be evidenced by repeated choices. Sparse evidence should reduce confidence and
widen the defensible range, not force a neutral score or invalidate the case.
Short tenure may permit judgment of direction and mobilization while leaving
outcomes unresolved. Closed systems may document plans abundantly while hiding
failures; official volume is not proof of execution.

## Holistic 1-10 Chapter Rubric

Synthesize the causal chain from program through action to durable progress.
Lenses are redundant and qualitatively weighted: clear plans with no execution
cannot score highly; favorable outcomes without ruler-attributable direction or
action warrant limited credit; a major strategic collapse can outweigh several
administrative successes. Never calculate a simple mean or score by citation
count.

Strategic clarity, centralized control, personnel discipline, and planning without
independently evidenced implementation and goal progress cannot exceed 5. Scores above
5 require attributable implementation plus progress across material parts of the
frozen portfolio. Scores above 7 additionally require independent evidence of outcomes,
durability, and adaptation rather than official architecture, coercion, longevity, or
survival alone. Harmful programs remain morally neutral here, but they must still show
actual advancement rather than mere capacity to command.

| Score | Chapter anchor |
|---:|---|
| 1 | No coherent program or effective governing control, or catastrophic inability to mobilize and execute: recurring chaos, paralysis, collapse, or decisive movement away from the ruler's own objectives. |
| 2-3 | Weak or unstable direction and serious operational failure; plans remain largely symbolic, coordination repeatedly breaks down, adaptation is poor, and major objectives substantially recede. |
| 4-5 | Mixed competence: some clear priorities and real delivery coexist with major implementation gaps, inconsistent control, failed initiatives, weak adaptation, or fragile/temporary results. |
| 6-7 | Generally effective direction and execution across important priorities, with meaningful mobilization and progress but notable delays, failures, constraints, reversals, or durability concerns. |
| 8-9 | Strong strategic-operational alignment, capable personnel and coordination, adaptive implementation, and substantial durable advancement of the ruler's program across major priorities. |
| 10 | Exceptional, sustained mastery of direction, mobilization, execution, adaptation, crisis/resistance management, and durable goal realization under demanding conditions. |

These anchors apply equally to benevolent and harmful programs. The rationale
must separately name the program and state that moral effects are evaluated in
other chapters.

## Confidence

Report confidence separately on a 0-100 scale. Consider program identification,
ruler attribution, causal trace from inputs to implementation/outcomes, source
independence, time fit and lag, external-shock controls, source/theme diversity,
closed-system opacity, and contrary explanations. Missing lenses lower confidence
rather than automatically lowering the chapter score. Give a defensible score
range where evidence or attribution is materially uncertain.

## One-Judge Comparative Workflow

One judge owns 8B across all eligible rulers for the target year/period:

1. Read every 8B dossier, identify each ruler's explicit or reliably revealed
   program, and quarantine identity failures—not sparse evidence.
2. Separate moral evaluation from execution before scoring. Record cross-chapter
   harms without deducting them here.
3. Establish batch anchors spanning democratic and authoritarian, beneficial and
   harmful, modern and historical, high- and low-capacity contexts.
4. Assign tentative holistic scores along the program-to-results chain, then
   compare neighbors for consistent treatment of inherited capacity, resources,
   shocks, tenure, lag, luck, resistance, and durability.
5. Revisit cases dominated by generic governance indicators, official claims,
   survival/tenure, favorable outcomes without attribution, or moral halo/horns.
6. Emit one chapter score, confidence/range, identified program, rationale,
   strongest execution and failure evidence, unresolved causal alternatives,
   missing themes, and review flag.

## Acceptance Checklist

- Missing implementation evidence never establishes ineffectiveness; it produces a
  null when no ruler-attributed execution fact remains. Scores above `5` require
  observable implementation or coordination, and scores above `7` additionally
  require independent evidence of attributable outcomes, adaptation, or durable
  progress across more than one material program component.

- All ten authoritative lenses are present verbatim and serve one holistic score.
- Ideology-neutrality is explicit and moral worth is separated from execution in
  research, scoring, calibration, and rationale.
- An explicit or reliably revealed program is established before execution is
  judged; hindsight speculation is labeled and constrained.
- Local-first order, citations, source profiling, and run profile are retained.
- Evidence items satisfy the common traceability, temporal-fit, attribution,
  evidence-role, and lens-link semantics.
- Ruler action is separated from inherited capacity, subordinates, luck, trends,
  shocks, tenure, and realistic outcome lags.
- Missing lenses reduce confidence rather than invalidate or mechanically depress
  the score.
- One judge compares all rulers using one meter; lenses are neither equally
  weighted nor mechanically averaged.
- Final output uses the common chapter-judge envelope, including adjacent-anchor
  comparisons and an explicit manual-review flag.
- Smoke testing and revision occur before production activation.

Como muestra, en ese año se dejó de gastar el 100% (2.2 mmdp) del presupuesto para la sustitución del Hospital General de San Alejandro Puebla o el 70% (2.7 mmdp) para la sustitución de equipo médico a nivel nacional, según la Cartera de Inversión 2019.\nEs común que a inicios de un Gobierno se reduzca la inversión, a causa del relevo de personal y la curva de aprendizaje del equipo entrante. Berg\nand\n\nEmiliano Polo\nPublished September 5, 2023\nThe armed forces’ accelerating takeover of civil institutions will be one of the main\nlegacies\nof Mexican president Andrés Manuel López Obrador (AMLO). A 2021\nstudy\nby CIDE, a Mexican research organization and university, found that between 2006 and 2021, over 246 government functions previously overseen by civilian authorities were transferred to the Mexican armed forces.\nThe pace of handovers has only accelerated over the past four and a half years. Mexico’s armed forces now oversee\nmajor infrastructure construction\n, the\ndevelopment of tourism\n, and the\nmanagement of customs\nat ports of entry and exit, among other things. The Mexican army, for instance, is accustomed to playing a role in domestic security, but previous efforts to limit and even conceal this fact have now been overturned by the disregard and even encouragement of military takeover of civilian tasks shown by AMLO. To sum up, the use of Mexico’s armed forces—as seen typically in the militarization of the country’s internal security policy—has evolved, altering the Mexican political system as a whole and giving rise to a new phenomenon: militarism—a political project that seeks to place a purportedly “incorruptible” armed forces at the center managing key sectors such as customs,\nmigration\n, infrastructure, and public health.\nWhile the Mexican army reconciled its involvement in internal security long ago, Mexico’s\nconstitution is clear\nabout the limited role of the armed forces during peacetime: “During peacetime, no military authority can exercise more functions than those that have an exact connection with military discipline.” Furthermore, in a\n2018 ruling\nagainst Mexico, the Inter-American Court of Human Rights indicated that using the armed forces in public security could only be implemented following strict criteria of exceptionality and subordination to civilian authorities. From the start of his administration, AMLO has attempted to belittle this legal framework with several\nunconstitutional attempts\nto hand the army direct control over Mexico’s public security. The recently created National Guard for example is a de facto military body with no civilian supervision, despite\npromises\nto the contrary.\n\nYet, recent developments have taken Mexico even further from civilian oversight in both public security and important state functions. What distinguishes the current moment is that Mexico’s new militarism is not a security strategy—\nnot even a failed one\n, as AMLO’s administration has been the “\nbloodiest” in Mexico’s history\n, recording nearly as many homicides in four and half years as his predecessors did in six.\nRather, Mexico’s militarism is a political undertaking. It features the use of the army for political ambitions, where appeals to “public security” are just the smoke screen and costume. Specifically, political projects run by the armed forces benefit from the aegis of “national security,”\nexempting them\nfrom traditional scrutiny, greater transparency and public accountability, and the congressional budget process.\nSecond, Mexico’s militarism has not resulted in a strengthening of the armed forces, instead weakening the Mexican army by granting the institution manifold public contracts without the requisite anticorruption safeguards, violating transparency and tender requirements in Mexico, and bestowing the armed forces with civilian functions that, given their training, they are unprepared to accomplish. To name a few examples, the Mexican armed forces have launched a\nnew airline\n, oversee\ncustoms\nand\nadminister ports and airports\n, build\nsignature pieces of public infrastructure\n, and\ndistribute textbooks\nto schools.\nRemote Visualization\nThe Transformation of a Storied Institution\nIn the past, the Mexican government had a\ntacit and pragmatic understanding\nwith its armed forces. The terms of that agreement implied a high degree of autonomy—and, at times, opacity, and a tolerance of corruption—in exchange for distance from politics and the civil administration of government. Owing to this arrangement, Mexico is one of the few countries in Latin America that did not experience a military coup in the twentieth century. The understanding and objective within the Mexican political elite was to keep the military “far away and happy.” Now, Mexico’s new militarism implies that some elites within the Mexican army are now “close and angry” as they are ordered to perform tasks for which they are unprepared.\nThis transformation has political implications that are not yet fully appreciated—neither in Mexico nor in the United States, the country’s\n\nclosest security\nand economic partner.\nFirst, militarism implies a shift in the armed forces’ loyalties and allegiances from a constitutional and institutional framework to personal and partisan fidelity. Militarism will upset the army’s erstwhile positive reputation as corruption scandals inevitably accumulate and the force becomes identified as supportive of one political party or, even worse, AMLO’s political project.\nFurther, militarism has blurred the lines between state, government, and party responsibilities. The division between national security and public safety is now scrambled with the defense of a political party. For the first time in modern history, a minister of defense recently\nsupported\nAMLO’s Morena Party publicly, while the general in charge of the National Guard publicly included a Supreme Court judge on a “\nblacklist\n” for being against the Morena Party’s project. If the Mexican army identifies itself with a political party that persistently defines its enemies, it will eventually assume those same enemies, increasingly directing its resources to political targets.\nSecond, the army will become a less reliable and potentially unstable actor in Mexico. In the past, the Mexican army described itself as an institution that transcended the political context and looked beyond the current government; today, it has subordinated itself to not just the government but to the Morena Party.\nThird, Mexico’s militarism implies a different rationale for government action, usually outside the civilian considerations that should prevail in democratic systems. Government agencies and offices, many recently\novertaken\nby military personnel, require and are highly dependent on civil bureaucracies with highly technical know-how and personnel. For several institutions, such as\nCofepris\n, Mexico’s regulatory agency in charge of approving medicines and preventing health risks, a military command instantly renders technocrats superfluous and futile. Military codes such as efficiency and control are rarely the only guiding principles in civil bureaucracies.\nThe process of growing the armed forces’ role implies a certain contradiction: extending huge state budgets and responsibilities while assigning tasks incompatible with their core competencies. The budgets for the armed forces have\nsteadily increased\nsince 2018, yet budgets approved by Congress are just one part of the economic component of militarism. The military’s current economic sway is also comprised of revenue sources that are difficult to trace, including participation in\npublic trusts\n,\nstate-owned companies\n, and\ninfrastructure projects.\nThe widening gap between what the armed forces are asked to do and what they can do will corrode one of their main attributes: discipline and order. Militarism implies the relaxation of military discipline as an attempt to comply with a growing package of political responsibilities.\nUnder a constitutional framework, and prior to AMLO’s election, Mexico’s\ncivil bureaucracies and public institutions\n\nconstructed\npolitical channels\nto protect the army, allowing it to remain largely apolitical. Historically, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the Ministry of the Interior worked as intermediaries, interlocutors, and shock absorbers between the Mexican army and U.S. government agencies, for example. Addressing sensitive issues with foreign governments through civil institutions served well to protect Mexico’s armed forces from politicization.\nIt is inappropriate for the Mexican army to handle most dialogues with foreign or international security agencies single-handedly. The armed forces are not prepared—nor should they be—to deal with the public and the political scrutiny that militarism entails. The current spotlight will not translate into the political competence necessary to deal with the public scrutiny entailed by being a politicized army. Instead, this will open another flank for an already besieged institution. Mexico’s army was not built to make or handle the country’s complicated foreign policy.\nPutting the Genie Back in the Bottle\nFortunately, Mexico’s militarism is not an ineluctable process—especially\nafter AMLO’s current term ends\n. Further, the armed forces are not a homogeneous entity. Militarism in a highly vertical institution has exacerbated internal divisions and driven resentments between the military elite, which benefits from corruption and sweetheart deals, and the large swath of soldiers in the lower ranks. Corruption seldom benefits all members of an institution evenly, and opportunities for enrichment presented by militarism have been circumscribed to the upper rungs of the armed forces. The best known\ncorruption scandals\nare—and will likely remain—connected to the top echelons of the military hierarchy. Within the vast array of newfangled tasks, the natural tendency of the armed forces will be to focus on those representing large profits and less risk, such as customs control and public infrastructure construction projects.\nThe process of militarism also entails a blow to Mexico’s federalism. Militarism includes capturing\nlocal and municipal governments\nand agencies. Military personnel have been appointed to key local and municipal security positions regularly. Likewise, Mexico’s army delivers social programs and benefits as an essential component of militarism, as this enhances its political role (and benevolence) in the eyes of citizens. For example, the army was responsible for\nconstructing\nthe government-owned branches of banks that deliver monthly payments to elderly people. Less than a year into his administration, AMLO\ndeclared that\n“if it were up to him, he would disappear the armed forces,” (author’s translation). His actions have confirmed this intention, but not in the obvious sense. In this case, the army’s disappearing act would mean corrupting it, and eventually annexing it as a partisan tool for Morena.\nWhen thinking about bilateral relations, the United States should be wary of the military’s growing role, disguised as efficiency. Earlier attempts at militarization have not shown the desired results—a growing ideology of militarism could bring even worse results. A bilateral relationship channeled through the armed forces will likely turn more dysfunctional and fragile, as attempts at cooperation will be filled with suspicion, given that the army is unaccustomed to working with the types of transparency requirements promoted by civil institutions. Such an arrangement could be tempting given that within the context of U.S.-Mexico security cooperation, the armed forces, especially the army and the navy, are recognized as some of the most capable partners in Latin America.\nWith presidential elections coinciding in both countries in 2024, it will be no easy endeavor, but the current rhetoric about unilateral military action against Mexican drug cartels should cease in favor of\nmore effective policy options\n. Blowing up fentanyl labs or capturing high-profile drug kingpins might bring politically valuable photo ops in the short term. Still, this is far from a\nstrategy\nthat bets on building\ntrust and reliable institutional capacity\n. Worse still, such a strategy risks accelerating Mexico’s descent into militarism.\n\nThere are historical examples that should guide the U.S. approach without exacerbating Mexico’s tendency to advance militarism. During the mid-1990s, the\nHigh-Level Contact Group\nwas established between Mexico’s Foreign Ministry and the U.S. Department of State to promote coordination among different law enforcement agencies, share information, build confidence, and coordinate security efforts and objectives in a more technical manner, distant from quotidian political exigencies. In 2007, those efforts eventually blossomed into the Mérida Initiative, a wide-ranging, bipartisan effort that embraced the “shared responsibility” required to bring greater security to both Mexico and the United States. Today, the United States would benefit from strengthening civil institutions and intelligence-sharing mechanisms with Mexico and should seek to work with and support politicians who condemn the ideology of militarism. Despite AMLO’s rhetoric, defending civil institutions is not an improper foreign intervention; the civil control of government is mandated in the\nMexican constitution\n, and the United States will always benefit from defending the rule of law and constitutionalism in Mexico.\nFor its part, Mexico’s\nnext president will face\na dangerous dilemma. They will inherit the risks of confronting the\nvested economic and political interests\nthat AMLO’s militarism has engendered, especially within elite circles of the armed forces. Yet no risk is greater than the inertia of the current trajectory of militarism. As time passes, Mexico’s descent into militarism will be harder to course correct, and by comparison, the risks of facing the armed forces will always be smaller than the risk of advancing militarism in a constitutional democracy.\nIn this endeavor, Mexico’s next president may find allies in strange places. Indeed, members of the armed forces who believe in their institutional responsibility and do not agree with their current unconstitutional role, will be crucial allies in rolling back this new militarism. A\nrecent ruling\nby Mexico’s Supreme Court that military personnel are public servants and must disclose financial information, overturning the AMLO government’s attempt to hide the personal financial statements of generals who have been accused of corruption in the construction of Mexico City’s new airport, will assist in efforts to hold those who benefit from militarism accountable.\nThe Mexican president has driven the country’s politics to a critical juncture. AMLO came to power promising a “\nfourth transformation\n” that intended to sunder political and economic power once and for all. The great irony is that AMLO has reinforced the nexus between political and economic power in dangerous ways. Nowhere is this more obvious than in the armed forces’ growing economic and political clout. While AMLO and the ideology of militarism maintain that the military is “incorruptible,” the institution has grown so powerful as to decide\nnot to cooperate and turn over evidence\nin the country’s most outrageous human rights violation in recent memory: the case of the mass disappearance of 43 university students in Ayotzinapa. Under AMLO, the armed forces enjoy\nimpunity\nas far as the eye can see.\nMexico’s new militarism is rapidly transforming and politicizing a storied and indispensable Mexican institution. More importantly, militarism has already spawned profound and pernicious consequences on Mexico’s young constitutional democracy. Candidates would do well to address the impact of the still underappreciated offshoots of militarism and how it can be rolled back as they gear up for elections in 2024.\nRyan C. SUMMARY OF PROGRAM IMPLEMENTATION\nProgram and Portfolio Performance\n13. World Bank lending to Mexico has increased, driven by high growth in policy lending. IBRD lending\nhas increased by more than 25 percent in this CPF period relative to the previous one (FY14-19), propelled\nby an eightfold increase in DPF net commitments. At the end of FY23, total approved lending during the CPF\nperiod was US$4.3 billion (US$3.9 billion in DPF), versus US$3.4 billion during the previous CPF (US$0.5 billion\nin DPF). One additional DPF is planned for FY24. IFC’s commitments increased from US$1.62 billion in FY14-\n17 to US$1.75 billion in FY20-23. Annex 5 provides an overview of portfolio performance.\n14. Higher demand for policy lending and reduced appetite for investment lending can be traced to a\ncombination of global challenges and domestic policy shifts. Multiple global crises made fast-disbursing DPF\noperations the instrument of choice. Domestically, policy priorities were focused on a few large public\ninvestment projects, limiting space in the federal budget for other CPF focus areas. The government also\nwithdrew earlier requests for World Bank support in several areas, indicating shifting priorities or views of\nthe benefits of external project financing. Changing policy directions, institutional challenges, high turnover\nin key institutions, and security obstacles further affected policy continuity, implementation, and results.\nWith the 2024 electoral transition approaching, the window of opportunity for new policy reforms and the\npreparation of any further IPF pipeline is now limited.29\n15. The World Bank showed flexibility and pragmatism to deliver results through two main\nengagement strategies. First, IBRD responded to changing policy priorities by focusing on policy lending\nduring COVID-19 while continuing to invest in selected shared priorities: forestry preservation, financial\ninclusion, an improved business environment, and gender equality. IFC introduced upstream pre-investment\nprograms to support market creation and project structuring from an early stage, complemented by advisory\nprojects on climate, gender, and digitalization. Second, at client request, several investment operations were\nrestructured by the Bank, with cancellations of US$894 million out of US$1.28 billion in net commitments.\n16. The World Bank has maintained a strong analytical program to inform DPFs, to add value where\nmore intensive financial engagement was not possible, and to explore new entry-points. Demand-driven\nAdvisory Services and Analytics (ASA) informed key issues across the CPF objectives and maintained World\nBank engagement in many areas in the absence of lending (e.g., education, labor market policy, climate\nchange). The Reimbursable Advisory Services (RAS) instrument – a powerful instrument for middle-income\ncountries like Mexico – has been deployed in the transport sector, with a new agreement signed in July 2023.\n17. The CPF’s intended subnational focus on Mexico’s southern states was advanced through policy\nlending and advisory services. The CPF aimed for a greater impact on Mexico’s southern states, where a\nlarge share of the poorest population lives. This objective was affected by the reduced IPF program to\nchannel further resources to the poorest states in the south, as legal and institutional obstacles prevent direct\nlending to subnational government in Mexico. CPF design needs to adjust to the policy framework and operating environment, taking a\npragmatic, flexible, and creative approach to opportunities and risks. A realistic assessment of country\ncontext – policy priorities, financing constraints, political economy, and implementation capacity and\nlikelihood – is crucial. While the pandemic was impossible to predict, other governance, institutional, and\nsectoral risks could have been assessed more realistically and the program designed accordingly. The PLR\nbuilds on these lessons, with greater program selectivity and a more realistic risk assessment, seeking\nopportunities and balancing what is desirable with what is politically and technically possible at the federal,\nsubnational and sector levels. The Evolution Roadmap, with an enhanced value proposition for middle-\nincome countries, opens opportunities for a more integrated one World Bank Group approach at the project\nlevel and in joint knowledge engagements, such as on nearshoring, climate finance, financial inclusion, digital,\nenergy supply reliability and water. Global knowledge initiatives (e.g., the World Development Report on\nmigration) under the new Knowledge Compact may open doors to new forms of collaboration.\n34. Policy-lending can provide rapid support in response to crises, helping finance public expenditures\n32 Among them the European Commission, Germany, Spain, Sweden, Japan, United Kingdom, United States, and Australia.\n7\n\nand laying the foundations for a resilient recovery, while fostering innovations. In recent decades, Mexico\nhas followed a pattern of countercyclical lending, increasing exposure in downturns and crises (e.g., new\ncommitments of US$10.5 billion in FY08-10 and US$3.6 billion FY20-22). During COVID-19, three of four DPFs\napproved in FY20-22 directly supported pandemic crisis response and recovery, including the first “Build Back\nBetter” DPF. DPFs also supported innovative policy reforms (e.g., fintech, gender, and sustainable finance).\n35. IPF preparation can be challenging and fragile, yet investment lending operations have usually led\nto positive outcomes when implemented. Despite an investment pipeline reflecting the strength of the\ndialogue in many sectors, the government made a series of decisions not to proceed with many IPF\noperations. Complex project design, including inter- and intra-institutional coordination challenges,\nimplementation delays, partial cancellations, have jeopardized impact. Of the five IPF projects that closed in\nFY20-23, three were found satisfactory by the IEG (e.g., simple project design and client ownership\ncontributed to successful implementation). Four additional IPF projects are rated moderately satisfactory or\nbetter and are expected to achieve their objectives by closing in FY25. Reduced appetite for IPF and\nrestrictions on the use of this and other instruments (e.g., PforR) call for a close dialogue on design features,\nimplementation challenges, lessons learned to develop a new federal and potential also subnational pipeline.\n36. Analytical work has added technical value to policy making and served as a technical foundation\nfor policy lending. In the absence of new investment lending, the ASA program has positioned the World\nBank as a trusted adviser on evidence-based policy reforms, strengthening DPF design. Expansion of financial\naccess in Mexico was supported through the Financial Inclusion DPF, the COVID-19 Financial Access DPF, and\nthe Promoting Women’s Economic Opportunities and Sustainable Productivity Growth DPF. These operations\nbuilt on the 2016 and 2022 Financial Sector Assessment Programs, the Financial Inclusion Global Initiative,\nthe Gender Report, and IFC support on business regulations and SME access to credit.\n37. For IFC, deepening the relationship with the Ministry of Finance, diversifying business\ndevelopment efforts across industries and client types, while also emphasizing non-financial benefits, is\nexpected to both mitigate sector concentration risk and strengthen IFC’s value proposition. IFC investments\nin renewable energy in Mexico have grown substantially, representing almost 100 percent of new IFC\ninfrastructure investments from FY18 to FY20. Subsequent difficulties in the sector created a shock to IFC’s\ninfrastructure target, which was only partially mitigated by increased support for digital infrastructure. IFC’s\ndiversification to new players within the financial system and its use of the highest environmental, social, and\ngovernance standards have enhanced IFC’s contribution to the financial inclusion and climate agenda,\nincluded under CPF focus areas A and C. The latter can be illustrated by IFC investments in the Sustainable\nBond issued by GF Mega, structured under a sustainability framework to promote financial inclusion,\nwomen’s empowerment, and climate-friendly practices; and the Partial Credit Guarantee to IDEI Green Bond\nIssuance, to finance Excellence in Design for Greater Efficiencies (EDGE)-certified green residential projects.\nSynergies and complementarities among IFC, IBRD, and MIGA can also strengthen product and market\ndiversification.\nV.
