### Memo X

Concrete strengths:

- It discovers substantially more decision-relevant evidence: the invasion plan’s failure, war crimes and repression, mobilization, Central Bank stabilization, poverty data, NATO applications, grain diplomacy, and military expenditure.
- Attribution is consistently calibrated. For example, it assigns Putin strong responsibility for initiating the invasion but only moderate responsibility for tactical failures and credits Nabiullina and the Central Bank directly for financial stabilization.
- It handles legal evidence carefully: the ICC warrant is identified as a reasonable-grounds finding rather than a conviction, while the UN General Assembly resolution is not misrepresented as an individual criminal judgment.
- Contrary evidence is unusually useful rather than perfunctory: retained territory, mobilization’s eventual stabilizing effect, smaller numbers of Ukrainian violations, lower-than-forecast GDP contraction, reported poverty reduction, and continued non-Western relationships.
- Its information-environment analysis is excellent. It distinguishes official records of acts from the truth of official claims, explains unequal access to occupied areas, addresses polling preference falsification, and notes wartime statistical revisions.
- Its deeper-research questions are specific enough to guide document acquisition and attribution work.

Defects:

- Some locators point to summaries, graphics, report records, or index pages rather than the strongest underlying document. Examples include the Kremlin transcript index, the illustrated Commission of Inquiry findings, and the World Bank report record.
- The “Other useful sources” section contains numerous promising leads but often no extracted source-claim unit. These should not receive the same evidentiary credit as the 13 developed findings.
- A few formulations outrun strict 2022 period fit. “Caused NATO to expand” is stronger than the contemporaneous evidence of Finnish and Swedish applications; completed accession occurred later.
- The RUSI operational finding depends partly on undisclosed Ukrainian material. Memo X acknowledges this, but the claim about a ten-day plan still needs independent corroboration in later work.
- The same broad facts recur in the overview, evidence entries, limitations, source inventory, and research questions. The repetition helps readability but reduces efficiency.
- Some “complicating evidence” is contextual rather than genuinely contrary—for example Russian legal or security justifications that the cited institution rejected.

### Memo Y

Concrete strengths:

- Its atomic source-claim records are highly reusable. Canonical fact keys, locators, period fit, attribution limits, and contrary evidence make ingestion and deduplication easier.
- It exercises excellent attribution discipline. The mobilization decree proves Putin’s decision but not implementation quality; the Commission findings do not establish his individual criminal responsibility; Central Bank stabilization is not credited solely to him.
- Primary and authoritative records are used well for office, legal acts, aggression, annexation, and institutional response.
- Duplication control is better than in X. Reuse of the Carnegie source is divided into distinct facts about invasion decision-making and elite constraints.
- It is efficient and candid about gaps, especially the absence of a defensible personal-integrity finding.

Defects:

- It omits several of the year’s most material evaluative domains: the failed Kyiv campaign, casualties and operational performance, NATO enlargement consequences, detailed mobilization outcomes, household welfare, public opinion, military expenditure, and diplomacy.
- Its information-environment discussion is correct but thin compared with X. It does not develop access asymmetry, statistical suppression, polling bias, interested belligerent sources, or the distinction between exile evidence and domestic evidence.
- Source diversity is narrower: Carnegie, RUSI, HRW, and official/institutional records do much of the work.
- Several locators use “web lines,” which may be extraction-interface artifacts rather than stable locators for later researchers.
- Chapter-level wildcard mappings are convenient but do not show that the evidence was matched to particular questions.
- “Sources opened and retained” adds little beyond the accepted units and does not document cross-checking in comparable depth.

### Verdict

**Better memo: X.**

The difference is meaningful but not overwhelming. X is clearly the better reconnaissance artifact for later deep research because it discovers more material evidence and analyzes evidentiary limitations more deeply. Y is better at packaging and efficiency.

For Putin alone, the difference is **not large enough to justify abandoning the Y-style prompt wholesale**. It is large enough to revise the prompt toward X’s evidentiary breadth and bias analysis while retaining Y’s atomic output contract.

Valuable features from Y to preserve:

- Canonical fact keys and atomic source-claim units.
- Explicit period-fit and attribution-limit fields.
- Stable distinction between an accepted finding and a future search lead.
- Concise chapter-gap inventory.
- Strong duplication control and refusal to infer personal corruption from regime-level evidence.

### Memo X

Concrete strengths:

- It captures the central evaluative paradox: Tshisekedi consolidated national political authority but lacked reliable territorial, security, and administrative control.
- Its coverage is unusually well balanced across security, civil liberties, elections, macroeconomic management, education, rural development, extractive governance, Indigenous rights, and environmental policy.
- It makes careful causal and authority distinctions. M23 and foreign supporters are separated from Tshisekedi’s responsibility for strategic response; individual security-force abuses are separated from responsibility for maintaining the state of siege.
- Favorable evidence is substantive: IMF performance criteria, mining-led growth, free primary education, EITI validation, EAC accession, Indigenous-rights legislation, and the PDL-145T launch.
- It does not confuse announced programs with delivered results. The PDL-145T discussion explicitly treats ceremonies and appropriations as inputs, while the education section distinguishes enrollment from learning quality.
- It identifies a valuable data-reconciliation problem: UCDP’s apparent zero internationalized-conflict result versus UN evidence of cross-border involvement.
- Its research questions are exceptionally actionable, identifying exact audits, annexes, disaggregated data, court files, procurement records, and outcome measures needed next.
- Information-environment analysis is tailored to the DRC rather than generic: conflict access, witness risk, territorial fragmentation, “coupage,” media ownership, international-attention bias, and retrospective contamination are all addressed.

Defects:

- The section asserting independent evidence of Rwandan assistance is not perfectly aligned with the cited June 2022 Group of Experts report as described. The memo partly relies on later findings summarized by HRW; the exact evidentiary chain should be separated more clearly.
- Some evidence is temporally indirect: a 2021 Crisis Group analysis establishes the inherited coalition structure, while the Carter Center’s 2024 report tests risks through the later 2023 election. The memo flags this but still devotes substantial space to it.
- Several program claims depend on organizations financing or administering the programs, particularly the World Bank and UNDP. Their evidence is useful for design and rollout but weaker for independent effectiveness.
- The overview’s broad characterization of control could use a dated 2022 parliamentary or coalition record rather than relying primarily on the 2021 realignment.
- Source lists include materials merely located or only lightly used. The CENI page that timed out is appropriately disclosed but should remain a lead, not evidence.
- The artifact is sprawling. HRW, IMF, World Bank, and UN materials recur across developed findings, other sources, limitations, questions, and inventories.
- A few precise figures are presented through secondary summaries when the underlying tables or audit should be obtained—for example the Gécamines amount and some conflict totals.

### Memo Y

Concrete strengths:

- It provides clean, machine-reusable evidence units with strong attribution cautions.
- Several selected facts are particularly valuable: court noncompliance in Kabund’s detention, the 69% armed-group share of documented conflict abuses, the Buhene investigation failure, and the mixed audit-court/IGF evidence.
- It avoids blaming Tshisekedi personally for national conditions or individual abuses without a direct nexus.
- Contrary evidence is integrated well: amnesty alongside Kabund’s detention, plural media alongside state pressure, armed-group responsibility alongside state abuses, and reform activity alongside persistent impunity.
- The chapter-gap section is concise and correctly prioritizes exact institutional authority, decision chronology, disaggregated UNJHRO evidence, service-delivery outcomes, and a direct personal-integrity nexus.
- Its research-to-volume ratio is excellent.

Defects:

- It leaves out several of X’s most useful policy areas: free primary education as an implemented flagship, PDL-145T, EITI validation, oil and gas auctions, detailed electoral preparation, and the Nairobi/Luanda/EAC diplomatic response.
- It relies heavily on the US State Department report as an intermediary for UNJHRO, IGF, detention, media, and corruption claims. Later work would benefit from opening the underlying UN and Congolese records directly.
- Locator quality is uneven. “Opening political background,” broad report sections, and “search/open record discussion” are less durable than page, paragraph, table, or operative-clause references.
- The World Bank partnership announcement establishes intentions and baseline problems, not actual 2022 delivery; its accepted claim is therefore less material than X’s policy-specific education evidence.
- The information-environment section lacks X’s DRC-specific depth on media payments, ownership, local access, witness safety, geographic bias, and retrospective contamination.
- It does not explicitly reconcile conflicting datasets such as the UCDP internationalization issue.
- Wildcard methodology mappings are less useful than question-specific coverage.
- Source diversity is limited by repeated use of HRW and the State Department synthesis.

### Verdict

**Better memo: X.**

The difference is large enough to justify changing the prompt if the intended output is a substantive reconnaissance dossier for deep researchers. X finds materially more credible policy and outcome evidence, offers better balance, and produces a much stronger acquisition agenda.

The prompt should not simply ask for greater length. It should require:

- Coverage across security, institutions, economy, welfare, integrity, and effectiveness.
- Extracted evidence rather than source discovery.
- A dedicated information-environment analysis.
- Explicit separation of inherited conditions, presidential decisions, implementation, and outside constraints.
- A bounded number of atomic, reusable evidence units.

Valuable features from Y to preserve:

- Structured source-claim records.
- Canonical fact keys and clear dispositions.
- Compact chapter-gap summaries.
- Direct recognition that state abuses and armed-group abuses are different underlying facts.
- Strong personal-attribution safeguards, especially for corruption and chain-of-command claims.
- Efficient treatment of contrary evidence.

# Overall recommendation

Adopt a **hybrid prompt based primarily on Memo X’s research expectations and Memo Y’s evidence packaging**.

Require broad but bounded reconnaissance, direct inspection of the strongest underlying sources, stable locators, material contrary evidence, and domain-specific reporting-bias analysis. Then require every accepted finding to be emitted as a Y-style atomic source-claim unit. Separate three categories explicitly:

1. opened and extracted evidence;
2. opened only for corroboration;
3. discovered leads requiring later inspection.

This would preserve X’s superior discovery and analytical value while reducing repetition, unstable locators, and source-list inflation. Across both rulers, X wins consistently enough that the prompt should move toward its research behavior, but Y’s structure and efficiency should be retained rather than discarded.
