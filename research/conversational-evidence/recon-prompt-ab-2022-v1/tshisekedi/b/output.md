## 1. Overview of the ruler and the period

Félix-Antoine Tshisekedi Tshilombo was president of the Democratic Republic of the Congo throughout 2022, having taken office on 24 January 2019. The presidency is the head of state in a formally semi-presidential constitutional system; Prime Minister Jean-Michel Sama Lukonde headed the government and depended on a parliamentary majority. The president had particularly important influence over foreign and security policy, senior appointments, intelligence, the Republican Guard, emergency powers, and the governing coalition. [The presidency’s official biography](https://www.presidence.cd/president/le-president) confirms his tenure.

Tshisekedi’s practical authority was much stronger in 2022 than during his first two years. Initially, Joseph Kabila’s Common Front for Congo, or FCC, had won 342 of 500 National Assembly seats, dominated provincial institutions, and left Tshisekedi “in effect, unable to govern.” By April 2021, Tshisekedi had assembled the Sacred Union parliamentary coalition and installed the Lukonde government with 410 of 412 deputies present voting in favor. That shift effectively ended Kabila’s institutional tutelage and gave Tshisekedi control of the national governing agenda. [International Crisis Group, 5 May 2021](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/2050895.html), “A New Majority, but Not Yet a Cohesive One.”

That control was substantial but not unlimited:

- The prime minister and cabinet formally ran the government, while parliament retained legislative, budgetary, and oversight powers.
- The Sacred Union was a large alliance of defectors and regional party leaders, not a disciplined presidential party. Cabinet and provincial appointments therefore required bargaining.
- Courts and the electoral commission were constitutional checks, but their independence and composition were widely contested.
- Provincial governments, customary authorities, churches—especially the Catholic Church—and civil society retained meaningful autonomous influence.
- Much of eastern Congo lay beyond effective state control. Over 100 armed groups, weak and sometimes abusive security forces, and intervention by Rwanda, Uganda, and Burundi sharply constrained presidential power.
- The government remained dependent on MONUSCO, international financial institutions, donors, and neighboring states for security, humanitarian assistance, and development financing.

The defining finding for 2022 is therefore a paradox: Tshisekedi had consolidated central political power, but that did not translate into reliable territorial control, civilian protection, clean administration, or broadly shared improvements in living standards.

His government could claim some tangible progress: macroeconomic stability under an IMF program; continuation of free primary education; launch of the $1.66 billion Local Development Program for 145 Territories; improved extractive-sector disclosure; accession to the East African Community; passage of Indigenous-rights and conflict-victim legislation; and energetic regional diplomacy.

The largest failures were equally consequential: escalation of the M23 war; failure of the prolonged “state of siege” to protect civilians; continuing ADF and militia atrocities; abuses by state forces; repression of some protesters, journalists, activists, and opponents; persistent mass poverty and food insecurity despite mining-led growth; weak accountability for corruption and atrocities; and limited delivery of promised structural reforms.

No numerical ruler score should be inferred from this orientation dossier.

## 2. Most important evidence

### A. Tshisekedi had consolidated national authority through the Sacred Union

**Finding and importance.** Tshisekedi was no longer the heavily constrained president of 2019–20. By 2022, his coalition controlled the government and an overwhelming parliamentary majority, making major national policy and appointment decisions substantially attributable to him and his coalition.

**Source.** “DR Congo: No Grace Period for the New Government,” International Crisis Group, 5 May 2021, reproduced by [EUAA/ECOI](https://www.ecoi.net/en/document/2050895.html).

**Supporting location.** Opening section, paragraphs 1–5: the new majority ended the period of strong Kabila influence; the Lukonde government received 410 of 412 votes; Kabila’s FCC had previously held 342 of 500 seats and dominated provincial institutions.

**Time relationship.** Inherited institutional change from 2021 that defined the balance of power in 2022.

**Attribution.** Strong. Creating the Sacred Union, replacing the Kabila-aligned government, and choosing Sama Lukonde were presidential political projects.

**Complicating evidence.** Crisis Group emphasized that the alliance was heterogeneous, contained Kabila defectors, and remained vulnerable to disputes over offices and resources. Formally, the prime minister, parliament, courts, and provincial authorities retained independent powers.

**Cautions.** This is an expert analytical report, not an official seat register. Its principal evidence concerns the 2021 realignment, though that alignment remained operative in 2022.

### B. Security deteriorated sharply with the M23 resurgence and regional confrontation

**Finding and importance.** The M23 offensive, including the capture of Bunagana on 12 June, was the year’s central security event. It exposed the weakness of the Congolese military, displaced civilians, escalated confrontation with Rwanda, and undermined Tshisekedi’s central promise to restore security in the east.

**Source.** “Implementation of the Peace, Security and Cooperation Framework for the Democratic Republic of the Congo and the Region,” UN Secretary-General, 4 October 2022, [S/2022/735](https://documents.un.org/doc/undoc/gen/n22/612/52/pdf/n2261252.pdf).

**Supporting location.**

- Page 1, paragraphs 2–5: M23’s capture of Bunagana and the competing DRC–Rwanda allegations.
- Pages 3–5, paragraphs 14–23: Nairobi and Luanda diplomatic processes, political and military tracks, and regional-force planning.
- Page 7, paragraphs 35–37: disarmament appointments, promised FARDC reform, and the 2022–25 military programming law.
- Page 8, paragraph 41: DRC’s EAC accession.

**Time relationship.** Directly concerns March–September 2022.

**Attribution.** Mixed. M23 and foreign supporters bear direct responsibility for their offensive and abuses. Tshisekedi was directly responsible for the government’s strategic response: accusations against Rwanda, diplomacy, regional troop invitations, continued military operations, and military spending plans. He cannot reasonably be assigned sole responsibility for the underlying conflict or Rwanda’s actions.

**Complicating evidence.**

- The government did not rely only on force: it participated in the Nairobi political process, Angola’s Luanda mediation, and regional verification mechanisms.
- Fifty-six Congolese armed groups and representatives of 40 communities participated in consultations.
- Rwanda denied supporting M23 and accused FARDC of collaborating with the FDLR. Those claims require separate investigation rather than automatic acceptance.

**Cautions.** Secretary-General reporting frequently presents competing government claims before expert investigations have resolved them. The report’s period ends on 15 September and therefore does not cover the full late-2022 M23 advance.

**Important reconciliation issue.** The supplied UCDP orientation facts report zero “internationalized conflict events” and fatalities. That should not be read as evidence that no international involvement occurred. It is likely a consequence of a particular conflict/event definition, threshold, or coding state. UN reporting documents cross-border incidents, alleged RDF involvement, and invited foreign deployments; the discrepancy must be resolved at the dataset-definition level.

### C. Independent investigations supported the allegation of Rwandan assistance to M23

**Finding and importance.** The evidentiary basis for treating M23 as more than a purely domestic rebellion strengthened in 2022. UN experts assembled interviews, documents, photographs, aerial footage, and other evidence concerning M23’s rearmament and regional support.

**Source.** “Final report of the Group of Experts on the Democratic Republic of the Congo,” UN Security Council, 14 June 2022, [S/2022/479](https://documents.un.org/doc/undoc/gen/n22/338/71/pdf/n2233871.pdf), especially annex 1 and the section “Resurgence of the Mouvement du 23 mars and regional dynamics.”

**Supporting location.** Annex 1 describes more than 59 interviews with M23 members, FARDC officers, local authorities, civil society, foreign officials, and MONUSCO sources, together with documentary, photographic, audio, video, and aerial evidence.

A useful accessible synthesis is [Human Rights Watch’s World Report 2023](https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2023/country-chapters/democratic-republic-congo), “Attacks on Civilians by Armed Groups and Government Forces,” which reports that later UN findings found strong evidence of direct Rwandan support; Rwanda denied the allegation.

**Time relationship.** Direct 2022 evidence, supplemented by reports finalized later in 2022.

**Attribution.** Strong for M23 responsibility for its own operations; moderate-to-strong for Rwandan support depending on the specific episode. Weak as evidence of personal Tshisekedi culpability. Stronger as evidence against judging his security performance without accounting for foreign intervention.

**Complicating evidence.** Rwanda alleged FARDC–FDLR collaboration and cross-border attacks. Congolese forces also worked with or tolerated abusive local militias, an important subject for deeper investigation.

**Cautions.** Some sources and witnesses operated in conflict zones and could face coercion or possess partisan motives. Confidentiality prevents public verification of every interview. Later Group of Experts reporting should be used to trace individual claims and dates.

### D. The state of siege failed its principal security test and damaged civil liberties

**Finding and importance.** Military rule in North Kivu and Ituri, imposed by Tshisekedi in May 2021 and repeatedly extended through 2022, did not stop mass violence. Military authorities also used emergency powers against peaceful political activity.

**Source.** “World Report 2023: Democratic Republic of Congo—Events of 2022,” Human Rights Watch, January 2023, [country chapter](https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2023/country-chapters/democratic-republic-congo).

**Supporting location.**

- Introductory “Keynote”: military rule failed to curb atrocities; armed groups and government forces killed more than 2,000 people in Ituri and North Kivu from January to late October.
- “Freedom of Expression, Peaceful Assembly, and Media”: military authorities used emergency rule to suppress demonstrations and detain activists, journalists, and opposition members.
- “Attacks on Civilians”: at least 2,446 civilians were killed by armed actors across Ituri, North Kivu, and South Kivu through late October, including at least 155 attributed to Congolese security forces.

**Time relationship.** Directly covers 2022; the emergency itself was inherited from 2021.

**Attribution.** Strong for the decision to continue the state of siege: it originated with Tshisekedi and required repeated political continuation. Mixed for individual abuses, which were carried out by military and police personnel and may not have been personally ordered by him. As commander-in-chief and head of the governing coalition, he nevertheless bore high-level responsibility for oversight and remedial action.

**Complicating evidence.**

- The UN recorded a fall in total documented violations nationally: 5,969 violations and abuses in 2022, about 15 percent fewer than in 2021. See the [UN Joint Human Rights Office annual trends notice](https://monusco.unmissions.org/fr/node/11445), 11 February 2023.
- Congolese courts did convict some soldiers, police officers, prison personnel, and armed-group members. Earlier MONUSCO reporting identified 79 such convictions from December 2021 to March 2022.
- Violence would probably have remained severe even without emergency rule because armed groups and foreign-backed forces operated outside effective state control.

**Cautions.** HRW figures partly rely on the Kivu Security Tracker and may undercount inaccessible areas. “Failure” should mean failure to achieve civilian protection, not proof that violence would have been lower under an alternative policy.

### E. Political and civic space narrowed as the 2023 election approached

**Finding and importance.** Although the DRC retained opposition parties, plural media, protest activity, and eventual elections, authorities used force, detention, and speech offences against critics in 2022.

**Source.** Human Rights Watch, [World Report 2023](https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2023/country-chapters/democratic-republic-congo), “Freedom of Expression, Peaceful Assembly, and Media.”

**Supporting location.**

- January: security forces killed LUCHA member Mumbere Ushindi during a protest against military rule.
- April: police used excessive force against an opposition sit-in at parliament concerning the electoral law, injuring at least 20.
- August: 13 LUCHA activists were released after nine months’ detention.
- September: security forces injured participants while dispersing a peaceful medical-union demonstration in Kinshasa.

The arrest of former presidential-party leader Jean-Marc Kabund after he moved into opposition is documented in “Kabund arrêté pour outrage au chef de l’État…,” Actualité.cd, 9 August 2022, [direct report](https://actualite.cd/2022/08/09/rdc-kabund-arrete-pour-outrage-au-chef-de-letat-imputations-dommageables-et-diffamation).

**Time relationship.** Direct 2022 events.

**Attribution.** Moderate. Police, prosecutors, and military authorities carried out the acts, but continued use of “insulting the head of state” charges and repression by security institutions are matters within the president and government’s supervisory authority. Direct personal orders have not been established.

**Complicating evidence.**

- Parliament adopted a revised electoral law in June 2022, and the CENI published a calendar in November scheduling presidential and legislative elections for 20 December 2023.
- The constitutional deadline was ultimately met.
- Later Carter Center reporting found some professional improvements at CENI and a competitive field with more than 100,000 candidates, even while concluding that the election fell short on several standards.

**Cautions.** Opposition outlets may characterize ordinary prosecutions as political; conversely, formally legal charges can still be selectively or abusively applied. Case files and court rulings should be checked individually.

### F. Electoral preparation produced both a constitutional timetable and serious credibility risks

**Finding and importance.** Tshisekedi’s government and CENI proceeded toward an on-time 2023 election, but the process began with contested appointments, low public confidence, a compressed calendar, and opposition concerns about institutional independence.

**Sources.**

- “Final Report: General Elections in the Democratic Republic of the Congo,” Carter Center, 24 July 2024, [PDF](https://www.cartercenter.org/resources/pdfs/news/peace_publications/election_reports/drc/drc-final-report-2023.pdf).
- “Democratic Republic of the Congo: Freedom in the World 2022,” Freedom House, 2022, [country report](https://freedomhouse.org/country/democratic-republic-congo/freedom-world/2022).

**Supporting location.**

- Carter Center, pages 3–5: distrust arising from the 2018 election, Constitutional Court appointments, opposition underrepresentation on CENI, and controversy over its president.
- Pages 10–11: Carter Center began monitoring election developments in February 2022.
- Executive summary: the government met the constitutional deadline, but the subsequent process suffered participation, transparency, tabulation, and administrative-resource problems.
- Freedom House, “Political Rights”: parliament confirmed Denis Kadima as CENI president despite controversy over political interference.

**Time relationship.** The institutional preparations and monitoring began in 2022; much of the strongest testing evidence is a 2024 retrospective about the 2023 election.

**Attribution.** Moderate-to-strong for the political environment and governing coalition’s role in appointments and funding. Weaker for technical failures attributable to CENI as a formally independent institution.

**Complicating evidence.** The Carter Center recognized the enormous security, infrastructure, and logistical constraints, some CENI professional improvements, inclusive candidate registration, municipal-election pilots, and extensive domestic observation.

**Cautions.** Later election deficiencies should not automatically be backdated to 2022. Use the retrospective only to identify whether risks visible in 2022 materialized.

### G. The economy grew and macroeconomic management improved, but households saw little broad benefit

**Finding and importance.** Mining-led growth and compliance with an IMF program were significant administrative achievements. They did not substantially solve mass poverty, inadequate services, food insecurity, or regional inequality.

**Sources.**

- “Democratic Republic of Congo: Third Review Under the Extended Credit Facility,” IMF, 23 December 2022, [Country Report 22/390](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/cr/issues/2022/12/23/democratic-republic-of-the-congo-third-review-under-the-extended-credit-facility-527422).
- “Democratic Republic of Congo Economic Update: Harnessing Growth Through Digital Connectivity,” World Bank, 7 October 2022, [press release and report link](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2022/10/07/democratic-republic-of-congo-economic-update).

**Supporting location.**

- IMF summary: inflation exceeded 12 percent amid food and energy shocks; eastern conflict threatened recent economic progress.
- IMF authorities’ statement, “Program Performance”: all end-June quantitative performance criteria and five of six structural benchmarks were met, but health spending fell below target because of procurement delays.
- World Bank, paragraphs 139–142: recovery was driven principally by mining and telecommunications; poverty reduction remained elusive; growth benefits were not widely shared; the digital divide was severe.

**Time relationship.** Directly assesses 2022, with some 2021 growth statistics providing the starting point.

**Attribution.** Moderate. Fiscal and monetary discipline, IMF benchmarks, procurement systems, and reform legislation fall within government authority. Commodity prices, the Ukraine shock, mining investment, and inherited structural poverty are not primarily presidential choices.

**Complicating evidence.**

- Subsequent World Bank series estimate very strong real growth in 2022, around 8.9 percent.
- The government stopped routine central-bank financing, accumulated reserves, and maintained IMF engagement.
- Conversely, approximately 27 million people faced food insecurity, and the World Bank later estimated a 2022 poverty rate above 60 percent. These measures use different poverty definitions and should not be combined casually.

**Cautions.** National GDP is a poor proxy for household welfare in a mining-dependent economy. Macroeconomic figures depend on limited national statistical capacity and are frequently revised.

### H. Free primary education was a genuine flagship achievement, with serious capacity and integrity problems

**Finding and importance.** The free-primary-schooling policy, introduced in 2019 and sustained in 2022, reduced the direct fee burden and brought additional children into public school. It is one of the clearest policy continuities personally associated with Tshisekedi. However, overcrowding, hidden fees, teacher-pay disputes, payroll irregularities, and weak administration constrained quality and sustainability.

**Source.** Jean-Christophe Carret and Scherezad Latif, “Free Primary Schooling in the DRC: Progress Despite the Perils,” World Bank, 27 June 2022, [article](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/nasikiliza/free-primary-schooling-drc-progress-despite-perils).

**Supporting location.** Paragraphs 43–48: rollout began in September 2019; the policy remained operational after COVID closures; it reduced household burdens; teacher strikes and salary problems continued; a presidentially reporting finance inspectorate had found education-sector fraud; the World Bank supported an $800 million system-strengthening project.

**Time relationship.** Direct 2022 assessment of a policy inherited from 2019.

**Attribution.** Strong for political sponsorship and continuation. Shared for implementation, which involved the education ministry, provinces, schools, unions, and the World Bank.

**Complicating evidence.** Later World Bank project reporting indicates millions of additional children enrolled in selected provinces and improved regularity of some teacher payments. Those are meaningful successes, but enrollment alone does not establish learning quality.

**Cautions.** This World Bank article is supportive of a project the Bank financed and uses an illustrative family story. Independent enrollment, attendance, learning, teacher-payroll, and household-cost data are needed.

### I. The 145 Territories program was ambitious, but in 2022 remained mostly at the launch stage

**Finding and importance.** Tshisekedi’s Local Development Program for 145 Territories sought to address rural exclusion through schools, health centers, administrative buildings, roads, and local economic infrastructure. Its geographic ambition and $1.66 billion planned cost made it a central presidential program, but most 2022 evidence concerns operational launch rather than completed results.

**Source.** “Le lancement opérationnel du PDL-145 territoires désormais effectif dans les provinces,” UNDP DRC, July 2022, [article](https://www.undp.org/fr/drcongo/actualites/le-lancement-operationnel-du-pdl-145-territoires-desormais-effectif-dans-les-provinces).

**Supporting location.** Paragraphs 35–46: provincial launches, stakeholder workshops, planned implementation in 54 UNDP-supported territories, $610 million under UNDP management, total program cost of $1.66 billion, and severe road-access constraints.

**Time relationship.** Direct 2022 evidence.

**Attribution.** Strong for goal-setting and funding decisions; shared with the cabinet, implementation agencies, UNDP, governors, and local governments.

**Complicating evidence.** Entrusting implementation to three agencies, including UNDP, could reduce some procurement risks. Yet inaccessible roads, weak local administration, inflation, insecurity, and coordination problems threatened delivery.

**Cautions.** Launch ceremonies, appropriations, and planned infrastructure are inputs, not completed outputs. Claims of later completion need physical verification, contract data, expenditure records, and community interviews.

### J. Extractive transparency improved, while major governance vulnerabilities remained

**Finding and importance.** The DRC received a high EITI validation score and made progress publishing extractive information. At the same time, ownership disclosure, state-owned-enterprise oversight, contract publication, asset declarations, and anti-corruption institutional independence remained incomplete. This is strong evidence of mixed rather than uniformly failed governance.

**Sources.**

- “Democratic Republic of the Congo 2022 Validation Report,” Extractive Industries Transparency Initiative, October 2022, [report page](https://eiti.org/documents/democratic-republic-congo-2022-validation-report) and [full report](https://eiti.org/sites/default/files/2022-10/EITI%20Validation%20of%20DRC%20%282022%29%20-%20Final%20Validation%20Report_clean%20%28EN%29.pdf).
- “IMF Staff Reach Staff-Level Agreement … Third Review,” IMF, 18 November 2022, [press release](https://www.imf.org/en/news/articles/2022/11/18/pr22395-democratic-republic-congo-imf-staff-reach-staff-level-agreement-third-review-under-ecf).
- “Governance and Anti-Corruption Assessment,” IMF Country Report 21/95, published 26 May 2021, [report](https://www.imf.org/-/media/files/publications/cr/2021/english/1codea2021001.pdf).

**Supporting location.**

- EITI report page: overall score 85.5/100.
- EITI full report, page 29 and contract-transparency assessment: most recent contracts were published, but civil society raised the initially unpublished February 2022 Ventora/Dan Gertler agreement and gaps involving state enterprises.
- IMF November release, final substantive paragraphs: continuing need for anti-corruption and AML reform, simpler taxes, publication of all new and renegotiated mining contracts, and beneficial-owner disclosure.
- IMF governance assessment, paragraphs 56–58 and 79: SOE joint ventures fell outside effective portfolio-ministry oversight; financial statements and contracts were difficult to obtain; legal transparency obligations were inadequately enforced.

**Time relationship.** EITI and IMF program findings directly concern 2022; the IMF governance diagnosis describes an inherited institutional condition still relevant in 2022.

**Attribution.** Moderate-to-strong. Transparency rules, contract publication, anti-corruption agencies, and oversight of Gécamines were squarely within the government’s authority. Personal presidential enrichment is not established by these documents.

**Complicating evidence.**

- The EITI score is credible evidence of significant disclosure progress.
- The government’s renegotiated contract reportedly returned mining and oil assets to the state and was eventually published in December.
- IMF program performance was largely satisfactory.
- On the negative side, health procurement lagged, beneficial ownership remained incomplete, and an IGF audit reportedly could not trace more than $400 million in Gécamines payments said to have gone to the treasury.

**Cautions.** EITI evaluates compliance with a disclosure standard, not whether all contracts were fair or corruption-free. Government anti-corruption bodies may be selective or politically influenced. The underlying IGF Gécamines audit should be obtained before relying on press summaries.

### K. Environmental and rights policy was internally contradictory

**Finding and importance.** Tshisekedi’s government adopted an Indigenous-rights law but also auctioned 27 oil and three gas blocks, including areas affecting forests and peatlands. The combination illustrates competing goals: fiscal development and energy sovereignty versus climate, land, and community protections.

**Source.** Human Rights Watch, [World Report 2023](https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2023/country-chapters/democratic-republic-congo), “Environment and Human Rights.”

**Supporting location.** Paragraphs 108–110: Tshisekedi signed a law protecting Indigenous Pygmy peoples in November; the government’s July auction opened an estimated 11 million hectares of rainforest to possible drilling; HRW cites research estimating enormous potential carbon emissions.

**Time relationship.** Direct 2022 decisions.

**Attribution.** Strong for national policy direction and signing legislation; shared with the hydrocarbons and environment ministries for the auction and implementation.

**Complicating evidence.** The government argued that a very poor country was entitled to develop its resources and that auctioning blocks did not itself mean drilling would occur. The Indigenous-rights law was a significant legal advance.

**Cautions.** Estimates of potential emissions represent high-end consequences if resources are developed, not emissions that occurred in 2022. Land-rights outcomes depend on subsequent implementation.

## 4. Information environment and limitations

Reliable national-level information is available, but ruler-specific attribution is difficult.

The strongest evidence comes from UN reports, international financial institutions, EITI validation, court and legal records, and investigations by established rights organizations. These sources overlap substantially on the basic picture: severe eastern insecurity, weak civilian protection, widespread poverty, continuing civic-space violations, limited accountability, and some meaningful macroeconomic and transparency reforms.

Important limitations remain:

- **Conflict-zone access:** M23, ADF, CODECO, other militias, and military operations made many areas inaccessible. Fatality and abuse totals are minimum documented figures, not full counts.
- **Witness risk:** victims, journalists, activists, local officials, and soldiers could face retaliation from armed groups or state agents. Anonymous testimony is often necessary but harder to audit.
- **Weak statistics:** population estimates, poverty rates, displacement totals, school enrollment, security incidents, and public accounts are incomplete and revised frequently.
- **Fragmented territorial control:** a national indicator may say little about conditions in Ituri, North Kivu, Kinshasa, or an isolated rural territory.
- **Media ownership and poverty:** RSF reports that politicians commonly own outlets; public broadcasting lacks independence; journalists are poorly paid; “coupage,” or payment for coverage, is widespread; local officials pressure reporters; and self-censorship is common.
- **Legal intimidation:** criminal defamation and “insulting the head of state” provisions created risks for critics. Emergency military rule further constrained reporting and assembly in the east.
- **Government self-reporting:** presidency, ministry, army, EAC, and program-launch materials are valuable for confirming official decisions but cannot establish success without independent output and outcome evidence.
- **Advocacy-source selectivity:** HRW, Amnesty, Global Witness, and similar organizations specialize in abuses or corruption. Their investigations can be strong, but their mandate produces less systematic coverage of ordinary administrative successes.
- **International attention bias:** M23 and relations with Rwanda received far more coverage than local violence in western Congo, public-service delivery, provincial governance, routine policing, or less geopolitically salient armed groups.
- **Polarization and ethnic tension:** competing DRC and Rwanda narratives, hostility toward Kinyarwanda speakers, and political mobilization around the approaching election complicate witness and media accounts.
- **Retrospective contamination:** the disputed 2023 election and later escalation of the M23 war can distort assessments of what was knowable or accomplished by the end of 2022.
- **Attribution problem:** many negative outcomes were inherited or produced by armed groups, foreign states, provincial authorities, or weak institutions. Conversely, consolidated coalition control means Tshisekedi cannot be treated as a powerless figure when assessing emergency rule, security strategy, senior appointments, flagship programs, or reform follow-through.

Overall confidence is high on the identity and power structure, macro-level events, major policy decisions, and broad security/human-rights trends; moderate on program effectiveness and government corruption; and low-to-moderate on individual chains of command, exact casualty totals, and personal responsibility for many abuses.

## 5. Important questions for deeper research

1. What was the exact parliamentary composition of the Sacred Union during each part of 2022, and which coalition partners could credibly block Tshisekedi?

2. Which presidential, cabinet, military, and parliamentary decisions authorized each extension of the state of siege? What internal evaluations existed, and what alternatives were considered?

3. Did violence in North Kivu and Ituri become measurably worse or better relative to credible counterfactuals after military rule began?

4. Which FARDC officers commanded units implicated in 2022 civilian abuses, and what disciplinary or judicial action followed?

5. What evidence exists concerning FARDC cooperation with the FDLR or Congolese militias? Was it formally authorized, tolerated locally, or contrary to national policy?

6. What did the government know about Rwanda’s support for M23 at each stage, and how effectively did its diplomacy through Nairobi, Luanda, the EAC, AU, and UN respond?

7. Why did the EAC force’s original mandate and practical posture diverge? Who controlled its operations, financing, and rules of engagement?

8. Obtain the complete UN Group of Experts interim and final reports covering late 2022, including annexes on RDF deployments, M23 command, mineral networks, and FARDC-associated armed groups.

9. Obtain disaggregated UNJHRO 2022 data by perpetrator, province, violation type, victim, and month. Reconcile those data with Kivu Security Tracker, ACLED, and UCDP definitions.

10. What explains the apparent UCDP “zero internationalized conflict events” result despite extensive evidence of international involvement?

11. Which protesters, journalists, activists, and opposition politicians were detained in 2022; under which statutes; by which authority; for how long; and with what judicial outcome?

12. Did Tshisekedi or his cabinet intervene to investigate or sanction police and military personnel involved in the incidents documented by HRW and UNJHRO?

13. How were CENI members and Constitutional Court judges chosen, and what financial, legal, or administrative leverage did the presidency exercise over them?

14. Which parts of the revised June 2022 electoral law reflected opposition or civil-society demands, and which disputes remained unresolved?

15. For free primary education, obtain school-level enrollment, attendance, completion, learning, class-size, teacher-payroll, and household-expenditure data comparing 2018–19 with 2021–22 and 2022–23.

16. Obtain the education-sector IGF fraud findings and trace recoveries, prosecutions, payroll corrections, and disciplinary actions.

17. For PDL-145T, compare 2022 appropriations and disbursements with contracts, physical works, completion dates, costs, geolocated evidence, and beneficiary interviews.

18. What share of 2022 economic growth accrued to foreign mining firms, the state, Congolese employees, and households? Did real household consumption or poverty improve?

19. Why did health expenditures fall below IMF program targets? Which procurements were delayed, and what service consequences followed?

20. Obtain the underlying IGF Gécamines audit and reconcile the alleged $591 million in advances and loans with treasury receipts and accounting treatment.

21. Evaluate the February 2022 Ventora/Dan Gertler settlement asset by asset: valuation, royalties retained, sanctions implications, publication timing, legal authority, and eventual state receipts.

22. Were anti-corruption investigations distributed impartially across Sacred Union allies, former Kabila officials, opposition figures, and state enterprises?

23. Did senior officials, the presidential family, or politically exposed associates benefit from state contracts or mining rights? This requires beneficial-ownership records, procurement data, asset declarations, and corroborated investigations—not inference from association.

24. What happened after passage of the Indigenous Peoples law and conflict-victim reparations law? Were regulations, budgets, administrative bodies, and payments created?

25. Which oil and gas blocks overlapped peatlands, protected areas, or community lands, and what consultations and environmental assessments preceded the auction?
