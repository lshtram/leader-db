# Artifact X

## Orientation

Andrés Manuel López Obrador was president throughout 2022, with direct authority over the federal budget proposal, cabinet appointments, energy policy, state enterprises, social programs, and flagship infrastructure. Congress, the judiciary, autonomous regulators, Banco de México, state governments, and Mexico’s treaty obligations constrained him. Morena and its allies had legislative majorities but lacked the two-thirds Chamber majority needed for constitutional amendments.

The inherited economy combined strong macroeconomic institutions, low public debt by regional standards, deep North American manufacturing integration, and chronically weak productivity, investment, tax collection, social protection, and regional equality. Mexico entered 2022 still recovering from the unusually deep 2020 pandemic contraction.

The principal 2022 shocks were global food and energy inflation following Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, supply-chain disruption, higher global interest rates, slowing prospective U.S. growth, and continuing pandemic effects. Banco de México was operationally independent and carried primary responsibility for monetary policy.

Official economic statistics remained available from INEGI, Banco de México, the Finance Ministry, ASF, and CONEVAL. Independent evaluation therefore remained possible, although disputes over audit findings, weak procurement transparency, and executive criticism of autonomous bodies complicated accountability.

## Lens dispositions

### 5B.1 — Broad and sustainable prosperity

Developed, mixed record. The government materially expanded social protection and the minimum wage, supporting low-income households and domestic demand (WEB-5B-003, 004). These actions are consistent with broad distributional aims, and later poverty data show substantial improvement (WEB-5B-005). Conversely, investment was weakened by regulatory uncertainty, spending remained insufficient in health and education, and large resources flowed to hydrocarbons and selected presidential infrastructure projects with questionable cost discipline (WEB-5B-008, 010–012, 015).

### 5B.2 — Qualified economic leadership

Partially developed. Finance Minister Rogelio Ramírez de la O was an experienced economist and remained in office throughout 2022 (WEB-5B-006). Victoria Rodríguez Ceja became Banco de México governor after Senate approval; under the collective board, the bank continued aggressive rate increases (WEB-5B-007). Available evidence establishes professional experience and operational continuity but does not fully resolve how much independent influence either official exercised over presidential fiscal, energy, or project priorities.

### 5B.3 — Macroeconomic stability

Developed. Mexico retained market access, comfortable reserves, a well-capitalized banking system, public debt near 56% of GDP, and a broadly neutral fiscal stance. Banco de México tightened monetary policy as inflation rose (WEB-5B-001, 007). Risks included incomplete fiscal rules, low revenue, growing interest costs, and substantial PEMEX exposure (WEB-5B-003, 010). The evidence supports stability in 2022 while identifying medium-term fiscal and growth vulnerabilities.

### 5B.4 — Fair economic rules

Developed, substantially adverse with institutional constraints. OECD analysis linked weak investment to reform reversals and uncertainty, especially in electricity and oil (WEB-5B-008, 015). The U.S. initiated USMCA consultations over measures allegedly favoring Pemex and CFE and delaying or revoking private permits (WEB-5B-009). AMLO’s constitutional electricity amendment did not obtain the required supermajority, demonstrating that Congress constrained the proposed change.

### 5B.5 — Resistance to economic capture

Developed, adverse indicators but incomplete proof of personal enrichment. Energy rules favored state enterprises over private competitors (WEB-5B-009, 010), and procurement-risk analysis found deterioration between 2021 and 2022 (WEB-5B-013). ASF nevertheless continued auditing the flagship projects (WEB-5B-014). These records demonstrate elevated favoritism and accountability risks, but they do not by themselves prove that AMLO personally received private financial benefits.

### 5B.6 — Productive public investment

Developed, mixed-to-adverse execution evidence. Public investment increased, with an explicit regional-development emphasis, but Mexico still had large health, education, and infrastructure gaps (WEB-5B-003, 015). Dos Bocas and the Maya Train experienced major cost escalation during 2022 (WEB-5B-011, 012). Their ultimate economic returns were not yet observable within the target year, so cost overruns and opportunity costs are stronger evidence than later operating performance.

### 5B.7 — Evidence and correction

Developed, mixed. Mexico continued publishing statistics, poverty evaluations, fiscal reports, and independent federal audits (WEB-5B-001, 005, 014). The government supplemented PACIC as inflation persisted (WEB-5B-016), showing policy adjustment. Against that, the flagship-project record shows escalation after optimistic cost commitments, and AMLO publicly defended projects and minimized audit concerns rather than acknowledging systemic management failure (WEB-5B-011–014). Evidence of correction is stronger for inflation policy than for infrastructure governance.

### 5B.8 — Fair distribution

Developed. The 22% minimum-wage increase and higher social-protection expenditure favored workers and transfer recipients (WEB-5B-003–005). Multidimensional poverty declined by 2022, but access to health services deteriorated and regional, gender, and informal-sector inequalities remained pronounced. Fuel subsidies protected purchasing power but were poorly targeted and disproportionately benefited households consuming more fuel (WEB-5B-002).

### 5B.9 — Managing economic shocks

Developed. The government used fuel subsidies and PACIC agreements to limit energy and food-price transmission, while Banco de México raised rates (WEB-5B-001, 002, 007, 016). IMF analysis estimated that fuel stabilization reduced peak inflation by nearly two percentage points, but at high fiscal cost and with regressive incidence. The response was timely and funded but insufficiently targeted.

### 5B.10 — Lasting economic trajectory

Developed with attribution caution. Recovery accelerated during 2022 and poverty and labor-market conditions improved, but inflation was high and GDP had only recently recovered its pandemic loss (WEB-5B-001, 005). Long-standing low productivity, informality, regional inequality, weak investment, and policy uncertainty persisted (WEB-5B-008, 015). The 2022 endpoint was therefore stronger than the pandemic trough but did not establish a clearly durable high-growth trajectory.

# Artifact Y

## 1. Orientation

Andrés Manuel López Obrador controlled Mexico’s federal executive, proposed the federal budget, appointed senior economic officials subject to applicable confirmation rules, directed executive economic and energy policy, and exercised substantial influence over state enterprises such as Pemex and CFE. Congress, Banco de México’s statutory autonomy, courts, autonomous regulators, state governments, and Mexico’s USMCA commitments constrained that authority.

The 2022 baseline was unusually difficult:

- Mexico was recovering from its severe 2020 pandemic contraction, but recovery had lagged many peers.
- Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, energy and food-price shocks, supply-chain disruption, tighter global financial conditions, and weakening projected US growth complicated domestic policy.
- Mexico also entered 2022 with longstanding low productivity, weak investment, informality, inequality, limited fiscal capacity, and low tax revenue.
- Banco de México—not the president—held primary operational authority over monetary policy.
- Oil-price gains gave the public sector additional revenue while simultaneously raising the cost of the administration’s fuel-price stabilization policy.

Information remained sufficient for a substantive assessment: INEGI, Banco de México, SHCP, CONEVAL, ASF, procurement records, international institutions, civil-society analysis, and treaty partners supplied partially independent scrutiny. However, several official claims about program effectiveness are not backed here by program-level causal evaluations, and procurement opacity limited independent inspection.

The overall evidence is mixed. Mexico preserved macro-financial stability, employment recovered, the minimum wage rose sharply, and poverty subsequently measured lower in 2022. Conversely, inflation remained high, fuel support was expensive and regressive, productive public spending remained insufficient, energy intervention damaged regulatory predictability, procurement risks persisted, and flagship infrastructure exposed planning and oversight concerns. These are research findings, not a chapter score.

## 3. Lens dispositions

- **5B.1 — Broad and sustainable prosperity:** Substantively covered. The administration combined wage and social-policy priorities with conservative aggregate fiscal management, but directed considerable resources toward fuel support, Pemex, and favored infrastructure while productive investment, health, education, and fiscal capacity remained weak. See WEB-5B-001, 002, 005, 006, 010.

- **5B.2 — Qualified economic leadership:** Partially covered. AMLO appointed Victoria Rodríguez Ceja as Banco de México governor, and the Bank continued forceful tightening. Her appointment was legally confirmed and the institution retained operational autonomy in 2022. This dossier does not establish a systematic performance-versus-loyalty pattern across the full economic cabinet. See WEB-5B-001 and 008.

- **5B.3 — Macroeconomic stability:** Well covered. Mexico retained broadly neutral fiscal policy, manageable public debt, adequate reserves, and a well-capitalized banking system. Banco de México raised rates as inflation accelerated. Fiscal rules nevertheless afforded limited countercyclical capacity, and Pemex remained a contingent risk. See WEB-5B-001, 002, 005, 006.

- **5B.4 — Fair economic rules:** Well covered for energy, only partly for the wider economy. The United States initiated USMCA consultations over policies allegedly favoring Pemex and CFE and burdening private and renewable-energy firms. OECD and IMF analysis similarly described weakened predictability and investment confidence. See WEB-5B-005 and 007.

- **5B.5 — Resistance to economic capture:** Substantively covered but personal attribution is limited. Procurement analysis found restricted competition, missing documentation, and extensive direct awards; Mexico’s corruption-perception score remained unchanged and poor. These facts show institutional risk, not proof that AMLO personally profited or directed any particular corrupt award. See WEB-5B-009 and 011.

- **5B.6 — Productive public investment:** Partially covered. OECD evidence identifies insufficient investment in infrastructure and human capital, while ASF’s 2022 audit program covered numerous Tren Maya and Dos Bocas contracts. A defensible final assessment should inspect individual ASF audit sheets before relying on precise questioned amounts. See WEB-5B-005, 006, 010.

- **5B.7 — Evidence and correction:** Partially covered. Independent statistics, IMF consultation, audit, and procurement monitoring remained available. The administration reacted to inflation with PACIC and fuel subsidies, but the support remained broadly untargeted despite its high fiscal cost. Evidence here does not establish a comprehensive pattern of correcting failed flagship projects. See WEB-5B-001, 002, 009, 010.

- **5B.8 — Fair distribution:** Well covered. The 22% minimum-wage increase directly favored low-wage formal workers, and poverty fell by 2022. Countervailing evidence includes high inflation, unequal regional deprivation, worsening health-service deprivation, informality, and the disproportionate benefit of fuel subsidies to higher-income households. See WEB-5B-002, 003, 004, 012.

- **5B.9 — Managing economic shocks:** Well covered. The government suppressed part of the energy-price shock, while Banco de México tightened monetary policy. The response reduced measured inflation but incurred an estimated fiscal cost of about 1.4% of GDP and was poorly targeted. See WEB-5B-001 and 002.

- **5B.10 — Lasting economic trajectory:** Covered cautiously. Employment, GDP, and poverty improved during the rebound, and macro-financial buffers survived. Yet 2022 did not resolve Mexico’s weak productivity, investment, fiscal-capacity, energy-governance, or inequality problems. A single rebound year cannot establish durability. See WEB-5B-001, 004, 005, 006, 007, 012.

## 4. Source handling

### Independent corroboration

- IMF findings draw on official Mexican statistics but add mission analysis, cross-country expertise, and independent policy assessment.
- OECD independently corroborates weak investment, limited fiscal space, Pemex risk, and regulatory uncertainty.
- World Bank poverty material corroborates CONEVAL’s broad poverty direction while stressing persistent inequality and deprivation.
- USTR records independently establish the existence and stated legal basis of the USMCA consultations; they do not by themselves prove Mexico violated the treaty.
- IMCO procurement analysis is based largely on government contracting data but applies an independent risk methodology.
- Transparency International provides a separate perception-based institutional measure; it does not measure proven corruption cases.

### Reused sources

- IMF 2022 Article IV: reused across macro stability, shock management, distribution, personnel/institutional autonomy, and trajectory.
- OECD Economic Survey: reused across fiscal capacity, Pemex, investment, regulation, social spending, and durability.
- CONEVAL/World Bank poverty evidence: reused across distribution, outcomes, and trajectory.
- USTR consultation record: reused across fair rules, investment, and long-term trajectory.
- IMCO procurement work: reused across capture, evidence transparency, and implementation.

### Uninspected leads

- Individual ASF audit files for every 2022 Tren Maya and Dos Bocas contract.
- SHCP’s complete 2022 Cuenta Pública and program-level budget execution tables.
- Banco de México’s complete 2022 annual report and each monetary-policy decision.
- CONEVAL evaluations of individual AMLO social programs.
- COFECE enforcement decisions and competition-policy outcomes during 2022.
- Distributional microdata for the minimum-wage increase by sex, region, ethnicity, and informality.
- Precise 2022 public-investment execution for health, education, science, water, and transport.
- The earlier nuclear-related resources are irrelevant to chapter 5B and were not inspected.

### Rejected sources

- Reddit mirrors of news reports: rejected in favor of underlying institutional or publisher sources.
- Wikipedia biographies and summaries: rejected where Banco de México’s official appointment record was available.
- Later political assertions about cumulative flagship-project costs: rejected without inspection of their underlying accounting.
- Government assertions that PACIC resources “could not have a better use”: treated as rhetoric, not accepted evidence of effectiveness.

### Blocked or access-limited

- Some ASF result pages expose an audit index but not sufficiently extractable audit-level findings through the available interface.
- A precise contemporaneous Banco de México annual-report locator for every 2022 rate decision was not reliably extractable; IMF’s reviewed account was retained instead.

## 6. Remaining questions

Additional research is most likely to add material information in four areas:

1. Project-level ASF findings and remedial action for Tren Maya and Dos Bocas.
2. Distributional incidence and employment effects of the 2022 minimum-wage increase.
3. The qualifications, practical independence, and performance-management history of the broader economic-policy team.
4. Program-level effectiveness of social transfers, health, education, and regional investment.

Further general searches about 2022 GDP, headline inflation, debt, or the existence of the USMCA energy dispute are unlikely to add much. Targeted audit, budget-execution, and household-microdata research would.
