**New Evidence**
Local priors already show a strong baseline on health and schooling, so the key 2022 question is whether Erdogan’s policy choices and program delivery improved ordinary people’s life chances or mainly prevented a worse decline. The record is mixed: there were real welfare supports, especially for refugees and low-income households, but the macro policy mix sharply eroded purchasing power.

The clearest negative evidence is the low-rate macro regime and its household effects.

**Search and Rejection Summary**
I screened roughly 30 documents across the World Bank, OECD, IMF, WHO, UNICEF, Reuters wire mirrors, and Turkish government-linked reporting.

I kept the sources that best separated policy choice, implementation, and outcome:
- macro-stability and purchasing-power erosion,
- household and vulnerable-group mitigation,
- refugee health and education service delivery,
- and the appointment style of the economic team.

I rejected:
- duplicate Reuters mirrors of the same rate-cut and Nebati stories,
- broad global UNICEF/WHO pages that were not Turkey-specific enough,
- 2023/2024 Turkey economy pieces that would blur the 2022 target period,
- and structured indicator pages already covered by the local prior.

I also left out general WDI/UNDP/WHO datasets because the local prior already supplied the core baseline facts for 2022.

**Remaining Gaps**
- Direct Turkish budget, audit, or ministry-accountability evidence would sharpen how much of the social-service delivery was Erdogan-specific versus inherited or donor-driven.
- More household-level distribution data would help distinguish average gains from conditions in poorer regions and among women, refugees, and out-of-school children.
- I did not find strong evidence that welfare tools were systematically used for political loyalty or punishment in 2022, so that lens remains weaker than the health and refugee-service lenses.
