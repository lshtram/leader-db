## 1. Overview

Vladimir Vladimirovich Putin was President of the Russian Federation throughout 2022. Constitutionally, the president appoints senior officials, directs foreign policy, serves as commander-in-chief, and can issue decrees. In practice, Putin’s authority was substantially greater: United Russia controlled parliament, the “systemic opposition” generally supported the Kremlin, courts rarely constrained the executive in political cases, and the 2020 constitutional amendments had expanded presidential power and potentially enabled Putin to remain president until 2036. [BTI’s institutional assessment](https://bti-project.org/fileadmin/api/content/en/downloads/reports/country_report_2022_RUS.pdf) describes parliament and the judiciary as subordinate in politically important matters.

Important practical limits nevertheless remained. Putin depended on the armed forces, security services, central and regional bureaucracies, state firms, and technocratic bodies such as the Bank of Russia to implement policy. Their capabilities were uneven. Ukrainian resistance, Western military support and sanctions, the need to mobilize reservists, Russia’s remaining economic dependence on energy exports and imports, and the growing asymmetry of its relationship with China sharply limited what presidential authority could accomplish abroad. Regional officials had implementation discretion but not strategic autonomy: Putin’s mobilization decree, for example, ordered governors to supply conscripts in numbers and on schedules set by the Defense Ministry.

The defining decision was Putin’s initiation of the full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February. By year-end Russia had failed to capture Kyiv or subjugate Ukraine, but had occupied substantial southern and eastern territory, captured Mariupol, mobilized 300,000 reservists, and purported to annex four Ukrainian regions. The war caused mass civilian harm, exposed serious military weaknesses, heightened nuclear risks, produced extensive sanctions, and triggered a much harsher domestic censorship and repression regime. Russia’s economy proved considerably more resilient in 2022 than early forecasts predicted, although output contracted and transparency deteriorated.

## 2. Evidence records

### P22-01 — Putin’s dominant but institution-dependent authority

- **Fact and significance:** Putin was both the formal president and the effective center of strategic decision-making. Parliament and courts provided little effective political constraint, making major 2022 choices—especially invasion, mobilization, nuclear signaling, and annexation—strongly attributable to him.
- **Strongest source:** *BTI 2022 Country Report: Russia*, Bertelsmann Stiftung, 2022, [direct PDF](https://bti-project.org/fileadmin/api/content/en/downloads/reports/country_report_2022_RUS.pdf).
- **Locator:** PDF pp. 11–12, especially extracted lines 353–382: the presidency controls parliament; systemic opposition parties support the official line; political courts are influenced by the executive; the 2020 amendments expanded presidential power.
- **Temporal status:** Primarily an inherited institutional condition still operating in 2022.
- **Attribution:** **Very strong.** The structure was built and maintained under Putin, although implementation depended on officials, security agencies, judges, governors, and oligarchic networks.
- **Complicating evidence:** Courts retained more autonomy in ordinary commercial disputes; the bureaucracy and armed forces could obstruct policy through limited capacity, poor information, or implementation failures.
- **Cautions:** BTI is an expert-coded external assessment and its reporting period largely precedes the invasion.
- **Independent support:** [Freedom House, Nations in Transit 2022](https://freedomhouse.org/country/russia/nations-transit/2022), especially its description of increasingly personalist rule.

### P22-02 — Putin initiated a full-scale war against Ukraine

- **Fact and significance:** Russian forces began large-scale military operations throughout Ukraine on 24 February 2022 following Putin’s televised announcement. The UN General Assembly characterized this as aggression and demanded withdrawal. This was the central strategic decision of the period.
- **Strongest source:** *A/RES/ES-11/1, Aggression against Ukraine*, UN General Assembly, 2 March 2022, [direct resolution](https://documents.un.org/access.nsf/get?DS=A%2FRES%2FES-11%2F1&Lang=E&OpenAgent=).
- **Locator:** PDF pp. 1–3, especially lines 62–71 and operative paragraphs 1–4; the resolution describes Russian operations inside Ukraine, identifies the offensive as contrary to the Charter, and condemns the raised readiness of Russian nuclear forces.
- **Temporal status:** Direct 2022 evidence.
- **Attribution:** **Very strong.** Putin publicly announced the operation and was commander-in-chief; the General Assembly addressed Russia as the responsible state.
- **Complicating evidence:** The Kremlin claimed collective self-defence of Donetsk and Luhansk, protection of Donbas, “demilitarization,” and “denazification.” Those claims did not persuade the General Assembly majority. Russia also argued that NATO expansion created an acute security threat.
- **Cautions:** General Assembly resolutions are authoritative evidence of the international response and legal position of the voting majority, but they are political votes rather than judicial judgments.
- **Independent support:** [Kremlin transcript index for 24 February 2022](https://en.kremlin.ru/events/president/transcripts/by-date/21.02.2022); [RUSI operational study](https://www.rusi.org/explore-our-research/publications/special-resources/preliminary-lessons-conventional-warfighting-russias-invasion-ukraine-february-july-2022).

### P22-03 — The initial plan to seize Kyiv and rapidly subjugate Ukraine failed

- **Fact and significance:** Russia planned a rapid campaign centered on seizing Kyiv, but the operation lacked viable fallback plans and failed. This is strong evidence of a major intelligence, planning, and implementation failure against Putin’s apparent strategic program.
- **Strongest source:** Jack Watling and Nick Reynolds, *Preliminary Lessons in Conventional Warfighting from Russia’s Invasion of Ukraine: February–July 2022*, RUSI, 30 November 2022, [report page](https://www.rusi.org/explore-our-research/publications/special-resources/preliminary-lessons-conventional-warfighting-russias-invasion-ukraine-february-july-2022).
- **Locator:** Summary paragraphs corresponding to extracted lines 41–53: ten-day invasion plan, intended rapid seizure of Kyiv, tactical unpreparedness, lack of fallback options, inferior tactical performance, dishonest reporting culture, and a tendency to reinforce failure.
- **Temporal status:** Contemporary 2022 assessment.
- **Attribution:** **Strong at strategic level; moderate for operational defects.** Putin chose the war and headed the command system, while generals and security services shared responsibility for planning and execution.
- **Complicating evidence:** Russian deception initially succeeded and some Russian systems and experienced units performed effectively; Russia later made gains in Donbas. The Kremlin subsequently denied that storming Kyiv had been intended.
- **Cautions:** RUSI explicitly says much underlying Ukrainian operational material could not be published and should be treated as testimony rather than a reproducible academic study.
- **Independent support:** U.S. Defense Department, *Defense Official Not Ready to Call Russian Activity…*, 29 March 2022, [lines describing Kyiv’s capture as a failed objective](https://www.defense.gov/News/News-Stories/Article/Article/2982197/defense-official-not-ready-to-call-russian-activity-in-ukraine-a-retreat-or-wit/).

### P22-04 — Russia achieved limited territorial gains but not its maximal program

- **Fact and significance:** Russia captured substantial territory, including Mariupol and a southern land corridor, but failed to overthrow Ukraine’s government and later lost territory around Kyiv, Kharkiv, and Kherson city. By year-end the result was a costly partial achievement rather than the rapid subjugation apparently envisaged.
- **Strongest source:** U.S. Department of Defense, *Defense Official Not Ready to Call Russian Activity in Ukraine a Retreat or Withdrawal*, 29 March 2022, [direct page](https://www.defense.gov/News/News-Stories/Article/Article/2982197/defense-official-not-ready-to-call-russian-activity-in-ukraine-a-retreat-or-wit/).
- **Locator:** Lines 217–263: assessed intent to overthrow Ukraine’s government and occupy or annex territory; advances toward Mariupol and Kherson; failure to capture Kyiv or subjugate Ukraine.
- **Temporal status:** Direct 2022 assessment, early in the campaign.
- **Attribution:** **Strong** for overall objectives and continuation of the war; **moderate** for individual battlefield outcomes.
- **Complicating evidence:** This was a belligerent-supporting U.S. government assessment. Russia did occupy important territory and its official account narrowed the declared focus toward Donbas.
- **Cautions:** The source reflects U.S. intelligence judgments and predates later 2022 operations.
- **Independent support:** RUSI’s report records initial failure followed by more successful Russian operations in Donbas.

### P22-05 — Purported annexation of four Ukrainian regions

- **Fact and significance:** After occupation-organized “referendums,” Putin’s government purported to annex Donetsk, Luhansk, Kherson, and Zaporizhzhia. The UN General Assembly held that the referendums and status decisions violated Ukrainian sovereignty and had no legal validity.
- **Strongest source:** *A/RES/ES-11/4, Territorial integrity of Ukraine: defending the principles of the Charter*, UN General Assembly, 12 October 2022, [direct PDF](https://documents.un.org/doc/undoc/gen/n22/630/66/pdf/n2263066.pdf?OpenElement=).
- **Locator:** PDF p. 2, lines 38–52: decisions of 21 February and 29 September, illegal referendums of 23–27 September, and operative condemnation.
- **Temporal status:** Direct 2022 evidence.
- **Attribution:** **Very strong.** These were formal Russian state decisions publicly championed by Putin.
- **Complicating evidence:** Russia claimed the votes expressed self-determination and treated the regions as Russian territory. The votes occurred under military occupation, without accepted independent conditions for a free referendum.
- **Cautions:** Again a political resolution, not a court judgment, although grounded in established non-acquisition-by-force principles.
- **Independent support:** UN official meeting records and the Secretary-General’s 29 September statement cited in the resolution.

### P22-06 — Large-scale civilian harm and predominantly Russian violations

- **Fact and significance:** The UN Commission of Inquiry found indiscriminate attacks, attacks on fleeing civilians, executions, unlawful confinement, torture, sexual violence, and deportations in Russian-occupied areas. It attributed the vast majority of identified violations, including war crimes, to Russian forces.
- **Strongest source:** *UN Commission has found an array of war crimes…*, Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine/United Nations in Ukraine, 18 October 2022, [direct page](https://ukraine.un.org/en/203733-un-commission-has-found-array-war-crimes-violations-human-rights-and-international).
- **Locator:** Paragraphs/lines 62–74, particularly 66–69.
- **Temporal status:** Direct investigation of February–March 2022 events, published in 2022.
- **Attribution:** **Strong to the Russian state and military; moderate to Putin personally.** Putin initiated and sustained the campaign, but this source did not establish his knowledge of or orders for each offense.
- **Complicating evidence:** The Commission also identified Ukrainian violations, including two incidents qualifying as war crimes, and noted failures by both parties to keep military objects away from dense civilian areas.
- **Cautions:** Its first investigation covered only four northern regions and was not a complete national accounting.
- **Independent support:** OHCHR’s December casualty update; OHCHR’s thematic report on killings.

### P22-07 — At least thousands of verified civilian casualties, with a substantial undercount

- **Fact and significance:** By 26 December, OHCHR had individually verified extensive civilian deaths and injuries; most resulted from explosive weapons with wide-area effects. OHCHR warned that actual totals were considerably higher, especially in heavily contested or occupied cities such as Mariupol.
- **Strongest source:** *Ukraine: Civilian Casualty Update as of 26 December 2022*, OHCHR/UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine, 27 December 2022, [direct PDF](https://ukraine.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-12/Ukraine%20-%20civilian%20casualty%20update%20as%20of%2026%20December%202022%20ENG.pdf).
- **Locator:** PDF pp. 1–2, lines 22–30, 54–58 and 72–85; weapon patterns, delayed reports, and verification methodology.
- **Temporal status:** Direct 2022 evidence.
- **Attribution:** **Strong to the war Putin initiated; variable for individual casualties.** The update does not assign every casualty to a specific party.
- **Complicating evidence:** Casualties occurred on both government- and Russian-controlled territory, and both sides used military force.
- **Cautions:** This is a conservative verified minimum, not a final total; inaccessible areas produced systematic undercounting.
- **Independent support:** UN Commission of Inquiry findings; OHCHR thematic report on killings.

### P22-08 — Nuclear readiness signaling and danger around Zaporizhzhia

- **Fact and significance:** Putin raised the readiness posture of Russian nuclear forces early in the invasion and later coupled mobilization with threats to use all available means. Separately, fighting and Russian control around the Zaporizhzhia nuclear plant produced a persistent accident risk. These actions materially increased catastrophic-risk uncertainty.
- **Strongest source:** *A/RES/ES-11/1, Aggression against Ukraine*, UN General Assembly, 2 March 2022, [direct resolution](https://documents.un.org/access.nsf/get?DS=A%2FRES%2FES-11%2F1&Lang=E&OpenAgent=).
- **Locator:** PDF p. 2, lines 70–71, expressly condemning Russia’s decision to increase nuclear-force readiness.
- **Temporal status:** Direct 2022 evidence.
- **Attribution:** **Very strong** for nuclear signaling by Russia; **strong at campaign level but weaker incident-by-incident** for Zaporizhzhia.
- **Complicating evidence:** The Pentagon reportedly observed no clear corresponding change in nuclear force posture; some analysts interpreted the statements as coercive signaling rather than preparation for use. Responsibility for individual episodes of shelling around the plant was disputed.
- **Cautions:** The practical meaning of “special combat readiness” was opaque.
- **Independent support:** IAEA, *Update 128*, 20 November 2022, [explosions and damaged equipment at Zaporizhzhia](https://www.iaea.org/newscenter/pressreleases/update-128-iaea-director-general-statement-on-situation-in-ukraine); [Chatham House analysis](https://www.chathamhouse.org/2022/09/ambiguous-nuclear-threats-heighten-catastrophic-risks).

### P22-09 — Partial mobilization exposed military and political limits

- **Fact and significance:** On 21 September Putin ordered partial mobilization, directing governors to deliver conscripts according to Defense Ministry quotas. The call-up eventually reached the publicly stated target of 300,000, replenishing Russian forces but provoking protests, administrative disorder, and large-scale departures abroad.
- **Strongest source:** *Presidential Decree No. 647 “On the Announcement of Partial Mobilization”*, President of the Russian Federation, 21 September 2022, published by Rossiyskaya Gazeta, [official text](https://rg.ru/documents/2022/09/21/prezident-ukaz647-site-dok.html).
- **Locator:** Paragraphs 1–10, particularly lines 63–86; paragraph 7 remained classified, while paragraph 8 imposed regional quotas and schedules.
- **Temporal status:** Direct 2022 evidence.
- **Attribution:** **Very strong.** Putin signed the decree.
- **Complicating evidence:** Mobilization did supply badly needed manpower and the government provided formal pay and employment protections. Short-term public-opinion effects may have been less durable than the visible flight and protests suggested.
- **Cautions:** The published decree omitted the classified paragraph and did not itself state 300,000; that number came from Defence Minister Sergei Shoigu.
- **Independent support:** Russian government employment-protection notice; HRW’s 2023 world report; contemporaneous border-arrival data reported by Reuters.

### P22-10 — Anti-war speech, protest, and independent reporting were criminalized

- **Fact and significance:** New March laws effectively outlawed broad categories of anti-war expression. Authorities prosecuted peaceful protesters, journalists, activists, municipal officials, and people using words such as “invasion”; more than 15,000 protesters were arrested in the first month. This greatly reduced political competition, press freedom, and the reliability of observable public opinion.
- **Strongest source:** *World Report 2023: Russian Federation*, Human Rights Watch, 2023, covering 2022, [direct chapter](https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2023/country-chapters/russian-federation).
- **Locator:** “Freedom of Expression,” “Freedom of Assembly,” and anti-war-repression sections; extracted lines 100–109 and 124–133.
- **Temporal status:** Contemporary events summarized just after the period.
- **Attribution:** **Strong.** Putin signed the laws; parliament, police, prosecutors, Roskomnadzor, and courts implemented them.
- **Complicating evidence:** The government said the measures countered false military information and protected national security during conflict. Restrictions on speech can be lawful in narrow circumstances, but the documented breadth and penalties went far beyond narrowly tailored security measures.
- **Cautions:** HRW is an advocacy organization barred or constrained in Russia and depended partly on local monitors and remote documentation.
- **Independent support:** [Amnesty International’s March 2022 censorship report](https://www.amnesty.org/en/documents/eur46/5345/2022/en/); UN experts’ July statement citing more than 16,000 detentions.

### P22-11 — Political competition and institutional checks were already severely degraded

- **Fact and significance:** No presidential or State Duma election occurred in 2022, but inherited conditions shaped the period: opposition organizations had been dismantled, Navalny remained imprisoned, independent actors faced “foreign agent” and “extremist” measures, and parliament and courts offered little check on the executive.
- **Strongest source:** *Russia: Nations in Transit 2022*, Freedom House, 2022, [direct report](https://freedomhouse.org/country/russia/nations-transit/2022).
- **Locator:** Overview paragraphs 86–91: increasingly personalist rule, preventive repression of Navalny and the Anti-Corruption Foundation, selective restrictions on opposition activity, and greater reliance on security services.
- **Temporal status:** Inherited 2021 conditions directly relevant to 2022.
- **Attribution:** **Strong at regime level.** Putin’s administration shaped election access, legislation, appointments, and coercive priorities.
- **Complicating evidence:** Putin retained substantial measured popularity, and some support for the war and government was genuine rather than entirely coerced.
- **Cautions:** Freedom House has an explicit democracy-promotion mission; wartime polling in an authoritarian information environment has serious nonresponse and preference-falsification risks.
- **Independent support:** BTI 2022; HRW World Reports 2022 and 2023.

### P22-12 — Economic contraction was real but much smaller than initially expected

- **Fact and significance:** Despite sweeping sanctions and corporate withdrawal, Russia’s real GDP contracted by about 2.1–2.2% in 2022—serious, but far below some early double-digit forecasts. Capital controls, high energy earnings, fiscal buffers, import compression, and central-bank measures helped prevent an immediate financial collapse.
- **Strongest source:** *Macro Poverty Outlook for Russian Federation: April 2023*, World Bank, April 2023, [document page](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/099725104112326740).
- **Locator:** Country summary: “Russia’s economy contracted by only 2.1 percent in 2022, in the face of severe sanctions.”
- **Temporal status:** Near-term retrospective finding based on 2022 data.
- **Attribution:** **Mixed.** Putin was highly responsible for the war-induced shock; resilience also reflected the Bank of Russia, Finance Ministry, prewar reserves, energy prices, and external demand.
- **Complicating evidence:** The better-than-forecast headline did not show lost investment, technological isolation, emigration, future productivity costs, or the welfare value of military output. Conversely, claims of immediate economic collapse were plainly overstated.
- **Cautions:** Russian disclosure deteriorated after the invasion, and national accounts were subject to later revision.
- **Independent support:** Bank of Russia, *Annual Report for 2022*; AP’s February 2023 review noting the absence of an economic “knockout.”

### P22-13 — Social services continued, but health and welfare evidence was unusually weak

- **Fact and significance:** Russia maintained nationwide health, education, pension, and essential-service systems through 2022; there is no evidence of nationwide institutional collapse. Yet the period inherited severe COVID mortality and distrust, while wartime secrecy and disrupted international reporting made service quality and distribution difficult to assess. Available evidence supports continuity with significant strain, not a clean success or failure finding.
- **Strongest source:** WHO, *Russian Federation — Global Health Observatory*, continuously maintained, [country page](https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/countries/country-details/GHO/russian-federation).
- **Locator:** Country metadata and caution that Russian death-registration data have low completeness and/or moderate cause-of-death assignment problems.
- **Temporal status:** Mix of inherited health conditions and later-compiled statistics.
- **Attribution:** **Moderate at policy level; weak for individual outcomes.** Federal policy mattered, but regional governments and service institutions delivered care and education.
- **Complicating evidence:** Russia possessed broad service coverage and developed Sputnik V; the 2022 war did not erase those capacities. Measured inequality and official poverty did not show a catastrophic one-year collapse.
- **Cautions:** WHO country indicators are not a direct evaluation of Putin, and several health observations concern 2020–21 rather than 2022.
- **Independent support:** Freedom House’s discussion of pandemic mortality and vaccine distrust; World Bank macro-poverty reporting; Rosstat poverty publications.

### P22-14 — Wartime procurement and disclosure became substantially less transparent

- **Fact and significance:** In March 2022 the government allowed issuers to withhold business reports where disclosure might facilitate sanctions, while regional and municipal authorities received expanded noncompetitive procurement powers. By mid-September, TI Russia calculated that such procurements exceeded RUB 666 billion. This weakened accountability precisely as war spending expanded.
- **Strongest source:** *Annual Report 2022*, Transparency International Russia, published retrospectively, [direct PDF](https://ti-russia.org/en/annual-reports/2022-annual-report/).
- **Locator:** PDF pp. 1 and 3, especially lines 4–13 and 88–98.
- **Temporal status:** Direct 2022 measures, reported retrospectively.
- **Attribution:** **Strong to Putin’s government; moderate to Putin personally.** The measures were implemented across the executive and subnational governments.
- **Complicating evidence:** Protecting commercially sensitive information from sanctions can be a legitimate wartime objective; noncompetitive procurement can accelerate emergency acquisition.
- **Cautions:** TI Russia was under severe state pressure and later operated in exile; the report’s normative stance is explicit. The RUB 666 billion calculation should be independently reconstructed.
- **Independent support:** BTI’s prewar finding that anticorruption enforcement was inconsistent and politically selective.

### P22-15 — Putin-linked wealth was opaque and insulated from domestic accountability

- **Fact and significance:** Investigations and the U.S. Treasury described complex networks through which Russian elites and people close to Putin held or moved wealth abroad. This does not prove that every associated asset belonged beneficially to Putin, but it supports a strong finding of opaque personal networks, conflicts of interest, and an absence of credible domestic scrutiny.
- **Strongest source:** U.S. Treasury, *NDAA Russia Illicit Finance Report*, 2023, [direct PDF](https://home.treasury.gov/system/files/136/Treasury-NDAA-Ru-IFR-508.pdf).
- **Locator:** PDF p. 4, lines 85–100: public estimates of wealth linked to Putin and associates, shell-company and intermediary networks, and Treasury’s conclusion that Russian elites, including Putin, rely on complex support networks.
- **Temporal status:** Later retrospective synthesis, drawing partly on 2022 sanctions and financial analysis.
- **Attribution:** **Moderate.** The evidence strongly connects networks to Putin’s associates but does not transparently establish his beneficial ownership of every cited asset.
- **Complicating evidence:** Putin denied owning prominent alleged assets; his name often does not appear in leaked records. Sanctioning governments are geopolitical adversaries and sometimes rely on classified information that cannot be independently tested.
- **Cautions:** Public wealth estimates vary enormously and should not be converted into a precise personal-net-worth finding.
- **Independent support:** ICIJ, *How a network of enablers have helped Russia’s oligarchs hide their wealth abroad*, March 2022, [opened investigation](https://www.icij.org/investigations/russia-archive/how-a-network-of-enablers-have-helped-russias-oligarchs-hide-their-wealth-abroad/), especially its Roldugin and Kolbin sections.

### P22-16 — Russia participated in a successful limited grain agreement

- **Fact and significance:** Russia joined Türkiye, Ukraine, and the UN in the July Black Sea Grain Initiative and agreed to its November renewal. By 17 November, more than 11.1 million tonnes of food had been shipped. This was a concrete, if narrow, diplomatic success mitigating a global food-security consequence of the war.
- **Strongest source:** *Statement of the Secretary-General on the Renewal of the Black Sea Grain Initiative*, UN Office at Geneva, 17 November 2022, [direct page](https://www.unognewsroom.org/story/en/1374/statement-of-the-secretary-general-on-the-renewal-of-the-black-sea-grain-initiative).
- **Locator:** Lines 41–49 and 64–69: shipment total, parties, renewal, and unresolved obstacles to Russian food and fertilizer exports.
- **Temporal status:** Direct 2022 evidence.
- **Attribution:** **Moderate.** Russian participation required Kremlin authorization, but Türkiye, Ukraine, the UN, and technical negotiators were indispensable.
- **Complicating evidence:** The agreement addressed a crisis substantially created by Russia’s invasion and blockade; implementation was intermittently threatened, and Russia later withdrew in 2023.
- **Cautions:** Participation in a mitigating agreement should not be mistaken for resolution of the underlying war or full humanitarian cooperation.
- **Independent support:** UN Black Sea Grain Initiative FAQ and UNCTAD’s initiative summary.

### Later retrospective evidence

### P22-R1 — Russian military deaths fell disproportionately on poorer regions

- **Fact and significance:** A peer-reviewed study using more than 20,000 named Russian fatalities found much higher mortality in economically disadvantaged Siberian and Far Eastern regions and overrepresentation of Buryats and Tuvans. Much of the ethnic disparity diminished after socioeconomic conditions were controlled, suggesting regional inequality rather than ethnicity alone as the principal mechanism.
- **Strongest source:** Alexey Bessudnov, *Ethnic and regional inequalities in Russian military fatalities in Ukraine*, **Demographic Research**, 8 June 2023, [article and DOI](https://www.demographic-research.org/articles/volume/48/31/).
- **Locator:** Abstract, lines 72–81.
- **Temporal status:** Explicitly later retrospective evidence using February 2022–April 2023 data.
- **Attribution:** **Moderate.** Putin ordered and sustained the war and mobilization; recruitment structures, regional poverty, military geography, and local implementation shaped the distribution.
- **Complicating evidence:** After socioeconomic controls, ethnic differences were considerably reduced.
- **Cautions:** The named-dead dataset was crowdsourced and incomplete; the author labels findings tentative.
- **Independent support:** HRW’s account of mobilization protests and disproportionate burdens in minority regions.

## 3. Information environment and evidentiary limitations

Reliable information was difficult to obtain and became markedly harder after February 2022.

- Russia criminalized “false information” and “discrediting” the armed forces, blocked or pressured independent media, and arrested protesters and journalists. Public silence or poll responses therefore cannot safely be read as unconstrained approval.
- The state required media to rely heavily on official military information. Russian battlefield-loss reporting was sparse, while both Russia and Ukraine had strong incentives to conceal losses and exaggerate opposing casualties.
- Occupied areas and active battlefields were inaccessible. OHCHR therefore published conservative verified minima and expressly warned of much higher true civilian casualties.
- Government economic and corporate disclosure was curtailed. Official GDP, employment, poverty, and inflation statistics remain useful, but revisions, methodological changes, classified military spending, missing trade detail, and replacement of civilian with military output complicate welfare interpretation.
- Investigations of Putin’s personal wealth confront proxies, nominees, shell companies, secrecy jurisdictions, and the absence of independent domestic subpoena or judicial mechanisms. Strong network evidence is not identical to proof of Putin’s ownership.
- Russian state sources are indispensable for decrees and Putin’s stated program but unreliable as neutral descriptions of battlefield events or public sentiment. Western government sources also require caution because their states supported Ukraine.
- International attention was extensive, but uneven: Ukraine received intensive monitoring, while the effects on remote Russian regions, prisoners, minorities, conscripts, and ordinary service users were less consistently documented.

## 4. Questions for deeper research

1. What documentary evidence identifies who proposed, reviewed, and approved the February invasion plan?
2. How did Security Council members, the FSB, Defence Ministry, Rosgvardiya, and regional governors influence or constrain Putin?
3. What were Russia’s verified 2022 military deaths, injuries, desertions, equipment losses, and mobilization errors?
4. How much territory and strategic infrastructure did Russia control on 31 December compared with 24 February?
5. Which Russian commanders or civilian officials were credibly linked to specific war crimes, and what evidence reaches Putin’s knowledge or orders?
6. How did real household consumption, medicine access, education quality, pensions, and regional poverty change after inflation and mobilization?
7. Can the RUB 666 billion noncompetitive-procurement estimate be reproduced from procurement records?
8. Which Putin-associated assets have proven beneficial-ownership records, as distinct from allegation or association?
9. How much genuine war support can be separated from fear, nonresponse, propaganda exposure, and preference falsification?

## 6. Promising leads still needing full inspection

These appeared materially useful in search results but were not fully extracted and should not yet be treated as developed evidence:

- Bank of Russia, *Annual Report for 2022*—monetary response, inflation, financial stability, capital controls, and GDP composition.
- Rosstat’s revised 2022 poverty release and detailed real-income series.
- Full October 2022 report of the Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine.
- OHCHR, *Killings of Civilians: Summary Executions and Attacks on Individual Civilians*, December 2022.
- IAEA’s September 2022 mission report on Zaporizhzhia, including the seven nuclear-safety pillars.
- Russian federal budget execution and classified-spending changes for 2022.
- Levada Center microdata and survey-mode research on wartime preference falsification.
- OCCRP’s 2022 “LLCInvest” investigation connecting assets attributed to Putin through a shared digital and corporate infrastructure.
