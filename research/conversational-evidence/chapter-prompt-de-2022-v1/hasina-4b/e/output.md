## Scope and orientation

I interpret “2022–2022” as calendar year 2022. No chapter score is assigned.

Sheikh Hasina possessed strong formal and practical authority. Constitution Article 55 placed the cabinet under the prime minister and vested executive power in her or under her authority. Her Awami League’s overwhelming parliamentary majority, Article 70 party discipline, and the president’s generally prime-ministerial-advice-bound role further concentrated practical power. Security-force operations, individual prosecutions, court decisions, and local violence were not necessarily personally ordered by Hasina, but national policy, senior appointments, institutional design, and failures of discipline fall substantially within her authority.

The inherited baseline was already seriously compromised: the 2018 election returning Hasina for a third consecutive term was assessed as neither free nor fair, with ballot stuffing, intimidation, arrests, violence, and impeded observation. The 2014 and 2018 elections, weak parliamentary opposition, executive influence over courts, the Digital Security Act, and politicized oversight bodies all predated 2022. Thus 2022 principally tests whether Hasina corrected, preserved, or deepened those conditions.

There was no national election or constitutional transfer in 2022. The year instead contained institutional preparation for the next election, local elections, rising BNP mobilization, economic pressure following COVID-19 and Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, flooding, and continued responsibility for nearly one million Rohingya refugees. These pressures may explain heightened public protest and genuine public-order demands, but no formal national emergency displaced ordinary constitutional standards.

The favorable evidence is limited but real: Bangladesh continued to have opposition parties, large demonstrations, critical newspapers and NGOs; the government finally enacted a statutory process for appointing the Election Commission; the new commission published a detailed roadmap acknowledging electoral problems; some opposition amendments were accepted; women retained reserved parliamentary representation; the government cooperated with UN humanitarian agencies; and reported extrajudicial killings fell sharply. These facts show residual pluralism and some institution-facing activity.

The stronger pattern, however, is continued executive predominance and asymmetric political freedom: an appointment law enacted hurriedly without consensus; deficient election conditions and violent local contests; arrests, cases, police force, ruling-party intimidation, and broadcasting restrictions against opponents; weak courts, parliament, anticorruption and human-rights bodies; denial of Odhikar’s registration upheld by the Prime Minister’s Office; continuing Digital Security Act prosecutions despite an extensive UN critique; media pressure and self-censorship; and reported throttling around opposition rallies. Attribution is strongest for legislation, executive-controlled institutions, the Odhikar appeal, and Hasina’s rewarding of sanctioned RAB officials; it is authority-based or shared for police, party-wing, local-election, and minority-protection failures.

Information conditions materially lower confidence in incident totals. Domestic journalists and NGOs could report, but surveillance, owner pressure, advertising leverage, blocked outlets, criminal prosecution, and self-censorship create underreporting risk. Government denials and opposition allegations therefore require independent checks. English-access and international-NGO concentration are additional limitations.

## Ten-question disposition

1. **4B.1 — Electoral contestability:** Strong adverse evidence, with limited favorable process evidence. The inherited national mandate was based on a deeply defective election (WEB-4B-002). Local elections in 2022 involved intimidation, violence and irregularities, while opposition organizing was impeded (WEB-4B-005). The EC law and roadmap constitute favorable evidence, but their legitimacy and implementation were contested (WEB-4B-003–004).

2. **4B.2 — Manipulation of institutions/resources:** Strong coverage. Executive influence over the EC appointment process, parliament, courts and public institutions was substantial (WEB-4B-001, 003, 008). Police resources and criminal cases were used asymmetrically against opposition activity (WEB-4B-005).

3. **4B.3 — Tolerance of opposition, criticism and monitoring:** Strong adverse evidence. Large protests and critical reporting remained possible, but at significant risk: arrests, deaths, violence, DSA prosecutions, monitoring, media restrictions and action against Odhikar are documented (WEB-4B-005–007, 009–010, 012–013).

4. **4B.4 — Independent constraints:** Strong adverse institutional evidence. The EC roadmap is the main favorable item. Courts, parliament, the ACC, NHRC and NGO oversight lacked effective independence or protection (WEB-4B-003–004, 008–009).

5. **4B.5 — Cult, intimidation, loyalty and party capture:** Moderately strong adverse evidence. Article 70 and the Awami League’s parliamentary dominance suppressed internal constraint; party affiliates reportedly acted with impunity; DSA provisions protected the “father of the nation” and prosecutions disproportionately concerned criticism of Hasina and party leaders; Hasina personally awarded sanctioned RAB commanders (WEB-4B-006–008, 012–013). Evidence of formal personal loyalty tests is weak.

6. **4B.6 — Independent media and information:** Strong adverse evidence. DSA architecture and prosecutions, government intimidation, advertising pressure, self-censorship, state-media propaganda and rally-related connectivity restrictions materially narrowed information freedom. Some leading private papers retained limited independence (WEB-4B-006–007, 010, 013).

7. **4B.7 — Political equality:** Moderate, mixed evidence. No law formally barred women or minorities from participation, and 50 parliamentary seats remained reserved for women. Nevertheless, women, religious and ethnic minorities, Indigenous people, LGBTQI+ people and Rohingya remained underrepresented or restricted; failures to protect Hindu communities and Indigenous political security were reported (WEB-4B-011).

8. **4B.8 — Term limits and transfer:** Weak direct-year coverage. Bangladesh imposed no prime-ministerial term limit, and no succession or transfer occurred in 2022. The inherited defective 2018 election and rejection of the opposition’s caretaker-government demand are relevant, but 2022 alone cannot demonstrate an actual refusal of transfer (WEB-4B-002–003).

9. **4B.9 — Surveillance and digital/administrative harassment:** Strong evidence. DSA search, arrest and blocking powers; prosecutions; NGO and defender surveillance; Odhikar’s registration denial; and reported rally throttling establish a multi-mechanism pattern. The BTRC denied ordering shutdowns, so the precise chain of command remains disputed (WEB-4B-006–007, 009–010).

10. **4B.10 — Trajectory:** Strong evidence that 2022 did not materially repair the already eroded inherited system. The EC statute and roadmap were limited positive developments, and extrajudicial killings declined, but executive concentration, coercive opposition management, DSA enforcement, weak oversight and restricted monitoring persisted. Later RSF synthesis corroborates a continuing Hasina-era deterioration but is not used to import post-2022 events into the target year (WEB-4B-004, 008, 012–013).

## Discovery and saturation

Approximately 44 distinct leads appeared in supplied materials and searches. Thirteen records were accepted: 11 contemporaneous or immediate-retrospective records, one inherited-baseline record, and one later-retrospective record. Four source attempts were blocked or only partial; at least 12 low-value, duplicate, partisan-only, post-window, or tertiary leads were rejected; the remainder were corroborative or uninspected leads.

The evidentiary landscape is saturated for the central 2022 pattern: electoral baseline, executive concentration, opposition treatment, DSA enforcement, media conditions, oversight weakness, NGO pressure, and digital interference. Additional research could still materially improve:

- 4B.8, through primary constitutional history and contemporaneous debate over the absence of prime-ministerial term limits;
- 4B.5, through administrative appointment and civil-service politicization records;
- 4B.7, through Bangla-language minority and Indigenous political-participation sources;
- 4B.9, through BTRC/operator records or adjudication establishing the shutdown chain of command;
- trajectory precision, through locally stored V-Dem/Polity country-year data.
