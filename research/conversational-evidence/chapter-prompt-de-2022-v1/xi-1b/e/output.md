## Orientation

Period resolved as calendar year **2022**. Exposure classification: **material_exposure**.

Xi Jinping possessed exceptionally strong authority-based responsibility. He was CCP general secretary, PRC president, and chairman of both Central Military Commissions; his October report reaffirmed the “CMC chairman responsibility system,” absolute Party control of the PLA, centralized national-security leadership, and expansion of “strategic deterrence.” Public evidence does not reveal the precise nuclear-release procedure, but major changes to force structure and doctrine were unlikely to proceed without his approval.

The inherited baseline combined a comparatively small arsenal, a longstanding declaratory no-first-use policy, reportedly low peacetime readiness and centralized custody, with modernization already underway. In 2022, the decisive adverse development was independently observed rapid expansion: more than 300 new missile silos and a U.S. estimate exceeding 400 operational warheads. The expansion was opaque, outside any negotiated numerical limit or inspection arrangement, and accompanied by work on survivability, early warning and broader strategic-deterrence systems.

Favorable evidence includes China’s participation in the January P5 risk-reduction statement; continued no-first-use and negative-security-assurance declarations; support for the NPT, CTBT, safeguards, JCPOA restoration and human control of military AI; an established civil nuclear-safety statute; and IAEA verification at selected Chinese facilities. These were primarily declaratory, legal or limited-scope institutional facts rather than demonstrated restraint of the weapons buildup.

The clearest 2022 crisis evidence is adverse: during the Taiwan crisis, Beijing conducted unprecedented exercises and suspended senior military and maritime-security dialogue with Washington—the channels most useful for preventing miscalculation. China also vetoed additional UN sanctions after North Korea’s accelerating missile tests, while arguing that diplomacy and relief from pressure offered a better solution.

Important constraints included U.S.–China rivalry, U.S. and Russian much larger arsenals, missile defence concerns, Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, the Taiwan crisis, North Korean escalation and the stalled Iran talks. China’s closed information system makes absence of accidents or unauthorized-use incidents non-probative. Civil nuclear regulation is more observable than military custody, launch authority, fissile-material stocks or operational alerting.

Overall, the record is mixed but asymmetrical: formal restraint and risk-governance positions were substantial, while observable force expansion, opacity and interruption of crisis communications materially increased uncertainty and escalation risk. No score is assigned.

## Ten-question disposition

1. **1B.1 — Mixed, adverse force trend.** The P5 statement and NFU diplomacy sought risk reduction (WEB-1B-002, 005), but silo construction and arsenal growth were materially contrary evidence (WEB-1B-003, 004). Public evidence does not establish whether expansion was solely retaliatory survivability or also coercive leverage.

2. **1B.2 — Generally restrained explicit nuclear language, with limits.** China reaffirmed NFU, defensive purpose and the proposition that nuclear war cannot be won (WEB-1B-002, 005). No Xi-attributed 2022 explicit nuclear-use threat was located. This finding concerns known rhetoric only; silence is not proof of responsible operational planning.

3. **1B.3 — Favorable formal safeguards; military implementation opaque.** The P5 pledge addressed unintended and unauthorized use, China had detailed civil nuclear-safety law, and selected peaceful facilities remained under IAEA safeguards (WEB-1B-002, 010, 011). No independent target-year evidence establishes military warhead custody, personnel reliability, launch authentication or accident rates.

4. **1B.4 — Mixed.** China supported NPT, CTBT, safeguards, fissile-material negotiations and JCPOA restoration (WEB-1B-005, 008, 011), but did not accept arsenal limits or intrusive weapons transparency while expanding rapidly (WEB-1B-003, 012).

5. **1B.5 — Material adverse crisis evidence.** China’s arsenal was not openly invoked to threaten nuclear use, but strategic-deterrence expansion occurred alongside intensified Taiwan coercion; unprecedented exercises and severed communications increased escalation risk (WEB-1B-004, 006). Evidence does not prove that Xi explicitly used nuclear weapons as a shield for the exercises.

6. **1B.6 — Mixed to adverse.** China promoted restoration of constraints on Iran (WEB-1B-008), but vetoed new DPRK sanctions amid accelerating prohibited missile activity (WEB-1B-007). Beijing’s stated rationale—sanctions could intensify confrontation and diplomacy was preferable—is relevant limiting evidence, but the veto removed a concrete enforcement measure.

7. **1B.7 — Institutional investment visible, expert independence unclear.** Xi called for strengthened risk-monitoring, nuclear, cyber and biological security systems; China’s AI paper required risk assessment and accountability (WEB-1B-001, 009). No reliable evidence was found on whether nuclear decision-making privileged independent technical expertise over political loyalty.

8. **1B.8 — Mixed, with a major adverse crisis choice.** China advocated strategic-stability dialogue and JCPOA diplomacy (WEB-1B-002, 005, 008), but suspended senior military and maritime-safety communications during the acute Taiwan crisis (WEB-1B-006). Strategic-stability dialogue remained underdeveloped (WEB-1B-012).

9. **1B.9 — Favorable formal policy, weak transparency.** Military-AI policy endorsed human control, operator accountability, testing and restraint; Xi called for biological, cyber, data and nuclear risk systems; civil nuclear law imposed preventive and emergency duties (WEB-1B-001, 009, 010). Evidence of implementation, independent audits or disclosure of military cyber/biological programmes remains inadequate.

10. **1B.10 — More dangerous observable nuclear posture by year-end, despite formal restraints.** Expansion, opacity, entanglement and crisis-channel suspension increased capability and miscalculation risk (WEB-1B-003, 004, 006, 012). NFU, low-alert traditions and risk-governance commitments are genuine counterweights, but no verified reduction or binding constraint offset the growth.
