## Orientation

**Period:** 1 January–31 December 2022. Olaf Scholz became chancellor on 8 December 2021, so this is effectively his first full calendar year.

**Authority:** Scholz determined the government’s general policy guidelines, chaired the cabinet and participated in the Federal Security Council, but he was not the Bundeswehr’s peacetime operational commander; that authority belonged to Defence Minister Christine Lambrecht. Armed foreign deployments normally also required Bundestag approval. Arms-export decisions were shared among ministries and, for sensitive cases, the Federal Security Council. The Bundestag approved and scrutinised foreign missions and the €100 billion special fund.

**Conflict/exposure gate:** Germany did not deploy combat forces into Ukraine or become a NATO belligerent there. Its exposure was nevertheless substantial: weapons and training for Ukraine, sanctions and economic coercion against Russia, deterrence deployments on NATO territory, inherited UN/NATO/EU missions, arms exports to other states, and significant diplomatic influence. Germany therefore is not a “no-exposure” case.

**D13 UCDP qualification:** UCDP’s current country-year API requires an access token, and the downloadable Germany row could not be opened through the available interface. Consequently, this dossier does **not** promote an inferred zero-conflict value into an extracted UCDP statistic. The operational record independently shows no German combat deployment in Ukraine, while the Parliamentary Commissioner recorded nine other Bundestag-mandated missions at year-end. The unresolved UCDP row is listed as an uninspected lead.

**D14 SIPRI facts:** SIPRI estimated German military expenditure at **$55.8 billion in 2022**, 2.3% higher in real terms than 2021 and 33% higher than 2013; this included about $2 billion in military aid to Ukraine. The €100 billion fund was largely intended for use from 2023. Germany was also the fifth-largest exporter of major arms over 2018–22. [SIPRI expenditure](https://www.sipri.org/sites/default/files/2023-04/2304_fs_milex_2022.pdf), [SIPRI transfers](https://www.sipri.org/publications/2023/sipri-fact-sheets/trends-international-arms-transfers-2022).

**Inherited baseline and shocks:** Scholz inherited Russia’s 2014 annexation of Crimea, the Donbas conflict and faltering Minsk/Normandy process; long-standing NATO, Kosovo, Mali, Iraq, Lebanon and Mediterranean commitments; depleted Bundeswehr stocks; and heavy Russian-energy dependence. Russia’s 24 February invasion, mass displacement, attacks on civilians and energy infrastructure, nuclear-escalation risk and the collapse of German assumptions about economic interdependence dominated the year.

**Information conditions:** Germany had open parliamentary and media contestation: Scholz was criticised both for excessive caution and for escalation through arms transfers. Operational stocks, intelligence, arms-delivery details and Federal Security Council deliberations were less observable. Official speeches reliably establish decisions and stated purposes, but not efficacy or private motives.

**Central balance, without a score:** The strongest favorable evidence is pre-invasion diplomacy; the absence of German combat entry; insistence on Ukrainian self-determination and territorial integrity; rejection of annexation; escalation limits; defensive and humanitarian support; continued contacts with Putin; support for the ICJ, ICC and OSCE investigations; and participation in multilateral peacekeeping. Adverse or limiting evidence includes the scale of rearmament, opaque and initially flawed special-fund planning, arms exports to Saudi Arabia and other high-risk recipients, weak public evidence of recipient-specific end-use/IHL conditions, and delay or opacity in some Ukraine deliveries. The regional end-state was dramatically less peaceful, principally because of Russia’s invasion; Germany helped contain wider NATO–Russia war but did not secure a 2022 ceasefire or settlement.

## Ten-question disposition

1. **2B.1 — Substantial favorable evidence, with a contested post-invasion balance.** Scholz pursued NATO–Russia, OSCE, Normandy and bilateral diplomacy before the invasion (WEB-2B-004), kept channels open afterward (WEB-2B-008), and imposed escalation limits (WEB-2B-007). Critics argued that arms deliveries displaced diplomacy or risked escalation; others argued his delays weakened Ukraine’s defensive position (WEB-2B-018).

2. **2B.2 — No evidence that Scholz initiated a war of choice or German cross-border combat campaign.** Germany supplied Ukraine’s defence and strengthened NATO territory but explicitly avoided NATO combat entry (WEB-2B-005–007). Other deployments were inherited and multilaterally mandated (WEB-2B-016). Arms support materially affected a war but followed Russia’s invasion.

3. **2B.3 — Generally clear separation of defensive aims from conquest or revenge.** Scholz framed the threat as forced border change and imperial restoration, limited Germany’s objective to Ukraine’s defence and NATO security, rejected a dictated peace and refused direct NATO war (WEB-2B-005, 007, 009). His historical rhetoric was forceful but tied to observable Russian conduct, not a manufactured German grievance.

4. **2B.4 — No German combat conduct in Ukraine to assess directly.** Germany funded humanitarian and medical assistance and supplied air defence (WEB-2B-010). Recipient forces nevertheless committed documented violations involving civilian positioning and POW treatment (WEB-2B-011–012). No inspected source tied a specific German weapon to those abuses.

5. **2B.5 — Mixed and incompletely observable.** Export licensing and multilateral coordination supplied some restraint, and the early Saudi ban showed recipient-risk awareness (WEB-2B-017). But public evidence did not demonstrate that Scholz conditioned Ukrainian aid on specific remedial IHL measures after documented violations. Germany remained a major global exporter (WEB-2B-003) and approved cooperation-related Saudi exports.

6. **2B.6 — Public justification was broadly corroborated, though official rhetoric cannot prove private motive.** Russian military buildup and the invasion itself corroborated Scholz’s warnings; his description of an unlawful attack matched UN/OHCHR findings. His claim that Putin sought imperial revision was interpretive but grounded in Russia’s annexation attempts and invasion (WEB-2B-004–005). No evidence emerged of fabricated German intelligence or a knowingly false casus belli.

7. **2B.7 — Credible diplomatic and legal efforts, but no settlement achieved.** Scholz sought de-escalation before invasion, repeatedly called for ceasefire/withdrawal, kept contact with Putin, supported a UN-brokered grain agreement and insisted Ukraine control its peace terms (WEB-2B-004, 008–009). Germany supported the ICJ and post-war reconstruction (WEB-2B-014). Evidence does not show that Scholz possessed and rejected a clearly viable 2022 settlement.

8. **2B.8 — Real security rationale with important governance weaknesses.** Russia’s invasion, depleted stocks and alliance obligations supply a concrete security basis for the special fund and spending increase (WEB-2B-002, 007). The Federal Audit Office found overplanning, omitted debt-service costs and opaque aggregate titles (WEB-2B-015). No inspected evidence establishes personal enrichment or rewarding a Scholz patronage network.

9. **2B.9 — Institutional accountability was meaningful but incomplete.** Germany supported OSCE, ICC and ICJ mechanisms (WEB-2B-013–014), parliamentary scrutiny was open, and Scholz publicly acknowledged inadequate ammunition preparation (WEB-2B-007). There was no German operational civilian-harm finding requiring personal admission. Public evidence of a Scholz response to Ukrainian recipient violations was thin.

10. **2B.10 — Outcome is externally dominated and cannot be characterised as a peaceful improvement.** Europe ended 2022 at large-scale war, with strained German–Russian relations and greater militarisation. Russia’s invasion was the primary cause. Germany helped prevent direct NATO–Russia combat, sustained multilateral missions, supported refugees, humanitarian relief and reconstruction, and strengthened NATO deterrence (WEB-2B-007, 010, 016), but did not end the war.

## Remaining questions and saturation

Further research could materially improve three areas:

- an authenticated extraction of Germany’s 2022 UCDP country-year row;
- classified or parliamentary-confidential evidence on Ukraine end-use monitoring and Federal Security Council reasoning;
- recipient-specific evidence connecting German arms, training or diplomatic leverage to corrective action after Ukrainian IHL violations.

Open-source searching otherwise reached practical saturation: further results largely repeated the diplomacy, arms chronology, spending, escalation-restraint and accountability evidence already captured.
