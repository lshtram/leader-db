## 1. Orientation

### Scope and exposure gate

The period is calendar year 2022 only. Putin plainly passes the conflict-exposure gate: he exercised supreme command over Russian forces, personally announced the 24 February operation, subsequently ordered mobilization, and presided over attempted annexations. The evidence concerns an inherited internationalized conflict that he radically escalated into interstate war—not ordinary domestic policing.

### Authority

Articles 80 and 87 of Russia’s Constitution make the president responsible for basic foreign-policy direction and Supreme Commander-in-Chief. Putin’s public decisions—recognition of the separatist entities, announcement of military operations, September mobilization, and the annexation ceremony—confirm practical as well as formal authority. Operational crimes cannot all be attributed personally, but strategic initiation, escalation, mobilization, annexation, failure to comply with the ICJ order, and system-level remedy fall within his authority.

### Inherited baseline

Russia entered 2022:

- occupying and claiming Crimea since 2014;
- supporting armed formations controlling parts of Donetsk and Luhansk;
- participating in the Minsk, Normandy Four, and Trilateral Contact Group processes;
- facing an unresolved but substantially lower-intensity Donbas conflict;
- confronting a genuine dispute over NATO enlargement and European security.

The OSCE recorded a fragile ceasefire and serious monitoring restrictions. Nevertheless, after the Trilateral Contact Group’s 22 December 2021 recommitment, ceasefire violations fell 60% during the following month. Violations then rose sharply immediately before 24 February, but the OSCE did not assign all incidents to one side and was operating under access constraints. This is an inherited-security problem, not evidence that a nationwide invasion was necessary.

### External shocks and constraints

Relevant constraints included NATO–Russia distrust, Russia’s large force buildup, the deteriorating Donbas ceasefire, Ukraine’s military cooperation with Western states, and failed implementation of Minsk. Yet NATO and the United States delivered written proposals covering dialogue, risk reduction, transparency, and arms control on 26 January. No NATO attack on Russia or internationally accepted genocide finding preceded the invasion.

After 24 February, Ukrainian resistance, Western arms assistance and sanctions, Russian battlefield setbacks, and the absence of mutually accepted security guarantees made settlement harder. These later constraints do not explain the initial decision to invade.

### Information environment

Russian wartime censorship and punishment of dissent make domestic silence weak evidence. Putin’s own speeches are direct evidence of his claims, but not proof those claims were true. International monitoring also had limitations: the OSCE faced movement restrictions; OHCHR and the ICRC lacked full Russian-controlled access; wartime fatality estimates remain incomplete; and the Istanbul negotiations are reconstructed partly from participants and draft texts.

## 3. Disposition by evidence lens

### 2B.1 — Initiation, diplomacy, and alternatives

Strong adverse evidence, with meaningful contrary material. A real security dispute and unstable Donbas conflict existed, but so did the Minsk/OSCE machinery, NATO’s written arms-control proposals, and a recently improved ceasefire. Putin chose a nationwide attack before those avenues were exhausted (WEB-2B-002–006). Spring negotiations and the grain agreement show Russia was capable of diplomacy, but occurred after the invasion (WEB-2B-013–014).

### 2B.2 — War of choice, expansion, coercion, and annexation

Strongly documented. Putin announced operations across Ukraine, continued them after the ICJ suspension order, mobilized additional forces after battlefield reversals, and attempted to annex four Ukrainian regions, including territory Russia did not fully control (WEB-2B-004–008, 015).

### 2B.3 — Defensive necessity versus other objectives

The defensive interpretation has a credible foundation only at the level of general threat perception: NATO enlargement, Western military cooperation with Ukraine, and Donbas insecurity were real Russian concerns. However, the breadth of the invasion, “denazification” and regime-directed rhetoric, attacks far beyond Donbas, and attempted annexations are inconsistent with a narrowly tailored defence of Donetsk and Luhansk. NATO’s January offer of dialogue and risk reduction further limits a necessity claim (WEB-2B-003–007).

### 2B.4 — Civilian protection, proportionality, and prisoners

Extensive adverse institutional findings. OHCHR and the UN Commission documented summary executions, attacks lacking distinction or precautions, torture, sexual violence, arbitrary detention, filtration, and unlawful child transfers. The ICRC lacked access to thousands of POWs. Ukrainian forces also committed documented violations, including torture and likely indiscriminate attacks, but these do not negate the larger Russian patterns or Russia’s obligations as the invading and occupying power (WEB-2B-009–012, 017).

### 2B.5 — Control of proxies, affiliated forces, and clients

Adverse but less complete at the level of individual command orders. OHCHR attributed detention and torture to Russian forces and affiliated armed groups; the ICJ expressly ordered Russia to restrain military or irregular units it directed or supported. The continuation of these patterns, Russia’s integration and later annexation of separatist-held territory, and lack of independent investigation support responsibility at the policy and remedy level. Internal proxy-control records remain unavailable (WEB-2B-006, 009, 011–012).

### 2B.6 — Truthful explanation of threats

Strong evidence of manipulative or unsupported justification. Putin invoked genocide, an imminent existential Western threat, “denazification,” and collective self-defence. The ICJ found Ukraine had a plausible right not to be subjected to operations based on the genocide allegation; Russia presented no substantiated genocide case in that proceeding. The invasion’s territorial reach and subsequent annexations contradicted the narrower collective-defence framing (WEB-2B-004, 006–007).

### 2B.7 — Ceasefires and settlements

Mixed, but adverse overall. Russia participated in Belarus/Istanbul talks and accepted the Grain Initiative and its November renewal—real favorable evidence. The Istanbul drafts show mutual movement on neutrality and security guarantees, but unresolved territory, force limits, guarantee mechanics, and sequencing; there was no completed peace agreement that one outside actor simply vetoed. The reported Kozak proposal is relevant but remains anonymously sourced and unverified as to Ukrainian final acceptance. Annexation and mobilization later narrowed the bargaining space (WEB-2B-013–015).

### 2B.8 — Spending, mobilization, patronage, and intimidation

Escalatory use is established; enrichment motives are not. SIPRI estimated Russian military expenditure rose 9.2% in real terms to about $86.4 billion and 4.1% of GDP; actual national-defence spending substantially exceeded prewar plans. Putin ordered partial mobilization after major battlefield setbacks. Available sources do not establish what portion enriched personal networks or whether procurement corruption motivated the war, so that part remains unresolved (WEB-2B-015).

### 2B.9 — Accountability and correction

Strong evidence of non-correction at the ruler level. Russia continued operations despite the ICJ order, denied or deflected many abuse findings, restricted independent access, and produced no credible public investigation assigning senior responsibility during 2022. Putin escalated through mobilization and annexation rather than acknowledging initial military assumptions or civilian consequences. The later ICC warrant is not a conviction, but provides direct reasonable-grounds attribution concerning child transfer (WEB-2B-006, 009–012, 015, 017).

### 2B.10 — Peaceful, stable, lawful end state

Strong adverse outcome evidence. The year ended with an interstate war estimated by UCDP at more than 81,500 battle deaths, attempted annexations rejected by 143 UN members, 11.6 million Ukrainians still displaced, higher Russian and European military spending, and Finnish and Swedish NATO applications. Causation is shared after initiation—Ukraine resisted and Western states armed it—but the change from the January baseline is inseparable from Putin’s invasion decision (WEB-2B-005, 007–008, 015–016).

## 4. Source-state audit

### Independent corroboration

- UN General Assembly resolutions ES-11/1 and ES-11/4 corroborate the legal and territorial characterization independently of Ukrainian claims.
- OHCHR’s monitoring mission, its December killings investigation, and the independent Commission overlap institutionally but used different mandates and evidence collections.
- ICRC access evidence is independent of OHCHR abuse interviews.
- UCDP’s fatality dataset, UNHCR displacement totals, SIPRI expenditure data, and NATO accession records measure different consequences.
- Putin’s official speeches corroborate personal decision and justification, but not the truth of those justifications.
- Reuters’ Kozak account relied on anonymous leadership-adjacent sources and is not independent of later articles repeating it.

### Reused supplied sources

- **E001:** Kremlin authority page. Reused in concept; direct opening timed out, replaced by the Russian Supreme Court’s constitutional text.
- **E005:** ICJ order. Reused; direct page was blocked, but the searchable official order text and operative paragraphs were extracted.
- **E011:** UN Commission graphic report. Reused; PDF direct opening was blocked, but official indexed text was extracted and checked against OHCHR reporting.
- **E012:** ICC Ukraine situation page. Reused; direct opening was blocked, official indexed text extracted.
- **E039:** ICRC POW-access statement. Fully opened and extracted.
- **E040:** Grain Initiative. Reused and strengthened with UN signing, operational, and renewal pages.
- **E041:** UN Security Council diplomatic-channel coverage. Retained as corroboration; OSCE and NATO records supply the stronger baseline facts.
- **E047:** Treasury account of separatist recognition. Not used as final evidence because primary Kremlin and UN records were preferable.
- **E050:** Responsible Statecraft/Reuters summary. Retained only as a limited negotiation lead; the Reuters wire reproduction was inspected.

### Promising but still needing inspection

- Full Russian and Ukrainian spring 2022 draft-treaty texts and Istanbul communiqué variants.
- Russian General Staff operational directives and rules of engagement.
- Russian military-prosecutor files concerning Bucha, POW treatment, filtration, and child transfers.
- Detailed 2022 procurement and beneficial-ownership records.
- UCDP event-level download for actor-by-actor 2022 fatality coding.
- PA-X coding for any 2022 Russia–Ukraine humanitarian or prisoner arrangements.

### Rejected sources

- Wikipedia summaries: useful for discovery, not accepted as evidence.
- Reddit reproductions of Reuters and Kremlin material: derivative and unnecessary.
- Belligerent casualty totals from either defence ministry: not independently verifiable.
- U.S. Treasury characterizations of Putin’s rationale as “false”: replaced where possible by ICJ and UN material.
- Claims that Boris Johnson alone “blocked” a completed Istanbul agreement: the evidence reviewed shows unresolved substantive and guarantee issues.
- Claims that Minsk signatories’ later statements prove the agreements were solely a deception: retrospective political statements do not independently establish all parties’ 2014–2022 intent.

### Access-blocked

- ICJ node page: repeated direct opening returned HTTP 403; official search extraction exposed the order’s operative text.
- OHCHR Commission graphic PDF: direct opening returned HTTP 403; official indexed extraction and parallel OHCHR pages were available.
- ICC Ukraine situation page: direct opening returned HTTP 403; official indexed extraction was available.
- Several Kremlin transcript pages: direct requests timed out; Kremlin index/search extracts and independent transcript reproductions were used cautiously.
- SIPRI publication landing page intermittently timed out; the fact sheet, press release, and PDF-indexed text remained available.

## 6. Remaining questions and saturation

Further public-source research is unlikely to change the central findings on initiation, annexation, civilian harm, mobilization, or year-end instability. It could materially improve:

- the precise internal decision process before 24 February;
- Putin’s knowledge of particular operational crimes;
- actual proxy command-and-control arrangements;
- whether a specific spring settlement was acceptable to both governments at the same time;
- procurement enrichment and military-patronage beneficiaries;
- remedial orders, if any, issued privately.

Those gaps chiefly affect attribution granularity and lenses 2B.5, 2B.8, and 2B.9. They do not erase the well-documented strategic decisions.
