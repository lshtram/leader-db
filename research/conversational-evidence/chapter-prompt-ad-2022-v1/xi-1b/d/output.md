## Scope and bottom line

Period resolved as **1 January–31 December 2022**; the user’s “2022–2022” is treated as a single target year.

**Exposure classification: `material_exposure`.** Xi Jinping chaired the Chinese Communist Party, the state, and the Central Military Commission (CMC). Public scholarship indicates that alerting or launching Chinese nuclear forces required agreement at the highest party-military level, while Xi’s October 2022 report directed implementation of the “CMC chairman responsibility system,” expansion of “strategic deterrence,” and modernization of joint command, warning, cyber, and intelligent warfare capabilities.

The record is mixed but not symmetrical:

- China maintained its longstanding declaratory no-first-use (NFU) policy, joined the January P5 risk-reduction statement, promoted safeguards and nuclear-security cooperation, and Xi personally opposed nuclear use and threats amid Russia’s war.
- China did not issue direct nuclear threats in the Taiwan crisis.
- Against that, China was undertaking the largest expansion in its nuclear history, building more than 300 apparent missile silos and increasing readiness, diversity, and potential launch-on-warning capability. Its operational stockpile exceeded 400 warheads by the Pentagon’s 2022 estimate.
- During the August Taiwan crisis, China conducted unprecedented coercive exercises and suspended senior military and maritime-safety dialogue—the channels most relevant to controlling accidental escalation.
- China vetoed strengthened UN measures against North Korea after renewed ICBM testing, although it argued that sanctions were counterproductive and harmful to diplomacy and civilians.
- Formal nuclear-safety and military-AI rules were precautionary, but public evidence does not independently establish implementation inside the opaque nuclear command system.

This dossier describes evidence and does **not** assign a score.

## Orientation

### Authority

Xi’s personal authority was exceptionally broad. He was CCP general secretary and CMC chairman throughout 2022, and president of the PRC. His Party Congress report explicitly required stronger implementation of the CMC-chairman responsibility system and a stronger strategic-deterrence system. Independent analysis assessed that major nuclear-force changes were highly unlikely without Xi’s authorization, although operational records and specific orders remain secret.

Attribution is therefore strongest for Xi’s direct speeches, Party-Congress directives, leadership-level diplomacy, and broad force-development policy. It is weaker for technical implementation, individual PLA deployments, regulatory enforcement, and ambassadorial statements.

### Inherited baseline

China entered 2022 with:

- a longstanding official NFU doctrine and negative security assurances;
- a historically small, centrally controlled, low-readiness arsenal, with many warheads normally separated from launchers;
- an arsenal already undergoing rapid transformation since approximately 2019;
- three large apparent ICBM-silo fields already under construction;
- the NPT, IAEA safeguards, an unratified CTBT signature, a nuclear-safety statute effective since 2018, and participation in P5 risk-reduction diplomacy.

The silo expansion therefore was not initiated wholly inside calendar 2022, but Xi’s government maintained and developed it during the target year.

### Shocks and constraints

The central shocks were Russia’s invasion of Ukraine and nuclear threats; North Korean missile and nuclear developments; deteriorating US-China strategic relations; and the August Taiwan crisis after Nancy Pelosi’s visit. These created unusually discriminating opportunities for restraint, crisis communication, proliferation policy, and nuclear rhetoric.

### Information environment

China publishes doctrine and legal instruments but not a warhead total, fissile-material production data, alert records, nuclear exercises, incident reports, or detailed command arrangements. Independent assessment consequently relies heavily on satellite imagery and external intelligence estimates. Absence of public accidents, dissent, or nuclear threats is not proof of safety or restraint.

## Disposition by lens

1. **1B.1 — Risk reduction versus leverage:** Mixed, with substantial adverse evidence. WEB-1B-005, 006 and 008 show risk-reduction commitments; WEB-1B-003, 004 and 012 show rapid expansion. WEB-1B-009 shows conventional coercion and withdrawal of guardrails during crisis.

2. **1B.2 — Nuclear rhetoric:** Substantially favorable in the observable record. China retained NFU, joined the P5 statement, and Xi opposed nuclear use and threats (WEB-1B-005, 006, 008). No direct Chinese nuclear-use threat was found in 2022. This does not resolve whether nuclear capability was implicitly supporting Taiwan coercion.

3. **1B.3 — Command, custody and accident prevention:** Mixed and opaque. Centralized civilian authorization, separated warheads, and the statutory safety framework are favorable (WEB-1B-002, 007). Increasing readiness, possible launch-on-warning development, entanglement with conventional systems, and secrecy create countervailing risks (WEB-1B-003, 004, 012).

4. **1B.4 — Arms control and inspection:** Mixed. China supported the NPT, IAEA safeguards, CTBT objectives, fissile-material negotiations, and P5 dialogue (WEB-1B-005, 006, 013, 014). It had not ratified the CTBT, rejected the TPNW approach, did not enter a binding arsenal-control arrangement, and expanded its forces.

5. **1B.5 — Nuclear shield for conventional coercion:** Important adverse possibility, not conclusively proven. The Taiwan exercises and strategic-deterrence buildup coincide (WEB-1B-009, 012), and independent scholarship identified a possible “nuclear shield” for non-nuclear escalation. China did not explicitly threaten nuclear use, and there is insufficient evidence that Xi ordered the exercises under nuclear cover.

6. **1B.6 — Proliferation by allies or clients:** Adverse/disputed. China vetoed additional DPRK sanctions after ICBM tests (WEB-1B-010). China argued sanctions would worsen humanitarian conditions and reduce diplomatic prospects; the record does not establish Chinese assistance to the DPRK nuclear program in 2022.

7. **1B.7 — Expertise and institutions:** Limited favorable institutional evidence. The Nuclear Safety Law mandates qualified specialists, independent regulation, training, research, inspections, and emergency capacity (WEB-1B-007); Xi called for strengthened risk-monitoring and technical systems (WEB-1B-012). No sufficiently independent evidence was found about nuclear advisers’ professional autonomy versus political loyalty.

8. **1B.8 — Crisis behavior and channels:** Mixed but materially adverse in the Taiwan crisis. Xi’s Ukraine rhetoric was de-escalatory (WEB-1B-008), but Beijing suspended senior military and maritime-security dialogue while conducting unprecedented Taiwan exercises (WEB-1B-009).

9. **1B.9 — Other catastrophic technologies:** Formally favorable but implementation-uncertain. China’s military-AI paper required human control, suspension capability, accountability, lifecycle safeguards, and restraint against premature deployment (WEB-1B-011). Xi ordered stronger cyber, biological, nuclear, data, warning, and emergency systems while also accelerating intelligent and cyber warfare capability (WEB-1B-012). No independently verifiable 2022 outcome evidence was found for biological or frontier-AI safeguards.

10. **1B.10 — End-of-period posture:** More dangerous in capability and crisis-interaction terms than the inherited Xi-era baseline, despite retained doctrinal restraints. Force size, silo capacity, readiness options, and Taiwan escalation all increased; NFU, centralized authorization, safety law, and responsible rhetoric remained mitigating features (WEB-1B-002–009, 012).
