Average of the case-level overall scores:

- **Artifact X family: approximately 8.8**
- **Artifact Y family: approximately 8.7**

The numerical difference is small, but the distribution matters. X wins seven cases and is particularly stronger where the chapter requires broad domain coverage, retrospective adjudication, formal authority analysis, and extensive favorable/adverse reconciliation. Y is consistently more efficient and occasionally has a better independent-source mix.

## Recurring strengths of X

- More complete treatment of all ten chapter questions.
- Strong authority and practical-control orientation.
- Better inherited-baseline and external-shock separation.
- More explicit source-state registers and unresolved gaps.
- Better use of later adjudication when it directly resolves target-period conduct.
- More complete machine records with attribution, period fit, contrary evidence and lens mappings.

## Recurring defects of X

- Excessive length and record proliferation.
- Multiple records drawn from the same underlying report without always making the dependence prominent.
- “Fully extracted” labels applied to some facts available only through indexed text or partial landing pages.
- Too many low-discrimination contextual records.
- Occasional synthetic records that combine several sources and facts rather than remaining atomic.
- Counts and saturation claims are sometimes more elaborate than their decision value warrants.

## Recurring strengths of Y

- Higher evidence density and much better reading efficiency.
- More frequent use of admissions against interest, audits and parliamentary criticism.
- Cleaner separation between new records and reused evidence.
- Good residual-gap summaries.
- Strong locators in several cases.
- Willingness to leave thin lenses thin rather than inflate them with generic context.

## Recurring defects of Y

- Narrower substantive coverage, especially for secondary welfare, institutional or end-state domains.
- More frequent omission of useful favorable or adverse counterexamples.
- In some cases, inadequate replacement of earlier allegations with later final adjudications.
- Occasional machine-format defects or unstable URLs.
- Some “context” units included in accepted counts even though they should not carry full evidentiary weight.

# Overall recommendation

The ten-case evidence is **strong enough to promote the prompt behind Artifact X to the next end-to-end gate**, but the promotion should be conditional on incorporating Y’s efficiency and source-selection discipline.

The case is not that X’s raw average is dramatically higher; it is that X is better in seven of ten cases and its advantages occur in the most consequential research functions: chapter coverage, authority separation, target-period framing, retrospective adjudication, contrary evidence and reusable records. Its disadvantages are principally correctable production costs rather than missing analytical capability.

Before promotion, revise the prompt to require:

1. A compact core dossier before any extended narrative.
2. A presumption against multiple records from one source unless each adds a genuinely independent material fact.
3. Distinct statuses for:
   - fully opened underlying source;
   - partially opened or landing-page extraction;
   - indexed-text extraction;
   - allegation;
   - procedural action;
   - final finding.
4. A cap or stopping rule based on uncovered material questions, not record count.
5. Preference for admissions against interest, audits, final judgments and implementation data.
6. Machine validation for JSON and URL stability.
7. Separate counts for accepted evidence, context-only units and corroboration.
8. A requirement that every additional record state what new fact it contributes beyond existing records.

With those changes, X’s prompt is the better candidate: it has the stronger research ceiling, while Y supplies the model for reducing duplication and complexity without sacrificing evidentiary honesty.
