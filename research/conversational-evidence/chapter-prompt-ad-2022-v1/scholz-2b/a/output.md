Chapter 2B research is complete and unscored. The evidence strongly supports treating Germany as a defensive supporter rather than an initiator of the war, while preserving important limitations concerning arms-delivery delays, procurement controls, recipient conduct, energy dependence, and outcome attribution.

## Research synthesis

Scholz pursued intensive diplomacy before the invasion, including NATO-Russia, OSCE, Normandy-format, bilateral, and allied channels. On 18 January he publicly prioritized preventing aggression and finding a negotiated political way forward; on 24 February he said dialogue had continued until the last minute and demanded an immediate Russian ceasefire and withdrawal. [NATO transcript](https://www.nato.int/en/news-and-events/events/transcripts/2022/01/18/press-conference), [Federal Government address](https://www.bundesregierung.de/breg-en/service/archive/televised-address-by-federal-chancellor-olaf-scholz-on-the-russian-attack-against-ukraine-2007846).

After the invasion, Germany supplied Ukraine with weapons under the rationale of UN Charter self-defence. Scholz simultaneously rejected unilateral German action, direct NATO participation, and decisions carrying disproportionate escalation risk. This is consistent with defensive support, although critics plausibly argued that his caution delayed needed weapons. [8 May address](https://www.bundesregierung.de/breg-en/service/archive/televised-address-by-olaf-scholz-chancellor-of-the-federal-republic-of-germany-and-member-of-the-german-bundestag-on-the-anniversary-of-the-end-of-the-second-world-war-in-europe-berlin-8-may-2022--2037674), [Reuters report](https://www.euronews.com/2022/05/01/uk-ukraine-crisis-germany-scholz), [Bundestag debate](https://www.bundestag.de/dokumente/textarchiv/2022/kw17-de-selbstverteidigung-ukraine-891272).

Scholz distinguished Putin and the Russian government from the Russian population, rejected historical and “genocide” narratives used to justify the invasion, and described the operational threat as an attempted coercive alteration of recognized borders. These public explanations were broadly consistent with NATO, UN and subsequent observable events, though official speeches establish stated reasoning rather than private intelligence quality.

Civilian-protection and accountability evidence is more indirect. Germany supported humanitarian assistance, international criminal accountability, and air-defence deliveries, while the UN documented overwhelming Russian violations and two investigated incidents of Ukrainian mistreatment of Russian soldiers. No reviewed source established Scholz ordering German forces to conduct combat operations or knowingly facilitating Ukrainian atrocities. Conversely, the wave did not locate a strong 2022 German public end-use-monitoring record addressing foreseeable recipient misconduct.

The €100 billion Bundeswehr fund was publicly tied to inherited readiness deficiencies, territorial defence and NATO capability obligations. That security rationale was credible, but parliamentary and audit evidence identified weak needs analysis, procurement deficiencies, possible overplanning, rising costs and interest burdens. Thus the fund should not be treated as proof of patronage enrichment, but neither should announced defensive purposes substitute for effectiveness or financial-control evidence.

Regional stability worsened dramatically during 2022 because of Russia’s invasion. Germany reinforced NATO, aided Ukrainian defence and avoided direct NATO-Russia combat, but it did not leave the region more peaceful in an outcome sense. Personal attribution is sharply constrained because the invasion was an external shock and German action was coalition-, parliamentary-, EU- and NATO-mediated.
