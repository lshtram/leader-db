## Scope and research status

This dossier assesses Sheikh Hasina’s record during calendar year 2022 only. Evidence inherited from earlier years is marked accordingly; later publications are used only when they report or analyze 2022 events.

No chapter score is assigned. The evidence strongly establishes an authoritarian and erosive pattern, but scoring and calibration against the smoke-test batch remain tasks for the chapter judge.

A technical limitation affected the prescribed local-first procedure: the workspace shell and local registry were unavailable. I therefore used the supplied local-prior description and source-confidence cues, attempted to open every underlying web source, and did not treat search-result text alone as fully extracted evidence.

## 1. Orientation

### Authority

Bangladesh formally had a parliamentary system, but practical executive authority was highly concentrated. The prime minister headed both the government and the dominant Awami League. Article 70’s anti-defection rule allowed party leadership to prevent governing-party MPs from voting independently, while the AL’s overwhelming parliamentary majority left little effective legislative opposition. BTI described this combination as making separation of powers largely illusory and leaving the bureaucracy, Election Commission, judiciary, and parliamentary oversight vulnerable to executive or partisan control (WEB-4B-001, WEB-4B-002).

Attribution should nevertheless be differentiated:

- Hasina was directly attributable for public statements, cabinet direction, governing-party leadership, and her decision to award medals to sanctioned RAB commanders.
- Executive institutions—including police, BTRC, the NGO Affairs Bureau, ministries, and the Prime Minister’s Office—support authority-based or institutional attribution.
- Violence by AL or Chhatra League activists is shared attribution unless direct orders are established. The stronger attributable issue is reported impunity and police failure to protect opposition events.
- Courts, the president, Election Commission, and local officials retained formal powers, but evidence indicates weak de facto independence.

### Inherited baseline

Hasina entered 2022 already governing from a severely compromised electoral baseline:

- The 2018 parliamentary election had been marked by intimidation, arrests, violence, fraud allegations, restrictions on observers, and an AL-led result of 288 out of 300 directly elected seats.
- The BNP had been organizationally weakened; Khaleda Zia remained under a suspended sentence and travel restrictions.
- BTI’s review described the legislature as lacking meaningful checks, courts as politicized, and the bureaucracy and Election Commission as partisan.
- The Digital Security Act of 2018 already supplied broad powers over online expression, arrest, search, blocking, and content removal.

These inherited conditions are not newly charged to 2022, but Hasina’s government retained and used them rather than materially remedying them.

### Shocks and constraints

COVID recovery, inflation, fuel-price increases, Ukraine-war pressures, floods and climate shocks, Rohingya displacement, and Myanmar spillover constrained government capacity and helped prompt opposition demonstrations. They do not explain the selective arrests, rally-related connectivity restrictions, refusal to reform the DSA, or harassment of monitors. Bangladesh was not under a declared constitutional emergency.

Political violence was not one-sided. The BNP and AL had a long history of confrontational politics; both sides accused the other of instigating 2022 clashes. That limits attribution for individual street incidents but not for police conduct, selective arrest patterns, regulatory restrictions, or failures to investigate ruling-party violence.

### Information environment

Domestic reporting continued, and international organizations, foreign governments, NGOs, and some local outlets documented abuses. This shows that criticism was possible, not eliminated. But self-censorship, blocked outlets, DSA exposure, advertising pressure, surveillance, attacks, NGO-registration controls, and possible retaliation against relatives create substantial underreporting risk. Repeated NGO, diplomatic, and press accounts often trace back to the same local reports or incidents and should not be counted as independent events.

## 3. Disposition of the ten lenses

### 4B.1 — Electoral contestability

Strong adverse evidence, with limited formal contrary evidence.

There was no national election in 2022, so direct acceptance of an adverse national result cannot be tested. The inherited 2018 election was seriously compromised, and the 2022 opposition crackdown began as the next election cycle approached (WEB-4B-002, WEB-4B-005, WEB-4B-006). Hasina publicly claimed Bangladesh could conduct elections and peacefully transfer power, but conduct in 2022 did not substantiate that claim.

The strongest favorable evidence is the passage of the first law implementing the constitutional requirement for an EC-appointment process and the survival of some local electoral competition, including many independent victories over AL nominees (WEB-4B-003, WEB-4B-004). Those facts are limited by government dominance of appointments, the BNP boycott, and uncontested local seats.

### 4B.2 — Manipulation of electoral and state machinery

Strong adverse evidence.

The new EC law was a formal improvement over having no statute, but it was passed rapidly under an overwhelming AL majority; opposition requests for wider consultation and parliamentary scrutiny were rejected. The president generally acts on prime-ministerial advice, and most search-committee positions were filled by state officeholders or presidential nominees (WEB-4B-003). BTI and Freedom House described pre-existing partisan capture of election administration, bureaucracy, law enforcement, and parliament (WEB-4B-001, WEB-4B-002).

Police arrests, rally restrictions, ruling-party impunity, and connectivity interference ahead of opposition events indicate use of state machinery to disadvantage challengers (WEB-4B-005, WEB-4B-006, WEB-4B-010).

### 4B.3 — Tolerance of opposition, criticism, protest and monitoring

Strong adverse evidence.

Opposition protests continued, showing that civic space was constrained rather than completely extinguished. Nevertheless, police and AL supporters were implicated in repeated clashes; police reportedly arrested thousands of BNP members, used large “unknown accused” cases, raided homes, arrested senior BNP leaders, and failed to protect opposition gatherings from ruling-party attacks (WEB-4B-005, WEB-4B-006).

Odhikar lost registration after reporting security-force abuses, with its appeal rejected by the PMO (WEB-4B-011). Journalists and online critics faced DSA proceedings and self-censorship (WEB-4B-008, WEB-4B-009, WEB-4B-016).

### 4B.4 — Independent institutions

Strong adverse evidence; no consequential 2022 strengthening found.

BTI and the State Department described weak parliamentary oversight, politicized lower courts, executive influence, vulnerable judicial appointments, an ineffective ACC, and a questioned NHRC (WEB-4B-001, WEB-4B-002, WEB-4B-012). The EC law created a formal statutory process, but its structure and enactment did not establish an independent counterweight to the executive (WEB-4B-003).

One isolated favorable judicial action—the High Court ordering the release of a man left imprisoned years after acquittal—shows that remedies were not impossible. It does not establish ruler-attributable strengthening of judicial independence.

### 4B.5 — Politicization, intimidation and party capture

Strong adverse evidence.

BTI found the bureaucracy functioned in practice like a ruling-party wing and judicial recruitment and promotion were influenced by political affiliation (WEB-4B-001, WEB-4B-002). AL and Chhatra League actors allegedly attacked opposition events with impunity while police pursued opposition members (WEB-4B-005, WEB-4B-006).

Awarding national police medals to two RAB officials subjected to U.S. human-rights sanctions is direct Hasina-attributed evidence of rewarding rather than distancing the government from contested security-force conduct (WEB-4B-015).

Evidence specifically establishing arbitrary loyalty oaths or a quantitatively defined personal cult during 2022 was not found. DSA protection for the “father of the nation,” Hasina’s father Sheikh Mujibur Rahman, is relevant but inherited.

### 4B.6 — Media and information access

Strong adverse evidence.

OHCHR found multiple DSA provisions vague, overbroad and capable of criminalizing legitimate expression, with harsh penalties, warrantless arrest powers, and executive rather than independent control over blocking (WEB-4B-007). The government continued arrests and declined to suspend or materially amend the law in 2022 (WEB-4B-008).

Journalists faced DSA investigations, arrest, attacks, advertising pressure, censorship, blocked sites and self-censorship (WEB-4B-009, WEB-4B-016). The official position—that the DSA was needed for cybercrime, defamation and digital safety, and was not aimed at truthful criticism—is important contrary evidence but is weakened by application patterns and the absence of completed safeguards.

### 4B.7 — Political equality and inclusion

Mixed formal framework, materially adverse practical evidence.

No law categorically excluded women or minorities from political participation, women led both principal parties, and 50 parliamentary seats were reserved for women. Religious attacks reportedly declined in 2022, and the government responded promptly to some minority violence (WEB-4B-013, WEB-4B-017).

However, reserved-seat members were indirectly selected according to party strength; parties missed their 33 percent internal-committee target; women, LGBTQI+ people, religious and ethnic minorities remained underrepresented; and Indigenous and minority groups reported violence, land loss and weak remedies (WEB-4B-013). Rohingya restrictions concern civic equality but are complicated by refugee status and the Myanmar displacement emergency.

### 4B.8 — Term limits, succession and transfer rules

Partly unanswered for the target year.

Bangladesh imposed no prime-ministerial term limit, so Hasina did not violate a formal term limit during 2022. There was no transfer or succession event in the year. Her statements favored peaceful transfer, but the inherited compromised election system and refusal to accept a neutral caretaker arrangement materially weakened the credibility of future transfer (WEB-4B-002, WEB-4B-003, WEB-4B-005).

Later events should not be silently imported into this window. The 2024 election and Hasina’s 2024 removal may be valuable retrospective evidence for a later project, but are not necessary to establish the 2022 pattern and are excluded from the machine records.

### 4B.9 — Surveillance, digital controls and administrative harassment

Strong adverse evidence.

Authorities allegedly monitored private communications, used informers, blocked critical sites, throttled mobile internet around at least six or seven BNP rallies, and advanced draft data and platform rules that raised surveillance and censorship concerns (WEB-4B-007, WEB-4B-010, WEB-4B-016).

The Odhikar decision, foreign-funding monitoring, mass “unknown accused” cases, arrests of dissidents’ relatives, and lists of alleged “anti-state” expatriates demonstrate administrative harassment beyond ordinary law enforcement (WEB-4B-005, WEB-4B-011).

The BTRC and telecom ministry denied ordering shutdowns and cited network congestion. An email reportedly seen by The Daily Star directing operators to shut 3G/4G service in Barishal materially strengthens the contrary account, but the complete order was not independently obtained (WEB-4B-010).

### 4B.10 — Trajectory

Strong evidence of persistence or further erosion, with one narrow improvement.

Hasina inherited from her own preceding terms an already damaged competitive system. During 2022, she did not restore meaningful legislative, electoral, judicial, media, or civil-society checks. Instead, opposition repression intensified as the election cycle approached; digital restrictions and DSA cases continued; and a leading monitor lost registration.

Reported extrajudicial killings and disappearances declined sharply following U.S. sanctions (WEB-4B-014). This was an important favorable outcome, but the timing, the government’s denial of the underlying allegations, medals for sanctioned commanders, and retaliation against monitors indicate externally induced tactical restraint rather than durable democratic strengthening (WEB-4B-011, WEB-4B-015).

Overall, the evidence supports a weaker or at best persistently weak political-freedom environment by the end of 2022.

## 6. Remaining research questions and saturation

The core regime pattern is saturated: further English-language searching is likely to repeat the same opposition, DSA, shutdown, institutional-capture and civil-society evidence.

Material additional information remains possible in four areas:

- The EC’s complete roadmap and appointment records could sharpen whether the 2022 law produced any operational independence.
- Bengali parliamentary and judicial records could uncover favorable instances where Hasina accepted an institutional constraint.
- Complete local-election data could distinguish genuine opposition/independent victories from AL-faction competition and quantify violence or uncontested seats.
- Direct BTRC orders could resolve the shutdown attribution dispute.

Lens 4B.8 remains structurally weak because no transfer occurred and no formal prime-ministerial term limit existed. Evidence specifically about satire, personality cult practices, local-government autonomy, audit-body independence, and coalition commitments is also thinner than the core electoral, opposition, media and digital-control evidence.

More research could improve precision and contrary-evidence balance, but is unlikely to reverse the central conclusion that political freedom and democratic resilience were not strengthened in 2022.
