# Document-Level Evidence Paper  
## Andrés Manuel López Obrador (AMLO), Mexico, 2022 — Chapter 5B  
### Source: INEGI, *Encuesta Nacional de Ocupación y Empleo, Nueva Edición (ENOEN), Fourth Quarter 2022 Technical Note*

## 1. Source character, scope, and evidentiary limits

This document is an official statistical release from Mexico’s National Institute of Statistics and Geography (INEGI). It reports labor-market conditions in October–December 2022 and, principally, compares them with October–December 2021. It provides national, sex-disaggregated, state, locality-size, and metropolitan estimates. Its strongest contribution to Chapter 5B is therefore evidence about **economic outcomes and their distribution during AMLO’s fourth year in office**, especially employment recovery, labor-market participation, unemployment, underemployment, informality, employment protections, earnings-related job quality, and regional disparities.

The source does **not** identify which AMLO policies or budgets caused these outcomes. It does not examine legislative intent, procurement, corruption enforcement, economic appointments, fiscal or monetary rules, individual infrastructure or social-sector projects, or the transparency of specific executive programs. It consequently provides outcome evidence relevant mainly to Questions 5B.1, 5B.4, 5B.6, 5B.7, 5B.8, 5B.9, and 5B.10, but cannot by itself establish presidential credit or responsibility. It offers little or no direct evidence for Questions 5B.2, 5B.3, and 5B.5.

INEGI says the release uses a new population estimate and that historical microdata and tables had been updated from the first quarter of 2014 onward. The ENOEN employed a probabilistic, two-stage, stratified cluster sample of private dwellings, with households revisited quarterly for up to five visits. One-fifth of the sample was new at any time, while four-fifths had been interviewed three months earlier. Expansion factors accounted for selection probabilities, socioeconomic strata, and geographic domains. The survey was designed to represent Mexico nationally, all 32 states, four locality-size classes, and specified urban areas. INEGI states that its concepts follow OECD recommendations within the International Labour Organization framework and the Delhi Group’s recommendations on informal employment. [pp. 21–23]

There are two consequential comparability qualifications:

1. From the third quarter of 2020 through the second quarter of 2022, ENOEN used a pandemic-era supplementary rule that placed certain people temporarily absent from an activity and people needing or wanting work in the “available” component of the non-economically active population. Beginning in the third quarter of 2022, INEGI discontinued that rule and returned to its pre-pandemic classification. This affects interpretation of the large 2021–2022 decline in the “available” non-economically active population. [pp. 3, 22–23]
2. Measures expressed in ranges of minimum wages are mechanically sensitive to changes in the nominal minimum wage. For time comparisons, INEGI uses or recommends minimum-wage equivalents adjusted for consumer-price changes. Its comparable “critical employment conditions” series in this release uses a January 2022 base. [pp. 9, 15–16, 18, 20]

The release distinguishes raw annual comparisons from seasonally adjusted quarter-to-quarter movement. INEGI seasonally adjusts relevant series using X-13ARIMA-SEATS. [pp. 10–11, 14, 23]

## 2. Labor-force expansion and employment growth

### 2.1 More people participated, and employment rose faster than the working-age population

In the fourth quarter of 2022, Mexico had 99.516 million people aged 15 or older, up 1.031 million from a year earlier. The economically active population—people employed or actively unemployed—rose more rapidly, by 1.384 million, from 58.762 million to 60.145 million. The participation rate increased from 59.7% to 60.4%. Employment rose by 1.738 million, from 56.611 million to 58.349 million, while measured unemployment fell by 354,479, from 2.151 million to 1.796 million. Consequently, employment represented 97.0% of the active labor force, compared with 96.3% a year earlier. [pp. 1–3, table 1]

These are observed survey estimates, not INEGI claims about government causation. They show that by late 2022 Mexico’s labor market was absorbing both growth in the working-age population and a larger number of labor-market participants. For Questions 5B.1, 5B.9, and 5B.10, this is favorable outcome evidence consistent with continuing recovery from the pandemic disruption. It does not reveal whether recovery came from AMLO’s policies, autonomous reopening, external demand, remittances, U.S. economic conditions, demographic change, or other forces.

### 2.2 The expansion was disproportionately female

The male economically active population increased by only 189,950, from 35.556 million to 35.746 million; male participation edged from 76.4% to 76.5%. The female economically active population increased by 1.194 million, from 23.206 million to 24.400 million; female participation rose from 44.7% to 46.2%. Female employment increased by 1.332 million, compared with a 406,280 increase among men. Female unemployment declined by 138,149 and male unemployment by 216,330. [pp. 2–3, table 1]

Thus, women accounted for approximately 86% of the annual increase in the active labor force and roughly 77% of the increase in employment. This is material to Question 5B.8 because late-2022 labor-market gains were not confined to men. Nevertheless, the gender participation gap remained very large: 76.5% for men against 46.2% for women, a difference of 30.3 percentage points. The document records improved female participation without showing equality of access or identifying which policies contributed.

### 2.3 Nonparticipation fell, but the headline movement is partly affected by a classification change

The non-economically active population fell by 352,220, from 39.723 million to 39.370 million, reducing its share of the population aged 15 or older from 40.3% to 39.6%. Women still constituted 28.362 million of the non-active population, compared with 11.009 million men. Female nonparticipation declined by 376,699; male nonparticipation increased by 24,479. [p. 3, table 1]

Among the non-active population, 5.389 million said they had not sought employment but would accept work if offered. This “available” group fell by 2.071 million year on year, from 7.460 million to 5.389 million. A further 33.982 million were classified as unavailable because of other obligations or circumstances such as physical impediments and family duties; that group increased by 1.719 million. [pp. 3–4]

The apparent contraction of labor-market availability should not be interpreted entirely as an economic improvement. INEGI explicitly reports that in the third quarter of 2022 it returned to pre-pandemic classification criteria. Until the second quarter, temporarily absent workers and some people needing or wanting work had been included among those “available” for work; thereafter, the supplementary pandemic rule ceased. [pp. 3, 22–23] The annual decline in availability therefore mixes behavioral or economic change with a definitional change. This qualification is important to Questions 5B.7, 5B.9, and 5B.10: the source itself discloses a break that limits an unqualified recovery claim.

## 3. Unemployment, underemployment, and labor-market pressure

### 3.1 Open unemployment declined substantially

In October–December 2022, 1.796 million people were unemployed—meaning that they had worked no hours in the reference week, were available to work, and had undertaken an employment-search activity. The unemployment rate was 3.0% of the active labor force, down from 3.7% a year earlier. Male unemployment was 2.9%, down 0.6 percentage points; female unemployment was 3.1%, down 0.8 points. [pp. 12–13]

Seasonally adjusted unemployment was 3.1% and decreased by 0.1 percentage points from the third quarter of 2022. The seasonally adjusted male rate fell 0.1 points and the female rate 0.2 points. [p. 14] The annual improvement was therefore accompanied by a smaller favorable movement in the latest quarter, rather than being solely a base-effect comparison.

The broader “general pressure rate,” which adds employed people seeking another job to the unemployed, fell from 7.2% to 5.9%. The rate combining unemployment and employment of fewer than 15 hours per week declined from 9.8% to 9.0%. [p. 15, table 8] These complementary measures reinforce the finding that labor-market pressure eased, while also showing that the 3.0% open-unemployment rate did not capture all unmet demand for work.

### 3.2 Long-duration unemployment also fell, although younger people remained heavily represented

People unemployed for more than three months declined sharply. Those unemployed for more than three and up to six months fell by 66,778, to 147,840; those unemployed for more than six months and up to one year fell by 76,130, to 90,380; and those unemployed for more than one year fell by 54,957, to 35,619. In total, the document describes a 198,000 decline among those unemployed for more than three months. Meanwhile, 42.5% of the unemployed had searched for up to one month and 34.7% for more than one and up to three months. [pp. 13–14, table 7]

The age composition was less favorable to young people. Those aged 15–24 constituted 34.2% of all unemployed people, up from 33.1%, even though their absolute number declined from 712,860 to 613,470. People aged 25–44 constituted 45.6%, up from 44.0%, and numbered 819,228. [pp. 13–14] The fall in unemployment was broad enough to reduce absolute unemployment in every listed age group, but younger workers remained disproportionately represented in the remaining unemployment pool.

### 3.3 Underemployment fell markedly

INEGI defines underemployment as employed people who needed and were available to work more hours, expressed through seeking supplementary work or a job with longer hours. The number underemployed fell from about 6.0 million in the fourth quarter of 2021 to 4.4 million in the fourth quarter of 2022—a reduction of approximately 1.7 million. The rate fell from 10.6% to 7.5% of employment. Male underemployment declined from 3.7 million to 2.6 million and female underemployment from 2.3 million to 1.7 million; the corresponding 2022 rates were 7.6% and 7.2%. [p. 10]

After seasonal adjustment, the underemployment rate declined by 0.1 percentage points from the third quarter. [p. 10] This is favorable evidence for Questions 5B.8–5B.10: the recovery involved not merely additional nominal employment but fewer employed people reporting an unmet need for additional hours. Yet 4.4 million underemployed people remained, and the national figure concealed severe local concentrations: state rates reached 14.5% in Oaxaca, 13.5% in Tlaxcala, 12.7% in Tabasco, 12.3% in Campeche, 12.2% in Zacatecas, and 12.1% in Tamaulipas. [p. 18, table 9]

## 4. Composition of employment and indications of productive breadth

### 4.1 Employment growth was spread across sectors

Of the 58.349 million employed people in late 2022, 6.870 million worked in agriculture and related primary activities, 14.523 million in industry, and 36.593 million in services. Compared with a year earlier:

- primary employment rose by 63,389;
- industrial employment rose by 414,533;
- service employment rose by 1.253 million. [pp. 6, 8, table 3]

Within industry, manufacturing employment grew by 260,490 to 9.588 million, construction by 91,633 to 4.476 million, and extraction and electricity by 62,410 to 458,910. Within services, commerce added 278,779 workers; restaurants and accommodation, 216,895; professional, financial, and corporate services, 192,346; transport, communications, postal services, and storage, 137,128; social services, 107,521; miscellaneous services, 201,397; and government and international organizations, 118,693. [pp. 6, 8]

This spread matters to Questions 5B.1, 5B.4, and 5B.10. Employment gains did not consist solely of direct government hiring or a single patronage-prone sector: manufacturing, construction, commerce, hospitality, professional and financial services, transport, and social services all expanded. Government and international-organization employment accounted for about 119,000 of the 1.738 million total employment increase. The evidence is consistent with a broad reopening and expansion, but the release does not attribute sectoral changes to AMLO’s regulation, investment policy, trade policy, public spending, or any named program.

### 4.2 Paid subordinate employment accounted for most of the increase

Paid subordinate workers increased by 1.414 million, from 38.361 million to 39.775 million, and their share of employment rose from 67.8% to 68.2%. Self-employment increased by 150,674 to 13.160 million but declined as a share from 23.0% to 22.6%. Employers increased by 105,228 to 2.970 million, while unpaid workers increased by 68,031 to 2.444 million. [pp. 6, 8]

The paid-employment increase was highly concentrated among women: male paid subordinate employment rose by approximately 420,000, while female paid subordinate employment rose by approximately 995,000. Women’s self-employment rose by 242,000, whereas male self-employment fell by 91,000. [p. 6]

For Questions 5B.4 and 5B.8, these figures indicate that the labor-market expansion was not merely a rise in own-account survival activity: most of the increase was paid subordinate work. Contrary evidence remains in the continued scale of self-employment and unpaid work—together 15.604 million people—and in the weak employment protections described below.

### 4.3 Employment rose across business sizes, not just in microbusinesses

Among 48.662 million non-agricultural workers, 23.437 million worked in microbusinesses, 8.851 million in small establishments, 5.844 million in medium establishments, 5.506 million in large establishments, and 2.335 million in government. From late 2021 to late 2022, employment increased by:

- 198,032 in microbusinesses;
- 435,319 in small establishments;
- 467,672 in medium establishments;
- 364,519 in large establishments;
- 118,693 in government. [pp. 7–8, table 3]

Medium establishments recorded the largest absolute increase, followed by small and large establishments. Microbusinesses’ share of non-agricultural employment declined from 49.5% to 48.2%, while the shares of small, medium, and large establishments rose modestly. [p. 8]

This pattern is relevant to Question 5B.4 because job creation extended across established firms and business sizes, and was not confined to unregistered household enterprises. It still does not prove that government consistently enforced fair competition, property rights, or predictable regulation. ENOEN measures where people worked, not the legal or competitive environment that produced those jobs.

### 4.4 Hours became fuller, but long working weeks remained common

Employment between 35 and 48 hours per week increased by 1.623 million to 27.657 million and rose from 46.0% to 47.4% of employment. Employment of 15–34 hours fell by 104,962, while employment under 15 hours fell slightly. The average employed person worked 43 hours per week. [pp. 7–8]

At the same time, 15.757 million people—27.0% of all employed people—worked more than 48 hours per week. This included 32.4% of employed men and 19.0% of employed women. [pp. 7–8] Therefore, an increase in fuller working schedules coexisted with a large population working very long hours. The evidence cannot determine whether these hours reflected desired labor supply, strong demand, low hourly productivity, or economic necessity.

## 5. Job quality, employment protection, and informality

### 5.1 A majority of paid subordinate workers lacked at least one basic protection

Among the 39.775 million paid subordinate workers:

- 22.651 million, or 56.9%, had access to a health institution as an employment benefit, an increase of approximately 1.1 million from 2021;
- 16.720 million lacked such access;
- 26.095 million had labor benefits other than the separately counted health-access measure;
- 13.234 million lacked those benefits;
- 22.863 million had a written contract, 1.5 million more than a year earlier;
- 16.302 million had no written contract. [p. 9, table 4]

Of those with written contracts, 19.158 million had permanent or indefinite contracts and 3.505 million had temporary contracts. [p. 9]

This mixed record is central to Questions 5B.4, 5B.6, 5B.8, and 5B.10. Absolute access to health coverage and written contracts expanded, offering favorable evidence of improved formal job attachment. Yet roughly two in five paid subordinate workers lacked job-based health access, and approximately 16.3 million lacked a written contract. The document does not say whether enforcement improved, whether inspections produced the changes, or whether employers complied voluntarily as the economy normalized.

### 5.2 Most paid subordinate workers were concentrated at low multiples of the minimum wage

Among paid subordinate workers, INEGI recorded:

- 11.094 million earning up to one minimum wage;
- 16.003 million earning more than one and up to two;
- 4.708 million earning more than two and up to three;
- 1.876 million earning more than three and up to five;
- 594,483 earning more than five;
- 5.500 million with income unspecified. [p. 9, table 4]

Among workers with specified income, the first two categories dominated. These nominal categories cannot be used straightforwardly to infer real-income deterioration or improvement because AMLO’s government increased the minimum wage and because inflation changed its purchasing power. INEGI expressly cautions that time comparisons based on minimum-wage ranges require consumer-price-adjusted equivalent minimum wages. [p. 9] Accordingly, the distribution demonstrates low-wage concentration in late 2022 but does not by itself show whether real earnings improved under AMLO.

### 5.3 Informality remained the dominant structural weakness

INEGI’s broad informal-employment measure includes workers in informal-sector household businesses, unprotected agricultural and domestic work, and workers without social security even when employed by formal businesses, government, or institutions. In the fourth quarter of 2022, 32.157 million people were informally employed—55.1% of all employment. This was 548,021 more people than a year earlier, although the rate declined from 55.8% to 55.1%. Seasonally adjusted, the broad informality rate fell by 0.4 percentage points from the previous quarter. [pp. 10–11]

The components were:

- 16.402 million in the informal sector itself, 28.1% of employment and 80,661 more people than in 2021;
- 7.605 million informally employed in businesses, government, and institutions, an increase of 376,701;
- 5.887 million in unprotected agricultural work, a decline of 56,753;
- 2.263 million in unprotected paid domestic work, an increase of 147,412. [p. 11, table 5]

This supports two conclusions that must be held together. First, formal employment grew faster than informal employment, causing a 0.7-point decline in the informal share. Second, the absolute number of informal workers still increased by more than half a million, and a majority of all workers remained informal. For Questions 5B.4, 5B.6, 5B.8, and 5B.10, the labor market thus showed incremental formalization but no resolution of its central enforcement and social-protection deficit.

### 5.4 Informality shifted differently for men and women

Male informal employment fell by 188,405, from 19.068 million to 18.880 million, and its rate declined from 55.6% to 54.4%. Female informal employment rose by 736,426, from 12.541 million to 13.277 million; the female rate declined only marginally, from 56.2% to 56.1%, because female employment also expanded rapidly. [p. 11]

Within female informal employment, informal-sector work rose by 248,766; unprotected domestic work by 159,629; informal work in businesses, government, and institutions by 294,413; and informal agricultural work by 33,618. Among men, informal-sector and agricultural informal work declined, while informal work within businesses, government, and institutions increased by 82,288. [p. 11, table 5]

This is material contrary evidence to an unqualified claim of gender-equitable improvement. Women obtained most of the new jobs and improved their participation rate, but virtually all of the net national increase in informal employment was female. The female informality rate remained higher than the male rate, 56.1% against 54.4%. The data do not establish whether the new informal female jobs were transitional gains from nonparticipation, failures of enforcement, or both.

### 5.5 “Critical employment conditions” worsened slightly

INEGI defines critical employment conditions to include people involuntarily working fewer than 35 hours, people working more than 35 hours while earning less than one monthly minimum wage, and people working more than 48 hours while earning up to two minimum wages. Using equivalent minimum wages with January 2022 as the base, the rate was 30.4% in late 2022, up from 30.2% a year earlier. Male critical conditions increased from 32.3% to 32.6%; female conditions increased from 26.9% to 27.2%. [pp. 15–16]

Table 9 reports a national state-comparison value of 29.1%, while the accompanying time-comparable discussion and table 8 give 30.4%. The release explains that indicators involving wage ranges are sensitive to nominal minimum-wage changes and that equivalized wages are needed for time comparison. [pp. 15–16, 18] The 30.4% equivalized figure is therefore the appropriate annual comparison presented by INEGI; the differing table value should not be silently merged with it.

The slight deterioration in this broader quality measure qualifies favorable unemployment and underemployment trends. By late 2022, more people were working and fewer reported wanting extra hours, but approximately three in ten workers remained in combinations of involuntarily short hours, long hours, or low earnings that INEGI classified as critical. This is directly relevant to Questions 5B.8 and 5B.10.

## 6. Distribution across sex, geography, and types of locality

### 6.1 Female gains were substantial but did not erase unequal access or job quality

The strongest favorable distributional evidence is the increase of 1.332 million in female employment and the 1.6-point rise in female participation. Female unemployment fell to 3.1%, underemployment to 7.2%, and employment in 35–48-hour schedules increased by 927,000. [pp. 3, 7, 10, 12, 15]

The qualifications are equally consequential:

- female participation remained 30.3 percentage points below male participation;
- 28.362 million women were outside the active labor force, compared with 11.009 million men;
- female informal employment increased by 736,426;
- 56.1% of employed women remained informal;
- women made up most protected paid-employment growth but also most informal-employment growth. [pp. 3, 6, 11, 15]

Thus, the source supports an interpretation of rapid female re-entry or entry into employment accompanied by continuing structural inequality, not a simple finding of either inclusion or exclusion.

### 6.2 Low unemployment in poorer states did not necessarily mean good labor-market performance

Measured unemployment varied substantially by place. It was 4.5% in Mexico City and the State of Mexico, 4.2% in Tabasco, and 3.8% in Aguascalientes, but only 0.8% in Guerrero, 1.3% in Oaxaca, and 1.7% in Michoacán. [pp. 17–18]

INEGI explicitly warns against reading these low rates as proof of superior labor markets. Its interpretation is that state indicators cover different dimensions: organized labor markets in which people actively seek jobs, as well as less-developed markets in which people remain in precarious, unprotected work. It recommends considering participation and job conditions alongside unemployment. [p. 17]

That warning is borne out by the distributions:

- Oaxaca combined 1.3% unemployment with 81.2% informality;
- Guerrero combined 0.8% unemployment with 79.0% informality;
- Chiapas combined 2.3% unemployment with 75.2% informality and a 48.1% critical-conditions rate;
- Puebla had 71.3% informality and 36.5% critical conditions;
- Tlaxcala had 69.8% informality and 41.6% critical conditions;
- Veracruz had 67.3% informality and 36.3% critical conditions. [pp. 17–18, table 9]

By contrast:

- Coahuila had 34.2% informality;
- Nuevo León, 35.8%;
- Baja California, 36.6%;
- Chihuahua, 36.8%;
- Jalisco had a 15.1% critical-conditions rate;
- Nuevo León and Baja California Sur, 16.9%;
- Sinaloa, 16.8%. [pp. 17–18]

For Question 5B.8, this is strong evidence that the actual incidence of labor-market gains remained geographically unequal. For Question 5B.10, it shows why a national unemployment rate alone would materially overstate the quality and durability of the trajectory.

### 6.3 Employment was geographically broad, but regional labor-market structures remained sharply divided

Almost half of employed people—28.866 million or 49.5%—lived in localities of at least 100,000 inhabitants or state capitals. Another 11.918 million, or 20.4%, lived in rural localities below 2,500 inhabitants; 9.000 million lived in settlements of 15,000–99,999 and 8.566 million in settlements of 2,500–14,999. [p. 5, table 2]

Open unemployment declined in every locality-size class from 2021 to 2022:

- major urban areas: from 4.5% to 3.6%;
- medium urban areas: from 3.4% to 2.9%;
- small urban areas: from 3.0% to 2.7%;
- rural areas: from 2.3% to 1.7%. [p. 12, table 6]

But the source explains that unemployment is conditioned on active job search and is therefore normally higher in larger, more organized labor markets. [p. 12] Low rural unemployment should not be interpreted as necessarily better rural prosperity; it may coexist with informal or precarious work and fewer realistic search options.

### 6.4 Metropolitan detail exposes severe local disparities hidden by national averages

Across 39 covered cities, participation was 61.6%, unemployment 3.7%, underemployment 7.2%, critical employment conditions 26.2%, and informality 43.5%. [p. 19] The urban aggregate was less informal than Mexico overall, but internal differences were large.

Examples include:

- Ciudad del Carmen: 23.9% underemployment;
- Coatzacoalcos: 14.9% underemployment;
- Tapachula: 13.9% underemployment and 43.5% critical conditions;
- Tlaxcala: 13.4% underemployment, 40.2% critical conditions, and 66.8% informality;
- Reynosa: 44.5% critical conditions despite only 1.8% unemployment;
- Acapulco: 1.6% unemployment but 36.7% critical conditions and 59.9% informality;
- Guadalajara: 10.4% critical conditions and 39.8% informality;
- Monterrey: 13.8% critical conditions and 33.7% informality;
- Saltillo: 26.1% informality;
- Ciudad Juárez: 27.6% informality;
- Chihuahua: 28.5% informality. [pp. 19–20, table 10]

These comparisons reinforce INEGI’s warning: a low unemployment rate can coexist with weak earnings, excessive hours, or lack of protection. They also show that prosperity and job quality were not evenly distributed across regions under the same national administration.

## 7. Evidence about statistical publication and evaluability

The release itself is evidence that INEGI continued to publish detailed labor-market information during AMLO’s presidency. It reports national totals and complementary measures beyond unemployment, including labor-force participation, underemployment, labor-market pressure, informality, hours, benefits, contracts, earnings brackets, sex differences, state results, and metropolitan results. It also publishes definitions and methodological cautions that prevent misleading interpretation. [pp. 14–23]

The document discloses:

- the change from mixed telephone/face-to-face collection to exclusively face-to-face interviewing from the third quarter of 2022;
- the end of the emergency ENOEN program after the fourth quarter and resumption of regular ENOE monthly and quarterly releases beginning with January 2023;
- the new population estimates and historical updates from 2014;
- the pandemic-era classification rule and its discontinuation;
- sampling design and panel rotation;
- use of expansion factors;
- geographic domains;
- international conceptual frameworks;
- seasonal adjustment procedures;
- the sensitivity of wage-based indicators to minimum-wage changes;
- access to microdata and tables online. [pp. 9, 15, 18, 20–23]

For Question 5B.7, this is concrete evidence of official publication practices that permit outside analysis and reveal weaknesses as well as improvements. The note does not hide the 55.1% informality rate, the slight increase in critical employment conditions, gender differences, or extreme state disparities.

However, this document alone cannot establish that AMLO personally respected INEGI’s independence, that the underlying data were free from executive interference, that independent auditors validated the figures, or that his government corrected policies in response to them. The observed fact is publication of a technically documented and disaggregated official survey. Any conclusion about presidential tolerance, institutional independence, or policy correction requires separate evidence on INEGI’s governance, budgets, executive interactions, and policy response.

## 8. Question-by-question decision relevance

### 5B.1 — Broad-based sustainable prosperity versus rents or short-term popularity

The source shows broad employment gains across manufacturing, construction, commerce, hospitality, professional and financial services, transport, social services, establishments of multiple sizes, and paid subordinate employment. Government and international-organization employment rose by about 119,000, a small portion of the 1.738 million total increase. [pp. 6–8] This outcome pattern is more consistent with an economy-wide recovery than with employment gains generated only through government payrolls or a single loyalty-distribution channel.

The paper cannot establish the intent or content of AMLO’s legislative agenda, policies, or executed budgets, nor whether growth was sustainable. The continued 55.1% informality rate, slight increase in equivalized critical employment conditions, and extreme regional inequalities are substantial contrary evidence to any claim that broad-based prosperity had already been achieved. [pp. 11, 15–18]

### 5B.2 — Economic appointments and professional independence

No direct evidence. INEGI’s technical production demonstrates institutional statistical capacity, but the document does not discuss appointments, selection procedures, tenure, dismissal, or operational independence of economic officials.

### 5B.3 — Fiscal, tax, debt, monetary, and financial rules

No direct evidence. Labor-market improvements may be compatible with macroeconomic stability, but the note contains no fiscal balances, debt, inflation outcomes, interest-rate decisions, financial regulation, or evidence of compliance with formal rules.

### 5B.4 — Competition, entrepreneurship, trade, investment, and job creation

The source documents 1.738 million additional employed people, including increases in manufacturing, construction, professional and financial services, employers, self-employed workers, and small, medium, and large establishments. [pp. 6–8] These are relevant observed outcomes for job creation and business activity.

It does not identify the regulations, enforcement practices, property protections, trade measures, or investment decisions producing them. Persistent informality—32.157 million workers—and 16.302 million paid subordinate workers without written contracts indicate that legal formality and labor-rule coverage remained weak. [pp. 9–11]

### 5B.5 — Competition, procurement, disclosure, and anti-corruption enforcement

No direct evidence. Informal employment inside businesses, government, and institutions rose by 376,701 to 7.605 million, which reveals unprotected employment even within registered or public-sector settings but does not identify corruption, political connections, procurement favoritism, audit obstruction, or remedies. [p. 11]

### 5B.6 — Budget delivery of infrastructure, education, health, technology, and administrative capacity

The release records employment changes in potentially relevant sectors: construction rose by 91,633, social services by 107,521, transport/communications/postal/storage by 137,128, and extraction/electricity by 62,410. Government and international-organization employment rose by 118,693. [p. 8] These are labor inputs or sectoral outcomes, not proof that budgets delivered timely or high-quality public goods.

The survey itself shows continuing statistical administrative capacity. It does not evaluate roads, schools, hospitals, technology systems, project completion, cost, service quality, or patronage.

### 5B.7 — Reliable information, independent evaluation, and correction after failure

INEGI published detailed, disaggregated, and methodologically qualified information, including unfavorable indicators. It disclosed collection-mode changes, revised population estimation, historical-series updating, classification changes, seasonal-adjustment methods, and wage-comparison problems. [pp. 21–23] That makes the data more usable for independent evaluation and reduces the risk of false comparisons.

The source does not show how AMLO responded to the evidence, whether programs or implementers were changed, or whether the executive protected INEGI from interference.

### 5B.8 — Fair incidence across classes, regions, genders, and groups

The most important favorable evidence is the strong increase in female employment and participation, the fall in unemployment and underemployment for both sexes, and growth in paid subordinate work. [pp. 3, 6, 10, 12, 15]

The principal adverse or qualifying evidence is:

- a 30.3-point male–female participation gap;
- female informal employment rising by 736,426;
- 55.1% overall informality;
- 16.720 million paid workers without job-based health access;
- 16.302 million paid workers without written contracts;
- heavy concentration in earnings of up to two minimum wages;
- 30.4% in equivalized critical employment conditions;
- state informality ranging from 34.2% in Coahuila to 81.2% in Oaxaca;
- critical conditions ranging from 15.1% in Jalisco to 48.1% in Chiapas. [pp. 3, 9, 11, 15–18]

The source measures actual incidence but does not connect it to particular tax, wage, benefit, investment, or regional policies.

### 5B.9 — Response to inflation, unemployment, or other shocks

The 2021–2022 decline in unemployment and underemployment, increase in participation, and rise in employment provide outcome evidence of continued labor-market normalization after the pandemic shock. The return to face-to-face interviewing and the discontinuation of an emergency classification rule also mark the transition out of pandemic statistical arrangements. [pp. 10, 12, 21–23]

The source does not identify government relief measures, their funding, targeting, timing, monitoring, or correction. It also does not assess inflation policy. The slight increase in price-adjusted critical employment conditions suggests that stronger employment quantities did not translate uniformly into adequate real earnings and hours. [pp. 15–16]

### 5B.10 — A stronger and more durable economic trajectory than inherited

Relative to late 2021, late 2022 had higher participation, 1.738 million more jobs, lower unemployment, lower underemployment, fewer long-duration unemployed people, and growth across sectors and establishment sizes. [pp. 3, 6–8, 10, 12–15] These facts indicate a stronger immediate labor-market trajectory.

Durability remains uncertain. More than half of employment was informal; the absolute number of informal workers increased; approximately three in ten workers met the equivalized critical-conditions definition; large shares lacked health access or written contracts; and regional gaps were extreme. [pp. 9–11, 15–20] Moreover, the source compares only late 2022 with late 2021. It does not compare 2022 with AMLO’s 2018 inheritance, separate pandemic losses and reopening effects, measure productivity or real earnings, or identify causal policy contributions. It can support a finding of near-term labor-market improvement, but not by itself a finding that AMLO left a stronger, durable trajectory than he inherited.

## 9. Integrated evidentiary assessment without scoring

By the fourth quarter of 2022, Mexico’s labor market had improved appreciably in quantity relative to a year earlier. Participation rose, employment grew by 1.738 million, unemployment fell from 3.7% to 3.0%, underemployment fell from 10.6% to 7.5%, and long-duration unemployment contracted. Employment growth extended across sectors and business sizes and was dominated by paid subordinate work rather than government employment or own-account work alone. Women accounted for most of the growth in participation and employment.

The improvement was materially incomplete in quality and distribution. Broad informality remained at 55.1%, or 32.157 million people; its rate declined, but its headcount increased. Approximately 16.7 million paid subordinate workers lacked job-based health access and 16.3 million lacked a written contract. The price-comparable critical-conditions rate increased slightly to 30.4%. Women experienced rapid employment gains but also accounted for the net rise in informal work. Poorer southern states combined very low open unemployment with informality rates of roughly 67–81% and, in several cases, high rates of critically low-paid or excessive-hours employment. These patterns caution against treating the low national unemployment rate as sufficient evidence of broad or sustainable prosperity.

The document also demonstrates that an official Mexican statistical institution continued to release detailed, methodologically transparent evidence capable of contradicting a simplistic success narrative. It openly reports weak job quality, regional inequality, and methodological breaks. But it does not establish AMLO’s causal responsibility for either improvements or shortcomings, the quality of his economic appointments and institutional choices, or whether his government used this evidence to revise policy. Those questions require corroborating policy, budget, institutional, and causal evidence.

Población subocupada
Otra forma de caracterizar a la población ocupada es en función de su condición de
subocupación. Esta se entiende como la necesidad de trabajar más tiempo, lo que se
traduce en la búsqueda de una ocupación complementaria o de un nuevo trabajo con mayor
horario. Al respecto, durante el trimestre octubre-diciembre de 2022, la población
subocupada en el país fue de 4.4 millones de personas (1.7 millones menos que en el mismo
trimestre de 2021) y representó 7.5 % de las personas ocupadas, tasa inferior a la de un
año antes (10.6 %).
Al distinguir por sexo, la población subocupada en los hombres pasó de 3.7 millones en el
cuarto trimestre de 2021 a 2.6 millones en el mismo periodo de 2022. En las mujeres, pasó
de 2.3 a 1.7 millones en el lapso referido. De esta manera, la tasa de subocupación
masculina se redujo a 7.6 % de los hombres ocupados y la femenina alcanzó 7.2 % de las
mujeres ocupadas.

Informalidad laboral
El empleo informal —o medición ampliada de informalidad— añade las siguientes
categorías a la definición de empleo en el sector informal: el trabajo no protegido en la
actividad agropecuaria, el servicio doméstico remunerado no protegido, así como las y los
trabajadores subordinados quienes, aunque trabajan para unidades económicas formales,
lo hacen sin seguridad social. Así, la medición de la ocupación en el sector informal se
considera el punto de partida y un componente del cálculo de una medida más amplia: el
empleo informal.

POBLACIÓN DESOCUPADA
La ocupación muestra distintos grados de intensidad según sea la perspectiva. Una de estas
es la población desocupada, misma que no trabajó ni una hora durante la semana de
referencia de la encuesta. No obstante, manifestó su disposición para trabajar e hizo alguna
actividad para hacerlo.
En el trimestre octubre-diciembre de 2022, la población desocupada en el país fue de 1.8
millones de personas y la Tasa de Desocupación (TD) de 3 % de la PEA, porcentaje inferior
al del mismo trimestre de 2021 (3.7 %). Al estar condicionada a la búsqueda de empleo, la
TD es más alta en las localidades grandes, en donde el mercado de trabajo tiene mayor
organización. En las zonas más urbanizadas (con 100 mil y más habitantes), la tasa llegó a
3.6 %; en las que tienen de 15 mil a menos de 100 mil habitantes, 2.9 %; en las de 2 500 a
menos de 15 mil, 2.7 % y en las rurales (de menos de 2 500 habitantes) fue de 1.7 por
ciento.

Tasa de Informalidad Laboral 1. Se refiere a la suma, sin duplicar, de las y los ocupados que son laboralmente vulnerables por la
naturaleza de la unidad económica para la que trabajan, más aquellas personas cuyo vínculo o dependencia laboral no se reconoce
por su fuente de trabajo. Así, en esta tasa se incluyen —además del componente que labora en micronegocios no registrados o
sector informal— otras modalidades análogas, como las y los ocupados por cuenta propia en la agricultura de subsistencia, así como
a quienes laboran sin la protección de la seguridad social y cuyos servicios los utilizan las unidades económicas registradas.

ocupados no agropecuarios cuyo vínculo o dependencia laboral no se reconoce por su fuente de trabajo.
Tasa de Ocupación en el Sector Informal 2. Proporción de la población ocupada que trabaja para una unidad económica no
agropecuaria y que opera a partir de los recursos del hogar, pero sin constituirse como empresa. Así, los ingresos, los materiales y
equipos que se utilizan para el negocio no son independientes o distinguibles de los del propio hogar. Esta tasa se calcula teniendo
como referente (denominador) a la población ocupada no agropecuaria.

LA OCUPACIÓN EN ÁREAS METROPOLITANAS5
A un nivel de desagregación por área metropolitana, donde el mercado de trabajo está más
organizado y se puede conocer con mayor precisión la complejidad del fenómeno
ocupacional, la ENOEN presenta los indicadores para 39 ciudades o áreas metropolitanas
con más de 100 mil habitantes.
En el periodo octubre-diciembre de 2022, la PEA para el agregado urbano de 39 ciudades
fue de 29 millones de personas y representó una tasa de participación de 61.6 por ciento.
Asimismo, se reportó una tasa de desocupación de 3.7 %, una tasa de subocupación de
7.2 %, una tasa de condiciones críticas de ocupación de 26.2 % y una tasa de informalidad
laboral de 43.5 por ciento.
Las áreas metropolitanas con una tasa de participación superior a la del agregado fueron:
Tepic (67.6 %), Ciudad Juárez (67 %), La Paz (66.1 %), León (65.5 %), Mérida (65.1 %),
Colima (64.3 %), Cancún (63.7 %), Torreón (63.6 %), así como Guadalajara y Mexicali
(63.5 %), entre otras.
De la población ocupada, 64 % se concentró en las áreas metropolitanas de Ciudad de
México, Guadalajara, Monterrey, Puebla, Tijuana, León, Ciudad Juárez y Mérida.
El mayor porcentaje de población ocupada que declaró tener necesidad y disponibilidad

Nota al usuario
La Encuesta Nacional de Ocupación y Empleo (ENOE) toma como referencia la estimación
de población que se genera mediante el marco de muestreo del INEGI. Además, se continúa
con el plan de actualización gradual respecto a incorporar la estimación de población del
INEGI en la serie histórica. Para la presente publicación se encuentran actualizados los
microdatos y tabulados a partir del primer trimestre de 2014.
A partir de la publicación con mes de referencia a enero de 2023, se reanudará la difusión
de resultados mensuales y trimestrales de la ENOE como Información de Interés Nacional.
Este programa estadístico incluye, dentro de su metodología, la captación de información a
través de entrevistas cara a cara y telefónicas, la actualización de catálogos, clasificadores,
así como la incorporación de las preguntas de migración y lugar de trabajo. Además,
iniciarán los trabajos para llevar a cabo la captación de información con herramientas
digitales. Así, se da por terminado el programa estadístico emergente ENOEN: la difusión
de sus resultados concluye con el cuarto trimestre de 2022.
Nota metodológica
Desde el tercer trimestre de 2020 se inició el levantamiento de la ENOEN con el mismo
diseño conceptual, estadístico y metodológico de la ENOE tradicional, pero bajo una
estrategia operativa mixta, de levantamiento de entrevistas telefónicas y cara a cara. A partir
del tercer trimestre de 2022, la información se levanta exclusivamente bajo la modalidad de
entrevistas cara a cara. De esta manera se inicia con la estrategia de retorno al
levantamiento regular.
La ENOEN respondió a la necesidad de volver paulatinamente al levantamiento regular de
la ENOE y dar continuidad a la información sobre las características y condiciones de la
población vinculada al mercado laboral. La ENOEN se aplica a los miembros del hogar de
una vivienda seleccionada por medio de técnicas de muestreo. El esquema de muestreo es
probabilístico, bietápico, estratificado y por conglomerados. Además, tiene como unidad
última de selección las viviendas particulares y como unidad de observación a las personas.
Una vez que se selecciona una vivienda, se la vuelve a visitar cada tres meses hasta
completar un total de cinco visitas. Una vez que el grupo de viviendas completa el ciclo, se
sustituye por otro grupo que inicia el propio. Se denomina «panel de muestra» a estos
grupos de viviendas según el número de visitas que han tenido. En cada momento en campo
hay cinco paneles, es decir, cinco grupos de viviendas que están por entrar a su primera,
segunda, tercera, cuarta o quinta visita. Así, siempre hay una quinta parte de la muestra que
es totalmente nueva en tanto que las otras cuatro quintas partes ya han sido visitadas tres
meses atrás. Este esquema permite dar a la muestra una combinación de estabilidad y
renovación. Asimismo, permite a las y los investigadores especializados rastrear los
cambios que han tenido los hogares que permanecieron en la muestra a lo largo del tiempo
(estudios longitudinales).

<<< UNIT 22 | page 22 >>>
El que cada vivienda tenga una probabilidad de selección se traduce en que todos sus
residentes son representativos de muchos otros en su área de residencia (dominio de
muestra), tanto en sus características sociodemográficas como socioeconómicas. Los
resultados se generalizan para toda la población que representan. Esto se hace mediante
los factores de expansión, que son el inverso de las probabilidades de selección de las
viviendas. Cada factor de expansión toma en cuenta el estrato socioeconómico y el ámbito
geográfico que corresponden a la vivienda seleccionada.
La encuesta está diseñada para dar resultados a nivel nacional, entidad federativa y áreas
urbanas para cada trimestre del año. También permite que el nivel nacional pueda
desagregarse en cuatro dominios: I) localidades de 100 mil y más habitantes, II) de 15 mil a
99 999 habitantes, III) de 2 500 a 14 999 habitantes y IV) menos de 2 500 habitantes.
El calendario de difusión del INEGI, disponible en su página de internet, indica la fecha en
la que se dará a conocer la información trimestral.
La ENOEN toma en cuenta los criterios que la Organización para la Cooperación y el
Desarrollo Económicos (OCDE) propone dentro del marco general de la Organización
Internacional del Trabajo (OIT). Lo anterior permite delimitar con mayor claridad a la
población ocupada y a la desocupada, además de facilitar la comparabilidad internacional
de las cifras de ocupación y empleo. La ENOEN asimismo incorpora el marco conceptual de
la OIT y las recomendaciones del Grupo de Delhi relativas a la medición de la ocupación en
el sector informal. La encuesta está diseñada para identificar los conceptos de
desocupación, subocupación e informalidad sin confundirlos. También toma en cuenta y da
un lugar específico a las personas que no presionan activamente en el mercado laboral
porque ellas mismas consideran que ya no tienen oportunidad alguna de competir en este
(mujeres que por dedicarse al hogar no han acumulado experiencia laboral, personas
maduras y de la tercera edad, etc.).
El diseño de la ENOEN se enfoca en proporcionar abundantes elementos para caracterizar
la calidad de la ocupación en México. Este aspecto se considera analíticamente tan
relevante como el de la desocupación misma o como cualquier otro fenómeno de
desequilibrio entre oferta y demanda de trabajo. El diseño de la encuesta ha abierto más
posibilidades para el enfoque de género relativo tanto al ámbito del trabajo como a la
marginación.
Para la ENOEN del tercer trimestre de 2020 al segundo trimestre de 2022, dentro de la PNEA
disponible, se clasificó a las personas ausentes temporales de una actividad u oficio y a las
personas con necesidad o deseos de trabajar. En la ENOE, estas estaban bajo el rubro
«otros» de la PNEA no disponible. La razón de este cambio fue que, debido a la pandemia
y al cierre de las empresas y negocios en actividades no esenciales, técnicamente estas
personas quedaron fuera de la fuerza de trabajo al perder el vínculo con la unidad
empleadora, pero muchas regresarían a su trabajo una vez que se reactivaran las
actividades. Por lo anterior, conceptualmente, fue más pertinente darles un tratamiento de
«personas disponibles para trabajar sin posibilidades de búsqueda de trabajo», que
dejarlas

La mayoría de las series económicas se ven afectadas por factores estacionales: efectos
periódicos que se repiten cada año y cuyas causas son ajenas a la naturaleza económica
de las series. Estas pueden ser: las festividades, meses más largos que otros, las
vacaciones escolares, el clima y otras fluctuaciones, como la elevada producción de
juguetes en los meses previos a la Navidad.
La desestacionalización o ajuste estacional de series económicas consiste en remover estas
influencias intra-anuales periódicas: su presencia dificulta diagnosticar y describir el
comportamiento de una serie económica, pues no permite comparar adecuadamente un
determinado trimestre con el inmediato anterior. Analizar la serie desestacionalizada ayuda
a realizar un mejor diagnóstico y pronóstico de su evolución, pues, en el corto plazo,
identifica la posible dirección de los movimientos que pudiera tener la variable en cuestión.
Las series originales se ajustan estacionalmente mediante el paquete estadístico
X-13ARIMA-SEATS. Para conocer la metodología, consúltese la siguiente liga:
