# Document-Level Evidence Paper

## Source identification and evidentiary posture

**Document:** Office of the United States Trade Representative (USTR), “United States Requests Consultations Under the USMCA Over Mexico’s Energy Policies,” press release, **20 July 2022**.  
**Target:** Andrés Manuel López Obrador (AMLO), Mexico, 2022.  
**Chapter:** 5B — economic governance.  
**Source type and perspective:** Formal trade-dispute announcement by a foreign government. It records the United States’ decision to request consultations under the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA) and sets out the measures the United States intended to challenge. It is therefore strong evidence of the existence, date, and stated legal basis of the U.S. action, and useful evidence about the identified Mexican laws, regulatory decisions, and administrative conduct. Its characterizations of those measures as “unfair,” investment-deterring, or inconsistent with the USMCA remain the position of the complainant rather than findings by a neutral panel.

The document does not contain Mexico’s response, the underlying consultation request, judicial decisions concerning the measures, private-company case records, data measuring investment or electricity prices, or a final USMCA adjudication. It does not establish the ultimate legality of the challenged policies. It also provides little direct evidence on fiscal policy, monetary governance, professional appointments, social distribution, or the quality of public-service delivery.

## Principal evidence: reorientation of energy policy toward CFE and PEMEX

USTR states that, beginning in **December 2018**, Mexico pursued an energy policy aimed at restoring the primacy of the state-owned electricity utility, **Comisión Federal de Electricidad (CFE)**, and the state-owned oil and gas company, **Petróleos Mexicanos (PEMEX)**. USTR presents the individual measures discussed below as instruments of that broader policy. This chronology associates the shift with AMLO’s presidential term, although the document generally attributes actions to “Mexico,” its legislature, regulators, or executive agencies rather than documenting AMLO’s personal instructions. [Unit 5]

The policy departed from the direction of Mexico’s **2013 energy reforms**. According to USTR, U.S. companies responded to those earlier reforms by investing in Mexican energy operations: they established and operated wind and solar farms, cogeneration facilities, and combined-cycle facilities capable of contributing electricity to the grid; strengthened cross-border interconnection arrangements supplying U.S. electricity to Mexico; and imported U.S.-origin fuels for sale through Mexican gas stations. This background matters because the 2022 dispute did not concern merely hypothetical market entry. The complainant asserted that firms had already undertaken investments and established operations in reliance on an earlier regulatory framework. [Unit 4]

USTR’s central interpretation is that the post-2018 measures disadvantaged private suppliers and foreign investment to promote CFE and PEMEX. It says the changes affected U.S. economic interests across multiple sectors and discouraged investment both by clean-energy producers and by companies seeking clean and reliable electricity. Ambassador Katherine Tai stated that the United States had repeatedly raised the measures’ consistency with USMCA obligations and had tried to address them constructively, but that U.S. companies continued to receive what she called “unfair treatment.” That is an official U.S. assessment, not an independently adjudicated fact. [Unit 3]

## Electricity dispatch: the March 2021 statutory amendment

USTR specifically challenged a **2021 amendment to Mexico’s Electric Power Industry Law**. The document later dates the amendment to **March 2021** and describes its operative mechanism: Mexico’s grid operator would prioritize electricity generated by CFE for dispatch to the national grid over electricity generated by **all private competitors**. USTR states that this priority applied “irrespective of cost or environmental impact.” [Units 4–5]

The significance of the measure is concrete. Dispatch priority determines which generating plants are used to supply the grid and therefore affects producers’ ability to sell electricity and recover investments. As USTR describes the rule, CFE obtained preference because of institutional ownership rather than because its electricity was cheaper, cleaner, or otherwise superior under neutral dispatch criteria. The rule consequently bears directly on whether Mexico enforced fair competition and predictable investment regulation under **5B.4**, and whether politically favored state enterprises received preferential treatment under **5B.5**.

USTR also says that the amendment prioritized CFE power over cleaner private sources, identifying wind and solar generation as examples. It interprets this as discouraging clean-energy investment and reversing steps taken under earlier reforms to advance Mexico’s Paris Agreement climate commitments. The source further argues that clean, reliable, and affordable energy is important to regional supply chains and to North American economic, development, and climate goals. These are the complainant’s causal and policy interpretations; the release supplies no dispatch statistics, comparative generation costs, emissions calculations, reliability data, or Mexican justification with which to test them. [Units 3–4]

The document thus supports the factual conclusion that the United States formally objected to an ownership-based dispatch preference embedded in Mexican law. It does **not** establish whether the preference had already altered actual dispatch volumes by July 2022, whether courts had limited implementation, or whether CFE priority produced lower consumer prices, greater security of supply, or other public benefits claimed elsewhere by Mexican authorities.

## Permit administration and restrictions on private operations

Beyond the electricity-law amendment, USTR alleged a pattern of administrative action and inaction that curtailed private participation in the energy sector. The conduct identified included:

- delaying, denying, or failing to act on applications for new permits;
- delaying, denying, or failing to act on permit-modification applications;
- suspending or revoking existing permits; and
- otherwise blocking private companies from undertaking specified energy activities. [Unit 5]

The affected activities were described in operational terms. They included operating renewable-energy facilities such as wind and solar installations; importing and exporting electricity and fuel; storing or transloading fuel; and building or operating retail fuel stations. USTR’s wording—Mexico “has taken, or is taking,” actions or inactions—indicates that the complaint covered both completed decisions and continuing administrative conduct as of July 2022. [Unit 5]

USTR separately summarizes the problem as Mexican “delays, denials, and revocations” of U.S. companies’ ability to operate, including in renewable-energy projects. It claims that these policies had “largely cut off” U.S. and other investment in clean-energy infrastructure and threatened to drive private innovation out of Mexico’s energy market. Those investment effects are consequential allegations, but the press release does not identify the affected companies, number of permits, project capacities, investment values, exact agency decisions, dates, stated grounds, or resulting cancellations. The magnitude and causation of the alleged investment decline cannot therefore be verified from this document alone. [Units 3–4]

For **5B.4**, the permit evidence raises concerns about regulatory predictability, market access, entrepreneurship, trade, and investment: firms allegedly could not obtain timely decisions or rely on existing authorizations. For **5B.7**, repeated delay or non-action may indicate administrative opacity or resistance to external evaluation, but the source does not describe disclosure rules, Mexican publication practices, audit findings, or whether reasons and appeal rights accompanied particular decisions. It therefore supports a question for further investigation rather than a firm conclusion about transparency or evidence-based correction.

## PEMEX’s diesel-sulfur compliance extension

USTR identifies a distinct regulatory preference granted in **December 2019**. Mexico’s energy regulator gave PEMEX a **five-year extension** to comply with maximum sulfur-content requirements for automotive diesel in certain parts of Mexico. The applicable standard otherwise required the sale of **ultra-low-sulfur diesel throughout the country**. According to USTR, no equivalent extension was granted to other companies, including U.S. firms. [Units 4–5]

The preference had an identifiable economic mechanism. USTR states that, without the extension, PEMEX would have needed either to purchase ultra-low-sulfur diesel imported from the United States or to upgrade its own facilities so that it could produce enough compliant diesel. The extension therefore relieved PEMEX, for five years and in the covered regions, of costs that compliance would otherwise have imposed. Private competitors remained subject to the standard as described. [Unit 5]

This is the document’s clearest example relevant to **5B.5**. A regulator allegedly applied a general product standard asymmetrically in favor of the politically important state oil company. The episode also bears on **5B.4**, because unequal compliance dates can distort competition and affect trade in U.S.-produced fuel. It may be relevant to **5B.1** and **5B.10** insofar as delayed refinery upgrades and extended sale of higher-sulfur diesel could substitute short-term protection of PEMEX for investment in productive capacity and environmental performance.

Important limitations remain. The release does not identify the regulator’s legal rationale, PEMEX’s technical constraints, the geographic coverage or affected diesel volumes, whether other firms sought extensions, or whether supply-security considerations motivated the decision. It also does not quantify compliance costs, pollution effects, fuel-price effects, or benefits to consumers. The preferential treatment is the U.S. government’s reported factual basis for consultations; its legality and policy justification were not adjudicated in the press release.

## June 2022 natural-gas transportation policy

The most recent measure described occurred in **June 2022**. Mexico’s **Secretary of Energy** notified the **Energy Regulatory Commission (CRE)** and the **National Natural Gas System Operator (CENAGAS)** of a policy change concerning access to Mexico’s natural-gas transportation network. USTR says the policy would require, “among other things,” users of the network to demonstrate that they sourced natural gas from PEMEX or CFE. [Unit 6]

This requirement tied access to shared transportation infrastructure to purchasing from one of the two state-owned energy companies. If applied as USTR describes, it could advantage PEMEX and CFE in upstream gas sales and disadvantage firms purchasing gas from private or foreign suppliers. USTR accordingly characterized the June action as favoring PEMEX, CFE, and their products in the use of the transportation network. [Unit 4]

The mechanism is relevant to **5B.4** because it conditions infrastructure access on the identity of the gas supplier rather than presenting neutral terms for all market participants. It is relevant to **5B.5** because the beneficiaries were state-owned entities favored by the administration’s stated energy-policy direction. It may also affect **5B.6** and **5B.10** by shaping whether gas infrastructure is used on predictable and economically efficient terms, but the document provides no information on network capacity, tariffs, actual denials of service, implementation after the notification, gas prices, reliability, or downstream production effects.

The phrase “would require” suggests a prospective or newly announced rule as of June–July 2022. The source should not be read as proof that the requirement had already been fully implemented or had produced measured economic outcomes by the consultation request of 20 July.

## Formal USMCA dispute and alleged treaty violations

On **20 July 2022**, U.S. Trade Representative Katherine Tai announced that the United States had requested formal dispute-settlement consultations with Mexico under the USMCA. The request covered the electricity dispatch rule, permit-related conduct, PEMEX’s diesel-sulfur extension, and the natural-gas transportation measure. USTR stated that the measures appeared inconsistent with Mexican obligations under several USMCA chapters, specifically naming **Market Access, Investment, and State-Owned Enterprises**. [Units 3–4]

The procedural framework was explicit. Under **USMCA Article 31.4.5**, the parties were to enter consultations within **30 days** of the request unless they agreed otherwise. Under **Article 31.6.1**, if consultations did not resolve the matter within **75 days**, the United States could request establishment of a dispute panel. [Unit 6]

This is evidence that concerns about Mexico’s energy governance had escalated beyond stakeholder complaints or diplomatic criticism into a treaty-based state-to-state enforcement process during the target year. It is not evidence that a panel found Mexico in breach. The release repeatedly uses complainant language such as “undermine,” “unfair treatment,” and “appear to be inconsistent.” Those propositions should remain identified as U.S. allegations and legal assessments.

USTR reports that it had raised concerns on numerous prior occasions: at USMCA Free Trade Commission meetings in **2021 and 2022**; at the Free Trade Commission Deputies Meeting in **2022**; and in meetings of the USMCA Committee on State-Owned Enterprises and Designated Monopolies. Tai also consulted members of the U.S. Congress and a broad range of stakeholders about what the release calls the “deteriorating trajectory” of Mexican energy policy. [Unit 6]

The repeated contacts support a sequence relevant to **5B.7**: concerns were communicated through several institutional channels before the United States initiated formal consultations, yet USTR claimed the underlying treatment continued. The document, however, does not reproduce Mexico’s replies, identify concessions or corrective measures offered by either side, or establish that the U.S. characterization of those exchanges was complete. It therefore supports the conclusion that the United States perceived unresolved problems after repeated engagement, not that Mexico categorically refused evidence or correction.

## Implications for the supplied Chapter 5B questions

### 5B.1 — policy orientation toward broad-based sustainable prosperity

The document provides adverse evidence about the orientation of one major policy area. USTR depicts Mexico’s post-2018 energy agenda as designed to restore the primacy of CFE and PEMEX through legal preferences, permit restrictions, delayed environmental compliance, and conditions on gas-network access. Its account suggests that the administration privileged state-company market position over cost-based dispatch, private investment, competition, and clean-energy development. [Units 4–6]

The release also connects energy regulation to longer-term prosperity mechanisms: investment, innovation, clean and affordable electricity, regional supply chains, climate goals, and infrastructure upgrading. The five-year diesel extension is particularly relevant because it allegedly allowed PEMEX to postpone purchasing compliant fuel or upgrading its facilities. [Units 4–5]

This source cannot determine the administration’s total economic-policy orientation. It contains no budget data and no systematic comparison of public versus private provision, energy security, consumer prices, universal service, or the use of state-enterprise revenues. It also omits Mexico’s possible claim that stronger state ownership served sovereignty, affordability, or security of supply. The evidence is therefore significant but sector-specific and complainant-framed.

### 5B.2 — appointment and independence of economic professionals

The source offers almost no direct evidence. It identifies the Secretary of Energy, CRE, CENAGAS, CFE, and PEMEX as institutional actors, but it does not discuss appointment qualifications, selection procedures, tenure, operational independence, dismissal, or performance assessment. The fact that the Secretary of Energy notified CRE and CENAGAS of a policy change may warrant investigation into regulatory independence, but this document alone does not show improper appointment or personnel control. [Unit 6]

### 5B.3 — fiscal, tax, debt, monetary, and financial rules

There is no direct evidence about fiscal balances, taxation, debt, monetary policy, banking regulation, or financial stability. The document’s concerns about investment and state enterprises may have indirect fiscal implications, but none are quantified or developed.

### 5B.4 — fair competition, trade, investment, and job-creation rules

This is the most strongly implicated question. USTR identifies four different mechanisms allegedly producing non-neutral treatment:

1. a March 2021 law prioritizing CFE electricity over all private competitors regardless of cost or environmental impact;
2. delays, denials, failures to act, suspensions, and revocations affecting private permits and operations;
3. a five-year diesel-standard extension granted to PEMEX but not competitors; and
4. a June 2022 gas-network rule linking access to purchases from PEMEX or CFE. [Units 4–6]

Together, these measures describe both formal legal preference and administrative restriction. They allegedly affected electricity generation, renewable projects, cross-border electricity and fuel trade, fuel storage and transloading, retail fuel stations, and access to gas transportation. USTR states that the result was diminished clean-energy investment and a threat to private innovation, but provides no project-level or aggregate economic data. [Units 3–5]

### 5B.5 — enforcement against connected actors and remedy of favoritism

The two most concrete favoritism episodes concern PEMEX. First, the energy regulator allegedly gave only PEMEX a five-year extension from the diesel-sulfur standard. Second, the June 2022 gas policy allegedly advantaged PEMEX and CFE through network-access conditions. The dispatch amendment also favored CFE by ownership status. [Units 4–6]

The document reports repeated U.S. efforts to obtain a remedy and the subsequent launch of formal USMCA consultations. It does not show that Mexican institutions investigated, sanctioned, or corrected the preferences before 20 July 2022. Conversely, it contains no audit findings, procurement evidence, corruption allegations, court records, or proof that state-company officials personally benefited. The relevant issue here is institutional favoritism toward state enterprises, not demonstrated private corruption.

### 5B.6 — infrastructure, technology, administrative capacity, and predictable regulation

USTR describes investments made after the 2013 reforms in wind, solar, cogeneration, combined-cycle generation, electricity interconnections, fuel imports, and retail distribution. It alleges that later permit restrictions and ownership preferences impeded such infrastructure and innovation. It also argues that clean, reliable, affordable electricity is necessary for regional supply chains and development. [Units 4–5]

The permit evidence is material to regulatory capacity and predictability: delays or failures to act can strand investment even without an express prohibition. Yet the source does not report processing times, administrative backlogs, completed infrastructure, service quality, education, health, or executed budgets. Its infrastructure evidence is confined to energy and is principally about barriers to private projects.

### 5B.7 — information, evaluation, audit, and correction

The United States states that it raised concerns repeatedly in 2021 and 2022 through the USMCA Free Trade Commission, a deputies meeting, and the committee addressing state-owned enterprises and monopolies. USTR says it attempted constructive engagement but that U.S. companies continued to face unfair treatment, leading to formal consultations in July 2022. [Units 3 and 6]

This chronology may indicate limited policy correction despite external feedback. However, the source does not provide Mexico’s responses, the evidence exchanged, independent Mexican evaluations, public data quality, audits, court review, or any internal correction process. It therefore cannot sustain a broad conclusion about whether AMLO generally published reliable economic information or responded to evidence.

### 5B.8 — distribution of gains and burdens

The source does not analyze incidence across income classes, regions, genders, workers, consumers, or other groups. It indicates that regulatory burdens differed between state firms and private competitors and that the diesel extension applied in “certain parts” of Mexico, but it does not identify those regions or affected populations. No conclusion about distributive fairness across Mexican society is possible from this document.

### 5B.9 — response to economic shocks

No shock-response policy is evaluated. The document does not discuss inflation, unemployment, debt, the pandemic, sanctions, commodity-price shocks, emergency subsidies, or targeted relief. Potential concerns about energy reliability and affordability are stated only at a general level.

### 5B.10 — durable economic trajectory

USTR portrays the energy-policy trajectory as deteriorating from the perspective of competition, foreign investment, clean energy, innovation, regional supply chains, and climate commitments. Its causal account is that firms invested after the 2013 opening; post-December 2018 policy restored state-company primacy; legislation and administrative measures then constrained private participation; and unresolved concerns culminated in a formal USMCA dispute in July 2022. [Units 4–6]

That sequence is relevant to institutional durability because it suggests a reversal of prior market-opening rules and reduced investor predictability. The diesel extension also suggests postponement of productive or environmental upgrading by PEMEX. But the release supplies no GDP, productivity, employment, electricity-price, reliability, emissions, investment-flow, fiscal, or distributional series. It cannot establish Mexico’s overall inherited-versus-bequeathed economic trajectory, and even within energy it documents a dispute at mid-2022 rather than long-run outcomes.

## Material qualifications and competing considerations

The document contains no substantive Mexican defense. A fair downstream use should therefore retain several boundaries:

- **Complainant status:** USTR was initiating the dispute and advocating the U.S. position. Its legal claims were not neutral findings.
- **Attribution:** The source documents actions by Mexico’s legislature, Secretary of Energy, energy regulator, CRE, CENAGAS, CFE, and PEMEX. It situates them within a policy pursued since AMLO took office, but it does not document his personal involvement in each decision.
- **Outcome limits:** No final panel ruling or negotiated resolution appears in the source.
- **Implementation limits:** The June 2022 gas measure is described prospectively; the release does not establish full implementation. The practical reach of the March 2021 dispatch rule is also not quantified.
- **Missing Mexican rationale:** The source does not assess possible arguments concerning energy sovereignty, universal access, consumer affordability, supply reliability, or correction of perceived deficiencies in the 2013 reforms.
- **Missing quantitative corroboration:** Claims that investment was “largely cut off,” innovation was threatened, and cleaner power was displaced are not accompanied by values, capacities, firm-level cases, generation data, or counterfactual analysis.
- **Sectoral scope:** The evidence is important for competition and investment governance but does not represent the entirety of AMLO’s economic policy in 2022.

## Decision-useful bottom line without scoring

The release documents a formal July 2022 interstate trade challenge arising from a sequence of Mexican energy measures adopted from December 2019 through June 2022. The most probative factual accounts are the ownership-based electricity dispatch priority, alleged permit obstruction across several energy activities, PEMEX’s exclusive five-year diesel-compliance extension, and the proposed conditioning of gas-network access on sourcing from PEMEX or CFE. These episodes provide substantial adverse evidence for questions about neutral competition, regulatory predictability, investment treatment, and favoritism toward state-owned enterprises. They provide narrower evidence about long-term policy orientation and infrastructure investment, and little or none about appointments, macroeconomic rules, social incidence, shock response, or economy-wide outcomes.

The downstream judge should treat the existence and timing of the U.S. consultation request and the measures identified in it as the document’s strongest evidence. Claims about treaty breach, unfairness, investment effects, environmental consequences, and economic trajectory should remain attributed to USTR unless corroborated by the underlying consultation request, Mexican legal texts and responses, firm or permit records, quantitative energy data, or a later dispute ruling.

United States Requests Consultations Under the USMCA Over Mexico's Energy Policies
July 20, 2022
WASHINGTON – United States Trade Representative Katherine Tai today announced that the United States has requested dispute settlement consultations with Mexico under the United States-Mexico-Canada Agreement (USMCA). The consultations relate to certain measures by Mexico that undermine American companies and U.S.-produced energy in favor of Mexico’s state-owned electrical utility, the Comisión Federal de Electricidad (CFE), and state-owned oil and gas company, Petróleos Mexicanos (PEMEX).
“We have repeatedly expressed serious concerns about a series of changes in Mexico’s energy policies and their consistency with Mexico’s commitments under the USMCA,” said Ambassador Katherine Tai . “These policy changes impact U.S. economic interests in multiple sectors and disincentivize investment by clean-energy suppliers and by companies that seek to purchase clean, reliable energy. We have tried to work constructively with the Mexican government to address these concerns, but, unfortunately, U.S. companies continue to face unfair treatment in Mexico. We will seek to work with the Mexican government through these consultations to resolve these concerns to advance North American competitiveness.”
Mexico’s actions include, but are not limited to, amendments to Mexico’s electricity law that would prioritize the distribution of CFE-generated power over cleaner sources of energy provided by private sector suppliers, such as wind and solar. They also include Mexico’s delays, denials, and revocations of U.S. companies’ abilities to operate in Mexico’s energy sector, including with regard to renewable energy projects.

<<< UNIT 4 | extracted HTML block 4 >>>
Mexico’s policies have largely cut off U.S. and other investment in the country’s clean energy infrastructure, including significant steps to roll back reforms Mexico previously made to meet its climate goals under the Paris Agreement. Mexico’s policy changes threaten to push private sector innovation out of the Mexican energy market. To reach our shared regional economic and development goals and climate goals, current and future supply chains need clean, reliable, and affordable energy.
Specifically, the United States is challenging a 2021 amendment to Mexico’s Electric Power Industry Law that prioritizes CFE-produced electricity over electricity generated by all private competitors; Mexico’s inaction, delays, denials, and revocations of private companies’ abilities to operate in Mexico’s energy sector; a December 2019 regulation granting only PEMEX an extension to comply with the maximum sulfur content requirements under Mexico’s applicable automotive diesel fuel standard; and a June 2022 action that advantages PEMEX, CFE, and their products in the use of Mexico’s natural gas transportation network. These measures appear to be inconsistent with several of Mexico’s USMCA obligations, including under the Market Access, Investment, and State-Owned Enterprises chapters.
Background
In 2013, Mexico pursued various energy reforms, in response to which U.S. companies invested in Mexico’s energy sector. Specifically, U.S. companies have: (1) established and operated wind and solar energy farms and cogeneration and combined cycle facilities in Mexico that would contribute electricity to Mexico’s grid; (2) strengthened interconnection contracts that bring U.S. electricity to Mexico across the border through wires; and (3) imported U.S.-origin fuels to Mexico for sale at gas stations.

<<< UNIT 5 | extracted HTML block 5 >>>
Since December 2018, however, Mexico has pursued an energy policy centered on reinstating the primacy of its state-owned electrical utility, CFE, and oil and gas company, PEMEX. Mexico has undertaken various measures to achieve this aim. For example, in March 2021, Mexico amended its Electric Power Industry Law so that its grid operator will prioritize for dispatch to Mexico’s grid CFE-generated electricity over electricity generated by all private competitors, irrespective of cost or environmental impact.
Mexico has also taken, or is taking, actions or inactions, which are curtailing the ability of private companies to participate effectively, if at all, in Mexico’s energy sector. These actions include, but are not limited to, delaying, denying or failing to act on applications for new permits or permit modifications; suspending or revoking existing permits; or otherwise blocking private companies’ ability to operate renewable energy facilities, such as wind and solar installations, to import and export electricity and fuel, to store or transload fuel, and to build or operate retail fuel stations.
In addition, in December 2019, Mexico’s energy regulator granted PEMEX – but not other companies, including U.S. companies – a five-year extension to comply with maximum sulfur content requirements under its fuel standard in certain parts of Mexico, which otherwise require the sale of ultra-low sulfur diesel fuel throughout the country. Without the extension, PEMEX would have to purchase ultra-low sulfur diesel imported from the United States and/or upgrade its facilities to produce ultra-low sulfur diesel in sufficient quantities.

More recently, in June 2022, Mexico’s Secretary of Energy notified the Energy Regulatory Commission (CRE) and the National Natural Gas System Operator (CENAGAS) of a change in policy that would require, among other things, that users of Mexico’s gas transportation network demonstrate that they source natural gas from PEMEX or CFE.
The United States has raised concerns with Mexico regarding its energy policies on numerous occasions, including in connection with the USMCA Free Trade Commission meetings in 2021 and 2022, the Deputies Meeting of the USMCA Free Trade Commission in 2022, and in meetings of the USMCA Committee on State-Owned Enterprises and Designated Monopolies. Ambassador Tai also consulted members of Congress and a broad range of stakeholders to hear their serious concerns with the deteriorating trajectory of Mexico’s energy policies.
Under USMCA Article 31.4.5, the parties shall enter into consultations within 30 days of the U.S. request, unless the parties decide otherwise. Under USMCA Article 31.6.1, if the parties do not resolve the matter through consultations within 75 days of the U.S. request, the United States may request the establishment of a panel.
