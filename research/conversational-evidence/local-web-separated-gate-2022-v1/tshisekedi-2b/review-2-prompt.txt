## Scope and orientation

Period assessed: **1 January–31 December 2022**. “2022–2022” is treated as a single calendar year. No score is assigned.

### Authority

Félix Tshisekedi was president throughout 2022. Constitutionally and practically he was the supreme commander of the armed forces and police, chaired the Higher Defence Council, appointed senior security officials, directed foreign policy, and controlled the presidential decrees sustaining the state of siege. A formal declaration of war nevertheless required cabinet deliberation, Defence Council advice, and parliamentary authorization. Parliament repeatedly approved two-week extensions of the state of siege, so responsibility was shared but presidentially initiated and sustained.

Tshisekedi directly participated in the Luanda interstate negotiations and authorized the Nairobi process, Ugandan operations on Congolese territory, and deployment of the East African Community Regional Force. Weak command systems, fragmented FARDC units, provincial military government, foreign partners, MONUSCO, and autonomous or semi-coordinated militias substantially limited practical control over individual operations.

### Baseline and conflict gate

The international-security exposure gate is decisively met. Tshisekedi inherited:

- Decades of regionalized warfare in eastern DRC, involving Rwanda, Uganda, Burundi, foreign armed groups, and numerous Congolese militias.
- A 2021 state of siege in North Kivu and Ituri.
- Operation Shujaa, the joint FARDC–Ugandan campaign against the ADF, begun in late 2021.
- M23’s reactivation in November 2021 and unresolved obligations arising from earlier peace arrangements.
- Extremely weak civilian protection and military accountability.

The local D13/UCDP prior records conflict events, fatalities, one-sided violence and government-actor killings but does not expose its numerical rows in the supplied material. An opened UCDP-authored publication provides the strongest accessible D13 fact: **at least 3,200 civilians were killed in one-sided violence in DRC during 2022**, while expressly warning that this excludes crossfire and indiscriminate shelling. UCDP classified Rwandan support to M23 against the Congolese government as one of five 2022 cases of state support to a rebel organization fighting another state.

The D14/SIPRI prior and SIPRI-derived series report approximately **US$371 million** in DRC military expenditure in 2022, about **21.2% above 2021**. This measures spending, not procurement integrity or ruler enrichment.

### Shocks and constraints

The major period shock was the sharp M23 offensive, including the capture of Bunagana and other territory, with increasingly strong evidence of direct Rwandan military intervention. The ADF, CODECO, Nyatura and other inherited threats continued simultaneously. Conflict displaced hundreds of thousands of people, while humanitarian agencies operated under severe access and funding constraints. The Ukraine-related food and energy shock added fiscal and humanitarian pressure but does not explain the core military decisions.

Reporting was comparatively plural but incomplete. MONUSCO, UN experts, NGOs, regional bodies and local journalists documented major incidents, yet military-site restrictions, insecurity, intimidation and inaccessible M23-held territory produced substantial undercounting and attribution uncertainty.

## Disposition of the ten lenses

### 2B.1 — Diplomacy versus force

**Mixed, with meaningful favorable evidence.** Tshisekedi joined the Luanda and Nairobi processes, signed the 23 November ceasefire framework, accepted monitoring and withdrawal arrangements, and supported DDR and humanitarian commitments (WEB-2B-004, 005). He also continued Uganda’s Operation Shujaa, sought a force-capable EAC deployment, strengthened FARDC “strike capacity,” and excluded direct M23 bargaining unless withdrawal conditions were met (WEB-2B-010, 011). The underlying M23/Rwandan and ADF threats were credible, so military resistance was not simply a war of choice.

### 2B.2 — War of choice, cross-border coercion or proxy conflict

**No persuasive evidence that Tshisekedi initiated an annexationist or external war of choice.** The 2022 M23/RDF offensive was directed against Congolese territory (WEB-2B-003). However, DRC authorized Ugandan forces to operate on its territory and FARDC elements cooperated with FDLR and other armed groups, extending regional proxy dynamics and creating foreseeable escalation risks (WEB-2B-007, 010). The available evidence does not establish that Tshisekedi personally ordered the FDLR cooperation.

### 2B.3 — Defensive need versus prestige or manufactured threats

**The central external threat was genuine, but security policy was not cleanly confined to lawful defense.** UN, EU, UCDP and independent reporting corroborated RDF support for M23 (WEB-2B-002, 003, 007). Tshisekedi’s description of Rwandan aggression therefore was not manufactured. Calling M23 “terrorists,” expanding military capacity, and relying on abusive militias nevertheless simplified a conflict containing unresolved political, communal and prior-agreement issues (WEB-2B-005, 007, 011). Evidence for diversionary or personal-prestige motivation is insufficient.

### 2B.4 — Civilian protection and humanitarian law

**Adverse overall, with limited remedial evidence.** Armed groups caused most documented abuses, but FARDC personnel committed extrajudicial killings and conflict-related sexual violence, while state-of-siege authorities killed protesters and subjected civilians to military courts (WEB-2B-008, 009). Some soldiers and police were convicted, and action plans and training were initiated, but impunity—especially at higher levels—remained pervasive. Evidence tying individual battlefield violations directly to Tshisekedi is generally absent.

### 2B.5 — Control of allies, proxies and arms recipients

**Serious adverse evidence.** UN findings and the EU’s year-end response identified FARDC cooperation with FDLR and other Congolese armed groups, despite their abusive records. Arms-trafficking charges against FARDC officers and abuses by allied Burundian actors reinforce the control problem (WEB-2B-007). The Luanda commitment to end all support to armed groups and some arrests count as contrary evidence, but implementation was incomplete. Direct presidential authorization of particular militia cooperation remains unproved.

### 2B.6 — Truthful public explanation

**The principal accusation was substantially corroborated, but official framing was selective.** Independent UN evidence supported the claim that Rwanda materially backed M23 and operated inside DRC (WEB-2B-003, 011). Official descriptions omitted or downplayed FARDC cooperation with FDLR, which provided Rwanda with a real—though not legally sufficient—security grievance (WEB-2B-007). There is no adequate evidence of fabricated intelligence directed by Tshisekedi.

### 2B.7 — Ceasefires, talks and reconciliation

**Favorable at the level of presidential commitment; weak and incomplete in outcome.** Tshisekedi signed or accepted the Luanda ceasefire, bilateral normalization, monitoring, withdrawal, cantonment, refugee-return and cessation-of-support provisions. The Nairobi dialogue added prisoner release, child-soldier release, humanitarian access, consultations and P-DDRCS review (WEB-2B-004, 005). M23 withdrew from Kibumba, but continued hostilities and incomplete compliance showed that the process had not produced peace by year’s end (WEB-2B-006).

### 2B.8 — Military spending and patronage

**Real security need is established; patronage conclusion is not.** SIPRI-derived data indicate spending rose to about US$371 million, 21.2% above 2021, during an exceptionally severe conflict year (WEB-2B-002, 012). Weak payroll and procurement controls make diversion plausible, but the searched 2022 material does not establish that the increase enriched Tshisekedi, his family, or a specified military network. This lens remains low-confidence.

### 2B.9 — Accountability and correction

**Partial institutional accountability, little ruler-level accountability.** Authorities convicted some soldiers, police and intelligence personnel and arrested officers accused of supplying CODECO; joint UN action plans and training were adopted (WEB-2B-007, 008). But the government repeatedly extended a demonstrably ineffective state of siege, did not provide an effective public remedy for its structural abuses, and higher-level impunity persisted (WEB-2B-009). No evidence was found of Tshisekedi accepting personal responsibility for failed protection or FARDC–FDLR cooperation.

### 2B.10 — End-state change

**Relations and security were worse at the end of 2022 than at its beginning, despite credible diplomacy.** The year ended with M23 holding territory, direct Rwandan involvement, expanded regional-force deployments, severed ambassadorial relations, mass displacement and continued fighting. Luanda and Nairobi created a lawful diplomatic architecture and produced one partial withdrawal, but not a durable settlement (WEB-2B-004, 005, 006, 013). Much deterioration was caused by external and inherited actors rather than initiated by Tshisekedi.
