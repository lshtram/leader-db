## Scope and orientation

This assessment covers calendar year 2022 only. Abdel Fattah el-Sisi was president throughout the period.

### Authority and practical control

Egypt’s constitution made the president head of state, supreme commander of the armed forces, chair of the National Defence Council, appointing authority for senior civil and military officials, and a necessary reviewing authority for military-court and emergency-court judgments. The 2019 amendments also strengthened presidential influence over senior judicial appointments. This supports strong attribution for presidential pardons, national security policy, emergency-law substitutes, senior appointments, and military policy. It does not establish that Sisi personally ordered every arrest, custodial abuse, or local police action.

Practical coercive power was distributed among the Interior Ministry, National Security Agency, General Intelligence Service, armed forces, military intelligence, prison administration, Supreme State Security Prosecution, and an executive-influenced judiciary. These were durable institutions with operational autonomy, but the evidence shows neither meaningful civilian independence from the presidency nor an effective institution capable of holding politically protected security personnel accountable.

### Inherited baseline

Sisi entered 2022 after nearly nine years of rule, not as a new ruler inheriting another government’s system. The relevant baseline was therefore largely his own accumulated record:

- mass political imprisonment and prolonged pretrial detention;
- recurring enforced disappearance and torture allegations;
- emergency courts and terrorism prosecutions used against peaceful critics;
- prisons largely closed to independent monitoring;
- a long-running North Sinai insurgency accompanied by displacement, demolition, civilian-harm allegations, and severe reporting restrictions;
- a public-order system in which peaceful protest had become rare because of arrest risk.

The nationwide state of emergency formally ended in October 2021, but contemporaneous legal reporting found that legislation enacted around its termination retained analogous presidential and military powers.

### Shocks and constraints

The continuing ISIS-Sinai insurgency was a real physical-security threat. The State Department counted at least 95 reported terrorist incidents and roughly 260 casualties in Sinai, while also finding a decline in terrorist activity compared with previous years. Its report expressly warned that casualty figures could not be confirmed because of restricted reporting. The Russia–Ukraine and currency shocks contributed to November protest calls, but do not excuse preventive arrests of peaceful critics and are not otherwise treated as chapter evidence.

### Information environment

Independent access to prisons and North Sinai was highly restricted. Domestic monitors operated under surveillance, prosecution, travel bans, asset freezes, and closure pressure. Complaint and arrest counts are documented cases, not prevalence estimates. Sparse reporting cannot be treated as evidence of safety.

The supplied local D12 prior says PTS, CIRIGHTS, V-Dem and UCDP all establish a severe national physical-integrity/repression context, but no underlying country-year rows were supplied, and I could not reliably extract exact 2022 values from their live interfaces. I therefore retain that prior as context, not as a fully extracted evidence record. UCDP’s thresholded one-sided-violence series also should not be treated as a complete measure of custodial deaths, disappearance, torture, or individual executions.

## Disposition of the ten lenses

### 3B.1 — Protection from state violence

The strongest evidence is adverse. Annual monitoring found credible reports of arbitrary killing, disappearance, torture, life-threatening detention conditions, and political imprisonment, with only exceptional prosecutions (WEB-3B-002). Hadhoud’s case supplies a detailed institutional example (WEB-3B-003), while the mass trial demonstrates exemplary punishment and deficient due process (WEB-3B-007). Pardoned releases are genuine contrary evidence but were numerically and structurally limited (WEB-3B-004).

### 3B.2 — Abuse by coercive services and aligned actors

Police, National Security, prisons, military forces, and army-aligned militias were all implicated. Accountability was exceptional, and Sisi reportedly pardoned 13 convicted police officers in three custodial-death cases (WEB-3B-005). Verified Sinai footage implicated army and aligned militia members without a reported investigation (WEB-3B-008).

### 3B.3 — Personal incitement or dehumanization

Sisi publicly described the Muslim Brotherhood as “people of evil” and spoke of a “cursed alliance” of evil and destruction (WEB-3B-015). That rhetoric supported political scapegoating and blurred the distinction between armed threats and the wider opposition. However, this research did **not** locate a 2022 speech in which he explicitly ordered violence, revenge, or private attacks against a protected group or peaceful opponent. The personal-incitement finding should therefore remain narrower than the institutional-repression finding.

### 3B.4 — Due process, complaints, oversight, investigation

Formal complaint routes, prosecutors, courts, a human-rights strategy, pardon committee, and dialogue existed. In practice, prosecutors repeatedly failed to investigate security-force abuse; prisons lacked independent monitoring; emergency judgments lacked ordinary appeal; and politically protected actors enjoyed impunity (WEB-3B-002, 003, 005, 007, 014). Hadhoud’s family pursued prosecution and court review, but authorities withheld evidence and the appeal was rejected.

### 3B.5 — Emergency, terrorism, surveillance, and administrative controls

The formal emergency ended before 2022, but successor legislation preserved curfew, evacuation, military infrastructure-protection, and other exceptional powers. Terrorism accusations and surveillance were applied to suspected peaceful protest and online activity (WEB-3B-002, 006, 007, 013). This was not a pattern of narrow counterterrorism confined to armed actors.

### 3B.6 — Fear and insecurity

ISIS activity reportedly declined, a real favorable outcome (WEB-3B-009). But that improvement coexisted with nationwide fear of arrest, disappearance, surveillance, and punishment for dissent (WEB-3B-002, 006). Counterinsurgency also involved aligned-force executions (WEB-3B-008). The evidence therefore does not support treating reduced insurgent capacity as an unqualified reduction of domestic fear.

### 3B.7 — Women, children, minorities, and vulnerable groups

The record is mixed but predominantly inadequate:

- authorities prevented some FGM procedures and obtained convictions under strengthened law;
- police and courts responded to several individual sectarian killings;
- Eritrean adults and children were detained and expelled without individualized protection;
- violence against women remained widespread without comprehensive legislation;
- LGBT people faced state entrapment and abusive examinations;
- some Copts and refugees faced identity violence and barriers to protection.

See WEB-3B-010 through WEB-3B-013. Child defendants also appeared in the torture-tainted mass trial (WEB-3B-007).

### 3B.8 — Peaceful protest and organization

The November crackdown is direct evidence that authorities treated mere responsiveness to protest calls as grounds for preventive arrest and terrorism investigation. Nearly 700 detentions were documented across 18 governorates, including about 40 people whose whereabouts were initially unknown (WEB-3B-006). Civil-society investigations, bans, and retaliation also undermined the announced “year of civil society” (WEB-3B-014).

### 3B.9 — Crisis and flare-up response

Two different crisis responses matter. Against ISIS, authorities degraded the organization and investigated or prosecuted alleged attackers, but aligned forces committed apparent executions and reporting remained opaque (WEB-3B-008, 009). Against spontaneous protest calls, the response was preventive nationwide detention, surveillance, intimidation, and terrorism accusations rather than restrained crowd protection (WEB-3B-006).

### 3B.10 — Inherited-to-end safety

Compared with the beginning of 2022, ISIS attack frequency and severity reportedly declined. Some detainees were released and a limited number of vulnerable-group offenders were prosecuted. But the inherited repression apparatus remained intact: disappearance, torture, custodial deaths, political detention, protest suppression, and impunity continued, while new arrests exceeded releases in the documented comparison. There is no defensible evidence that residents as a whole ended 2022 safer from political violence and state coercion, though they may have faced a weakened insurgent threat in parts of Sinai.
