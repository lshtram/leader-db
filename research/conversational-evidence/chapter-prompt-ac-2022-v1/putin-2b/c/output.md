## Research scope and conflict gate

Vladimir Putin was Russia’s president and Supreme Commander-in-Chief throughout 2022. His authority included directing foreign policy and the armed forces. The decision to recognize the separatist entities, launch the full-scale invasion, mobilize reservists, and proclaim annexation was publicly announced or signed by him. That establishes strong ruler attribution for the central strategic choices, though it does not establish his personal direction of every battlefield violation.

The conflict/exposure gate is decisively met. Russia inherited an internationalized armed conflict in eastern Ukraine and its occupation of Crimea. On 24 February 2022, Putin expanded this into a multi-front interstate invasion. The year also involved annexation, coercive nuclear signaling, mobilization, negotiations, prisoner exchanges, and the Black Sea Grain Initiative.

A local-access limitation matters: the filesystem-backed research skill and the repository’s local D13 UCDP and D14 SIPRI records could not be opened because the local command runner was unavailable. I verified SIPRI’s public 2022 figures directly. UCDP’s download center was located, but no numerical UCDP conflict-death estimate is asserted because the relevant row could not be extracted and checked.

## Overall account

The strongest evidence establishes that Putin personally chose and publicly justified a full-scale cross-border military campaign despite pre-existing diplomatic mechanisms and without a demonstrated defensive necessity. Russia then continued operations after the International Court of Justice ordered their suspension, attempted to annex four partly occupied Ukrainian regions, and escalated through mobilization and attacks on energy infrastructure.

The inherited setting was not peaceful. The Donbas conflict had persisted since 2014, Minsk implementation was stalled, ceasefire violations were substantial, NATO-Russia relations were deteriorating, and Russian officials claimed security fears arising from NATO enlargement and military cooperation with Ukraine. Those facts help explain a real security dispute; they do not demonstrate that Ukraine was conducting or imminently preparing an armed attack requiring a nationwide Russian invasion. Immediately before the invasion, OHCHR recorded fewer civilian casualties in December 2021 and January 2022 than earlier in the reporting period. Diplomatic channels—including Minsk, the Trilateral Contact Group, Normandy-format diplomacy, and bilateral contacts—still existed.

Russia participated during 2022 in talks, a large prisoner exchange, and the Black Sea Grain Initiative. These are material favorable or limiting facts. However, they produced bounded humanitarian benefits rather than a general ceasefire or lawful settlement. Evidence about why the March–April peace talks failed is contested: draft texts show substantial engagement and some convergence, but unresolved security guarantees, territorial issues, force limitations, battlefield developments, atrocities discovered around Kyiv, and mutual distrust remained. The claim that a complete peace agreement was available and rejected solely because of Western pressure is not established by the evidence reviewed here.

## Fully extracted evidence records

### WEB-2B-001 — Inherited Donbas conflict remained serious but was not at nationwide-war intensity

OHCHR reported that the period through 31 January 2022 remained affected by the Donbas conflict: the OSCE mission recorded 62,562 ceasefire violations from August 2021 through January 2022, and OHCHR recorded 49 civilian casualties—11 killed and 38 injured—during that six-month period. Yet ceasefire violations and civilian harm decreased considerably in December 2021 and January 2022. This establishes a dangerous inherited conflict while limiting claims that the monitored humanitarian situation itself demonstrated an immediately escalating nationwide emergency.

- Strongest source: *Report on the Human Rights Situation in Ukraine, 1 August 2021–31 January 2022*, OHCHR, 28 March 2022, [direct PDF](https://ukraine.un.org/sites/default/files/2022-03/33rdReportUkraine-en.pdf), paras. 26–28 and footnote 8.
- Period fit: inherited baseline immediately preceding the invasion.
- Attribution: Putin inherited and had substantial influence over Russia’s Ukraine policy and Russian-supported formations, but the statistics do not assign individual ceasefire violations.
- Limits: OSCE totals count incidents, not initiators; the conflict was active, Minsk implementation was deeply impaired, and late-February violence rose after this reporting cutoff.
- Lenses: 2B.1, 2B.2, 2B.3, 2B.10.

### WEB-2B-002 — Putin publicly ordered and justified the 24 February invasion

Putin’s televised address announced a “special military operation,” invoked collective self-defense of the separatist entities, alleged genocide in Donbas, and stated “demilitarization” and “denazification” objectives. The operation attacked Ukraine far beyond Donbas. The speech is direct evidence of ruler authority and of the stated threat narrative, but an official statement is evidence of what Putin claimed, not that the claims were true.

- Strongest source: *Address by the President of the Russian Federation*, President of Russia, 24 February 2022, official Kremlin transcript; the address is also reproduced and analyzed in the ICJ’s 16 March order, paras. 31 and 59–60.
- Independent legal record: [ICJ Order of 16 March 2022](https://www.icj-cij.org/node/106135).
- Period fit: direct 2022 decision.
- Attribution: direct and very strong.
- Contrary/limiting evidence: Russia cited NATO expansion, the Donbas war, separatist requests, and Article 51. The ICJ did not decide all jus ad bellum issues at the provisional-measures stage, but stated it was doubtful that the Genocide Convention authorized unilateral force and found no evidence before it substantiating Russia’s genocide allegation.
- Lenses: 2B.1, 2B.2, 2B.3, 2B.6, 2B.10.

### WEB-2B-003 — Broad international finding of aggression and demand for withdrawal

On 2 March, the UN General Assembly voted 141–5 to deplore Russia’s aggression, demand an immediate end to its use of force and unconditional withdrawal, and demand reversal of the 21 February recognition decisions. This is not a judicial judgment, but it is the clearest contemporaneous multilateral assessment of the invasion’s incompatibility with the UN Charter.

- Strongest source: *Aggression against Ukraine*, UN General Assembly Resolution ES-11/1, 2 March 2022; [UN meeting record](https://press.un.org/en/2022/ga12407.doc.htm).
- Period fit: direct 2022.
- Attribution: strong for Putin’s publicly announced strategic decision.
- Limits: General Assembly resolutions are authoritative political determinations rather than binding judicial merits decisions; Russia and four other states voted against.
- Lenses: 2B.1, 2B.2, 2B.3, 2B.10.

### WEB-2B-004 — Russia continued the war after binding ICJ provisional measures

On 16 March, the ICJ ordered Russia to suspend the military operations begun on 24 February, ensure that directed or supported regular and irregular forces did not continue them, and avoid aggravating the dispute. Russia did not suspend its operations. This is strong evidence concerning lawful restraint and institutional accountability, although the order did not finally decide the merits or every legal basis asserted by Russia.

- Strongest source: *Allegations of Genocide under the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (Ukraine v. Russian Federation), Order of 16 March 2022*, International Court of Justice, 16 March 2022, [operative paragraphs 86(1)–(3)](https://www.icj-cij.org/node/106135).
- Period fit: direct 2022.
- Attribution: strong; suspension of a presidentially launched war was within Putin’s practical command authority.
- Contrary/limiting evidence: two judges dissented from the first two measures; jurisdiction and merits were expressly left open.
- Lenses: 2B.1, 2B.2, 2B.5, 2B.7, 2B.9, 2B.10.

### WEB-2B-005 — Documented killings of civilians in Russian-controlled northern Ukraine

OHCHR documented killings during Russian control of parts of Kyiv, Chernihiv, and Sumy regions. In Bucha alone it documented 73 civilian killings and was corroborating 105 more. OHCHR found strong indications that documented summary executions could constitute the war crime of willful killing. This is direct evidence of grave failures of civilian protection.

- Strongest source: *Killings of civilians: summary executions and attacks on individual civilians in Kyiv, Chernihiv, and Sumy regions*, OHCHR/UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine, 7 December 2022, [report page](https://ukraine.ohchr.org/en/killings-civilians-summary-executions-and-attacks-individual-civilians-EN).
- Period fit: contemporaneous 2022 report about February–April conduct.
- Attribution: strong to Russian forces as an institution; indirect to Putin unless command orders, knowledge, tolerance, or failure to remedy are separately shown.
- Contrary/limiting evidence: Russia denied responsibility for Bucha. OHCHR used corroboration standards and left additional cases unverified; exact national totals were necessarily incomplete.
- Lenses: 2B.4, 2B.5, 2B.9, 2B.10.

### WEB-2B-006 — Broad patterns of Russian war crimes, with some Ukrainian violations also documented

The UN Commission of Inquiry later found that Russian authorities committed war crimes including willful killings, attacks on civilians, unlawful confinement, torture, rape and other sexual violence, and unlawful transfers or deportations of children. It described some detention and torture practices as widespread or systematic and found attacks using explosive weapons in populated areas with apparent disregard for civilian harm. The Commission also documented a small number of Ukrainian violations, including likely indiscriminate attacks and abuse of Russian prisoners.

- Strongest source: *Report of the Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine*, UN Human Rights Council commission, 16 March 2023, [summary report](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/hrbodies/hrcouncil/coiukraine/coI-ukraine-graphic_report-en.pdf), “Main Findings.”
- Detailed source: [A/HRC/52/62](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/hrbodies/hrcouncil/coiukraine/A_HRC_52_62_AUV_EN.pdf).
- Period fit: later retrospective findings about 2022.
- Attribution: high to Russian authorities and armed forces; variable and generally indirect to Putin for individual crimes.
- Limits: later fact-finding, restricted access to Russian-held territory and Russian official cooperation; “may amount to crimes against humanity” is not a final criminal conviction.
- Lenses: 2B.4, 2B.5, 2B.9, 2B.10.

### WEB-2B-007 — Escalatory campaign against energy infrastructure

On 10 October, Putin publicly announced strikes that included energy infrastructure. OHCHR reported that the attacks killed at least 12 civilians, injured more than 100, and damaged residential buildings and at least 12 energy facilities across eight regions; their timing and apparent targeting raised serious distinction, precaution, and proportionality concerns. Later UN investigation treated the waves of energy attacks as a distinct campaign and stated that aspects may amount to crimes against humanity.

- Strongest contemporaneous source: *Briefing note on Ukraine*, OHCHR, 11 October 2022, [direct report](https://ukraine.ohchr.org/en/briefing-note-spokesperson-un-high-commissioner-human-rights-ravina-shamdasani-ukraine-UA).
- Retrospective corroboration: [UN Commission report A/HRC/52/62](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/hrbodies/hrcouncil/coiukraine/A_HRC_52_62_AUV_EN.pdf), paras. concerning attacks beginning 10 October.
- Period fit: direct 2022 plus later legal assessment.
- Attribution: strong for the campaign-level choice because Putin publicly announced the attacks; specific targeting and proportionality judgments remain operation-specific.
- Contrary/limiting evidence: Russia described energy and command-related facilities as supporting Ukraine’s war effort. Dual-use status can make an installation a military objective, but does not remove proportionality and precaution duties.
- Lenses: 2B.2, 2B.4, 2B.5, 2B.6, 2B.9, 2B.10.

### WEB-2B-008 — POW access was obstructed, but a major exchange occurred

In October the ICRC said it had visited hundreds of POWs but lacked access to thousands more, including at Olenivka, despite being ready for months. Immediate access is required by the Third Geneva Convention. Separately, Russia and Ukraine completed an exchange of more than 250 POWs on 21 September, which the UN welcomed and credited to both parties.

- Strongest source on access: *Russia-Ukraine international armed conflict: ICRC asks for immediate and unimpeded access to all prisoners of war*, ICRC, 14 October 2022, [lines on access and legal obligation](https://www.icrc.org/en/document/ukraine-russia-icrc-asks-immediate-and-unimpeded-access-to-all-prisoners-of-war).
- Favorable corroboration: *Statement attributable to the Spokesperson for the Secretary-General—on Ukraine*, UN, 21 September 2022, [exchange statement](https://ukraine.un.org/en/200296-statement-attributable-spokesperson-secretary-general-%E2%80%93-ukraine).
- Period fit: direct 2022.
- Attribution: detention access involved authorities and local implementation under Russia and Russia-aligned entities; evidence does not prove Putin personally denied a particular visit. The exchange required high-level consent but was multilateral.
- Limits: the ICRC deliberately speaks neutrally and its statement addressed access across the conflict, not only Russian-held POWs.
- Lenses: 2B.4, 2B.5, 2B.7, 2B.9.

### WEB-2B-009 — Negotiations produced a framework but no verified completed peace agreement

Russian and Ukrainian delegations held talks in Belarus, virtually, and in Istanbul. By late March and April, documents contemplated Ukrainian neutrality, security guarantees, limits on foreign military activity, and further negotiations, but territorial questions, force limits, guarantor commitments, and implementation remained unresolved. Russia reduced operations around Kyiv amid military reversals and negotiations. The talks ended without a ceasefire.

- Strong contemporaneous analytical source: Sabine Fischer, *Peace Talks Between Russia and Ukraine: Mission Impossible*, Stiftung Wissenschaft und Politik, November 2022, [SWP Comment 65](https://www.swp-berlin.org/publications/products/comments/2022C65_PeaceTalksRussia_Ukraine.pdf).
- Additional reporting: Reuters reported, through three sources close to Russian leadership, that Putin rejected a provisional neutrality arrangement advanced by Dmitry Kozak and pursued broader objectives; [republished Reuters report](https://m.investing.com/news/world-news/exclusiveas-war-began-putin-rejected-a-ukraine-peace-deal-recommended-by-his-aide-sources-2891610).
- Period fit: 2022 evidence and contemporaneous analysis.
- Attribution: Putin necessarily controlled Russia’s ultimate settlement position; the Kozak episode, if accurate, connects rejection directly to him.
- Contrary/limiting evidence: Reuters relied on anonymous sources and reported conflicting timing accounts. Later examination of draft texts confirms serious talks but not a finalized, guaranteed agreement. Claims that one outside actor simply vetoed an otherwise complete deal are therefore too strong.
- Lenses: 2B.1, 2B.2, 2B.3, 2B.7, 2B.9.

### WEB-2B-010 — Russia joined bounded humanitarian arrangements

Russia, Ukraine, Türkiye, and the UN launched the Black Sea Grain Initiative on 22 July, establishing a monitored maritime corridor for Ukrainian food exports. Russia agreed to its November renewal. Russia also participated in POW exchanges. These show that negotiated, limited cooperation remained possible during the war.

- Strongest source: *Black Sea Grain Initiative—FAQ*, United Nations, updated institutional history, [agreement history](https://www.un.org/en/black-sea-grain-initiative/faq).
- Contemporaneous corroboration: *Statement of the Secretary-General on renewal*, UN, 17 November 2022, [renewal statement](https://www.un.org/sg/en/content/sg/statements/2022-11-17/statement-of-the-secretary-general-the-renewal-of-the-black-sea-grain-initiative).
- Period fit: direct 2022.
- Attribution: moderate to strong at state-policy level; Putin’s personal role in negotiating individual provisions is not established.
- Limits: this was a narrow humanitarian-commercial arrangement, not a ceasefire; Türkiye and the UN were central brokers, and implementation disputes continued.
- Lenses: 2B.1, 2B.7, 2B.9, 2B.10.

### WEB-2B-011 — Putin ordered mobilization and expanded war production after battlefield reversals

On 21 September, Putin ordered partial mobilization, directed regions to assist recruitment, and required defense-industrial enterprises to increase weapons production and capacity. SIPRI later estimated Russian military spending at $86.4 billion in 2022, up 9.2% in real terms and from 3.7% to 4.1% of GDP. Actual national-defense spending was 34% above the nominal level planned in 2021.

- Strongest policy source: *Address by the President of the Russian Federation*, President of Russia, 21 September 2022, [official Russian transcript](https://special.kremlin.ru/catalog/countries/UA/events/69390).
- Strongest expenditure source: *World military expenditure reaches new record high as European spending surges*, SIPRI, 24 April 2023, [Russia section](https://www.sipri.org/media/press-release/2023/world-military-expenditure-reaches-new-record-high-european-spending-surges).
- Period fit: direct 2022 policy; later statistical estimate of 2022.
- Attribution: direct for mobilization and production orders; strong at budget-policy level.
- Contrary/limiting evidence: a state in a large ongoing war has genuine manpower and materiel needs. SIPRI warned that Russian expenditure became increasingly opaque. The sources establish escalation and cost, but not whether particular contracts enriched patronage networks.
- Lenses: 2B.2, 2B.3, 2B.8, 2B.10.

### WEB-2B-012 — Attempted annexation converted occupation into a territorial claim

Putin recognized Donetsk and Luhansk entities on 21 February and, after “referendums” conducted under occupation, proclaimed the incorporation of Donetsk, Luhansk, Kherson, and Zaporizhzhia regions. The General Assembly condemned the attempted annexations by 143–5 and demanded reversal. This strongly indicates territorial expansion beyond a narrowly framed defense of Donbas.

- Strongest source: *Territorial integrity of Ukraine: defending the principles of the Charter of the United Nations*, UNGA Resolution ES-11/4, 12 October 2022; [UN meeting coverage](https://press.un.org/en/2022/ga12458.doc.htm).
- Contemporaneous legal-context source: [UN Security Council briefing on the occupied-territory “referendums”](https://press.un.org/en/2022/sc15039.doc.htm), 27 September 2022.
- Period fit: direct 2022.
- Attribution: direct and very strong; Putin signed and publicly celebrated the claimed incorporations.
- Contrary/limiting evidence: Russia asserted self-determination and popular support. The UN political-affairs chief found that votes held during armed conflict and occupation could not constitute genuine popular will or legal acquisition of territory.
- Lenses: 2B.2, 2B.3, 2B.6, 2B.7, 2B.9, 2B.10.

### WEB-2B-013 — End-of-year humanitarian and regional-security deterioration

By the end of 2022, 11.6 million Ukrainians remained displaced—5.9 million internally and 5.7 million abroad. Finland and Sweden, previously militarily non-aligned, applied to join NATO in May; NATO records that Finland’s position changed following Russia’s invasion. These are major end-state indicators of regional destabilization and failure to achieve Russia’s publicly stated objective of limiting NATO’s strategic position.

- Strongest humanitarian source: *Global Trends 2022*, UNHCR, June 2023, [Ukraine displacement figures](https://www.unhcr.org/publications/global-trends-2022).
- Regional-security source: *Relations with Finland*, NATO, institutional history, [road to accession](https://www.nato.int/en/what-we-do/partnerships-and-cooperation/relations-with-finland).
- Period fit: 2022 outcomes, reported or consolidated later.
- Attribution: strong causal connection to the invasion at aggregate level, but displacement also arose from Ukrainian defensive operations and the interaction of both armed forces; NATO applications were sovereign Finnish and Swedish decisions.
- Limits: NATO is an interested institutional source regarding enlargement. It nevertheless reports formal applications and Finland’s stated policy transition accurately.
- Lenses: 2B.3, 2B.8, 2B.10.

### WEB-2B-014 — Later ICC process connected alleged child transfers to Putin personally

The ICC opened its Ukraine investigation in March 2022. On 17 March 2023, its Pre-Trial Chamber issued an arrest warrant finding reasonable grounds to believe Putin bore individual criminal responsibility for the alleged war crimes of unlawful deportation and transfer of Ukrainian children from occupied areas.

- Strongest source: *Situation in Ukraine*, International Criminal Court, investigation and warrant entries, [case page](https://www.icc-cpi.int/situations/ukraine).
- Period fit: later retrospective legal action concerning conduct beginning in 2022.
- Attribution: unusually direct, but only at the “reasonable grounds” warrant stage.
- Contrary/limiting evidence: this is not a conviction or final factual judgment. Russia rejects ICC jurisdiction and disputes the characterization of the transfers.
- Lenses: 2B.4, 2B.5, 2B.9.

## Opened corroboration retained separately

- The April and July 2022 OSCE Moscow Mechanism reports found clear patterns of international humanitarian-law violations by Russian forces and also addressed violations attributed to Ukraine. They corroborate the UN findings but add limited distinct ruler-level attribution: [first report](https://odihr.osce.org/odihr/515868), [second mission notice](https://www.osce.org/permanent-council/522691).
- The UN General Assembly’s 2 March and 12 October votes independently establish the breadth of international rejection, but voting totals should not be treated as judicial proof.
- SIPRI’s detailed chapter emphasizes that Russia’s exact 2022 expenditure is uncertain because fiscal transparency worsened after the invasion: [SIPRI Yearbook extract](https://www.sipri.org/sites/default/files/SIPRIYB23c05sII.pdf).
- UNHCR’s 2022 Global Report gives equivalent year-end displacement figures and program context: [Ukraine situation report](https://reporting.unhcr.org/ukraine-situation-global-report-2022).
- NATO’s 18 May contemporaneous notice confirms the date and joint nature of the Finnish and Swedish applications: [NATO announcement](https://www.nato.int/en/news-and-events/articles/news/2022/05/18/finland-and-sweden-submit-applications-to-join-nato).

## Uninspected or incompletely resolved leads

- Exact UCDP 2022 battle-death figures should be extracted from the project’s local D13 artifact, with the UCDP version, dyad, best/low/high estimates, and whether deaths in separatist dyads are separately counted. The public [UCDP download center](https://ucdp.uu.se/downloads/) was located but not sufficient here for a checked numerical claim.
- PA-X should be checked for the Istanbul communiqué, Black Sea Grain Initiative, and any 2022 localized ceasefire or humanitarian arrangements.
- Russian Security Council minutes and military orders could clarify dissent, objectives, targeting policy, proxy command, and whether Putin received notice of recurring violations.
- Procurement records and independent audits are needed for 2B.8’s patronage component. Aggregate SIPRI spending cannot establish enrichment.
- A comprehensive inventory of 2022 prisoner exchanges, humanitarian corridors, local ceasefires, and Russian compliance would refine favorable evidence.
- Further Russian-language investigation is warranted into military prosecutors’ cases, discipline, compensation, and official responses to Bucha, Olenivka, torture allegations, and unlawful transfers.
- The exact March–April negotiating drafts merit document-level comparison. Later scholarship is valuable but must remain retrospective and should not be used to assert that a completed settlement existed without resolving guarantor and territorial terms.

## Rejected or unsuitable sources

- Wikipedia pages and Reddit reproductions were not used as evidence because primary, institutional, or original reporting was available.
- Responsible Statecraft’s summary of Reuters was not used as the principal source; the Reuters report itself was located. Even Reuters remains cautiously framed because its key Kozak evidence relied on anonymous sources and disputed timing.
- Later Kremlin claims that Russia withdrew from Kyiv solely as a peace gesture were not accepted as an established fact. They are retrospective belligerent claims and conflict with the documented battlefield context.
- US Treasury’s description of Russia’s recognition rationale as “false” was not used to prove falsity because Treasury was acting as a sanctions authority and political belligerent. The recognition sequence is better shown by Putin’s acts and UN records.
- NATO statements characterizing Russia’s operational performance were not used for battlefield factual findings; NATO was retained only for formal accession facts and Finland’s documented policy transition.
- Unverified belligerent casualty totals were excluded.
- The 2024 OHCHR energy-infrastructure study was retained only conceptually as a later lead; the contemporaneous October 2022 OHCHR notice and 2023 Commission findings were sufficient for the developed record.

## Remaining uncertainty and likely research value

The central strategic record—initiation, expansion, legal rejection, annexation, mobilization, humanitarian harm, and regional deterioration—is well established. Further broad searching is likely to repeat it.

Additional research could still add material information in four narrower areas:

- document-level reconstruction of pre-invasion alternatives and the Istanbul talks;
- Putin’s knowledge of, instructions concerning, or response to recurring battlefield violations;
- proxy and occupation-authority command relationships;
- defense procurement, patronage, and military accountability.

The record is weakest on internal deliberations, actual disciplinary measures, military-contract beneficiaries, and the degree of Putin’s personal involvement below campaign level. Closed decision-making and wartime censorship make an absence of domestic documentation non-exculpatory.
