# Vladimir Putin — Russian Federation, 2022 evidence dossier

## 1. Overview

Vladimir Vladimirovich Putin was president of the Russian Federation throughout 2022, serving the fourth presidential term he began in 2018. Constitutionally, the president was head of state, determined the main directions of domestic and foreign policy, commanded the armed forces, appointed senior officials, and chaired the Security Council.

In practice, Putin was the dominant decision-maker in an increasingly personalist authoritarian system. Parliament, courts, the governing United Russia party, security agencies, and most regional executives generally implemented or legitimized presidential policy rather than independently constraining it. The Security Council and military leadership supplied advice and operational capacity, but Putin publicly assumed responsibility for the invasion of Ukraine and personally issued mobilization and martial-law measures.

His most important constraints were practical rather than constitutional: shortcomings in military planning and execution; Ukraine’s resistance; foreign military assistance to Ukraine; international sanctions and diplomatic isolation; dependence on military, security, economic, and regional bureaucracies; and the risk that mobilization or deteriorating living standards could provoke domestic resistance. These constraints changed implementation, but did not prevent him from initiating or continuing the war.

The defining event was Putin’s decision to launch the full-scale invasion of Ukraine on 24 February. Russia failed to overthrow Ukraine’s government or seize Kyiv, later lost substantial occupied territory, and resorted to mobilization, annexation claims, and large-scale attacks on Ukrainian energy infrastructure. At home, the authorities contained the immediate economic shock better than early forecasts suggested, while sharply intensifying censorship, repression, propaganda, and restrictions on independent scrutiny.

## 2. Evidence records

### E01 — Putin held constitutionally and practically dominant power

- **Fact and significance:** Putin was president, head of state, commander-in-chief, chair of the Security Council, and the central authority over foreign and security policy. Parliament and courts provided few effective checks. This makes high-level war, mobilization, annexation, and repression policies strongly attributable to him.
- **Strongest opened source:** “Russia: Nations in Transit 2023 Country Report,” Freedom House, 2023, [direct link](https://freedomhouse.org/country/russia/nations-transit/2023).
- **Stable support:** “National Democratic Governance,” “Judicial Framework and Independence,” and “Local Democratic Governance”; particularly the findings that courts legitimized executive decisions, the Constitutional Court approved the annexation treaties, and regional autonomy remained curtailed.
- **Time:** Principally 2022, resting on an inherited concentration of power.
- **Attribution:** **Very strong.** Putin held the presidency, chaired and appointed the Security Council, and personally issued the year’s central decrees.
- **Complication:** The military, FSB, National Guard, central bank, economic ministries, oligarchic networks, and regional governments retained operational responsibilities and could shape implementation. Battlefield failures show that presidential authority did not guarantee administrative competence.
- **Caution:** Freedom House is an external democracy-monitoring organization funded substantially from the United States and applies a normative liberal-democratic framework.
- **Independent support:** Freedom House’s separate 2023 country assessment states that power was concentrated in Putin’s hands and that pro-Kremlin groups controlled parliamentary output. The U.S. Treasury describes the Putin-chaired Security Council as a central foreign-policy body, although Treasury is a belligerent-state sanctions authority.

### E02 — Putin personally announced and justified the full-scale invasion

- **Fact and significance:** On 24 February 2022, Putin announced a “special military operation,” invoking security threats and objectives including the “demilitarization” and “denazification” of Ukraine. The address is direct evidence of his ownership of the decision and its stated goals.
- **Strongest opened source:** “Address by the President of the Russian Federation,” President of Russia/Kremlin, 24 February 2022, listed in the Kremlin’s [transcript archive](https://en.kremlin.ru/events/president/transcripts/by-date/21.02.2022). The official page produced a retrieval error during this session, but the underlying Kremlin archive entry was located and examined through its indexed transcript.
- **Stable support:** Address dated 24 February 2022, 06:00, Kremlin, Moscow.
- **Time:** 2022.
- **Attribution:** **Direct and maximal.** It is Putin’s own announcement.
- **Complication:** Putin presented the action as defensive and linked it to NATO expansion, Donbas, and Russian security. These claims should be preserved as the government’s stated rationale, not accepted as independently established facts. The Federation Council formally authorized the use of force, and military institutions devised operational plans.
- **Caution:** This is an official advocacy document, valuable for orders and stated intent but unreliable as independent evidence of Ukrainian or Western conduct.
- **Independent support:** The UN General Assembly’s emergency-session record; contemporary international reporting; Putin’s earlier July 2021 essay concerning Russians and Ukrainians.

### E03 — Initial war aims were not achieved, prompting escalation and annexation claims

- **Fact and significance:** Russian forces failed to capture Kyiv or overthrow Ukraine’s government, withdrew from northern Ukraine, and later lost substantial territory in the Kharkiv and Kherson counteroffensives. Russia nevertheless claimed to annex four Ukrainian regions it did not fully control. This is strong evidence of a major gap between apparent initial aims and achieved results.
- **Strongest opened source:** “Russia: Nations in Transit 2023 Country Report,” Freedom House, 2023, [direct link](https://freedomhouse.org/country/russia/nations-transit/2023).
- **Stable support:** Lines/sections concerning State Duma endorsement of annexation, Constitutional Court approval, and the military’s failure to exercise full control over the claimed territories.
- **Time:** 2022.
- **Attribution:** **Strong for strategic policy; shared for operational failure.** Putin ordered the invasion and signed the annexation measures; military commanders and intelligence organizations bear substantial responsibility for planning and execution.
- **Complication:** Russia did retain Crimea, much of the pre-2022 separatist-held Donbas, and a land corridor along the Azov coast at year-end. It also demonstrated an ability to sustain the war despite sanctions and losses. Therefore, “complete failure” would overstate the evidence.
- **Caution:** Freedom House is not a specialist battlefield-assessment body, and its report summarizes rather than reconstructs military operations.
- **Independent support:** UN Commission materials refer to Ukrainian counteroffensives recovering large areas; extensive contemporary mapping and military analysis by RUSI, IISS, ISW, and national intelligence agencies should be examined next.

### E04 — Putin ordered partial mobilization after battlefield reversals

- **Fact and significance:** Presidential Decree No. 647 declared partial mobilization from 21 September 2022. The decision expanded the domestic burden of the war and demonstrated that the original force was insufficient for Russia’s objectives.
- **Strongest opened source:** “Decree of the President of the Russian Federation of 21.09.2022 No. 647,” Official Internet Portal of Legal Information, 21 September 2022, [direct link](https://publication.pravo.gov.ru/Document/View/0001202209210001).
- **Stable support:** Operative paragraph 1: partial mobilization was declared from 21 September.
- **Time:** 2022.
- **Attribution:** **Direct.** A presidential decree signed by Putin.
- **Complication:** Putin’s accompanying address said the call-up would focus on reservists with relevant service and experience. Mobilization increased Russian manpower and helped stabilize parts of the front, even though implementation produced documented errors, protests, and emigration.
- **Caution:** The public decree omitted detailed numerical and eligibility limits; classified provisions and uneven regional implementation make its exact original scope difficult to reconstruct.
- **Independent support:** Freedom House documents the absence of practical alternative service and regionally unequal burdens; contemporary Russian and international reporting documented call-up practices.

### E05 — Russian forces committed war crimes in occupied areas

- **Fact and significance:** UN investigators documented summary executions and other unlawful killings by Russian forces in Kyiv, Chernihiv, and Sumy regions. In Bucha alone, the UN mission documented 73 civilian killings and was corroborating 105 more. This bears directly on command responsibility, military discipline, and protection of civilians.
- **Strongest opened source:** “UN report details summary executions of civilians by Russian troops in northern Ukraine from February to April,” UN Human Rights Monitoring Mission in Ukraine/OHCHR, 7 December 2022, [direct link](https://ukraine.ohchr.org/en/UN-report-details-summary-executions-of-civilians-by-Russian-troops-in-northern-Ukraine-from-February-to-April).
- **Stable support:** Paragraphs reporting 441 documented violent civilian deaths in the three northern regions, the Bucha figures, and “strong indications” that documented summary executions may constitute the war crime of wilful killing.
- **Time:** 2022.
- **Attribution:** **Strong to Russian forces; medium to Putin personally.** The invasion and overall chain of command are directly attributable to him, but individual criminal responsibility requires proof of orders, knowledge, failure to prevent, or failure to punish.
- **Complication:** The later UN Commission also documented a small number of Ukrainian violations, including likely indiscriminate attacks and mistreatment of prisoners. Those findings do not negate the much broader Russian pattern but are necessary context.
- **Caution:** Investigators lacked unrestricted access to all Russian-occupied territory and to Russian official records; the UN states that confirmed counts are minima.
- **Independent support:** UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry; Human Rights Watch investigations; satellite imagery, victim testimony, forensic examinations, and Ukrainian case files.

### E06 — Torture, sexual violence, deportations, and attacks on civilians formed a wider pattern

- **Fact and significance:** The UN Commission found that Russian authorities committed war crimes including wilful killing, attacks on civilians, unlawful confinement, torture, rape and other sexual violence, and unlawful transfer or deportation of children. The breadth and recurring methods suggest failures extending beyond isolated misconduct.
- **Strongest opened source:** “Report by the Independent International Commission of Inquiry on Ukraine,” OHCHR/UN Human Rights Council, March 2023, [direct PDF](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/hrbodies/hrcouncil/coiukraine/coI-ukraine-graphic_report-en.pdf).
- **Stable support:** “Main Findings” and “Fact-Finding Methodology”: 595 interviews, 56 localities visited, and enumerated violations.
- **Time:** **Later retrospective evidence about 2022.**
- **Attribution:** **Strong to Russian state forces and occupation authorities; medium to Putin individually.**
- **Complication:** The Commission recorded some Ukrainian violations. Establishing a formal state policy or Putin’s criminal responsibility requires command documents and evidence of knowledge or instruction.
- **Caution:** The graphic summary condenses a longer legal report; each allegation should ultimately be tied to the full report’s incident-level findings.
- **Independent support:** OHCHR periodic reports, Human Rights Watch, OSCE Moscow Mechanism reports, and subsequent Commission reports.

### E07 — Russia attacked Ukraine’s energy system on a large scale

- **Fact and significance:** Beginning on 10 October, Russian forces launched repeated missile and loitering-munition attacks against energy infrastructure. By 31 January 2023, OHCHR recorded 14 attack waves damaging or destroying at least 76 energy objects, killing at least 116 civilians and injuring at least 379. The campaign impaired electricity, heat, water, health care, education, and civilian welfare.
- **Strongest opened source:** “Report on the Human Rights Situation in Ukraine, 1 August 2022–31 January 2023,” OHCHR, 24 March 2023, [direct PDF](https://www.ohchr.org/sites/default/files/documents/countries/ukraine/2023/23-03-24-Ukraine-35th-periodic-report-ENG.pdf).
- **Stable support:** Section C, “Strikes on Critical Energy Infrastructure,” paragraph 39.
- **Time:** **Later retrospective report covering October–December 2022 and January 2023.**
- **Attribution:** **Strong at strategic level.** A coordinated nationwide campaign using scarce long-range weapons is unlikely to have occurred without top political-military authorization, although the opened report does not identify the individual who approved each target.
- **Complication:** Some energy objects may have served both civilian and military functions. OHCHR therefore assesses attacks individually and stresses distinction and proportionality rather than assuming every strike was unlawful.
- **Caution:** The reporting period extends one month beyond the target year; 2022 and January 2023 incidents must be separated in downstream extraction.
- **Independent support:** UN Commission report; Ukrainian energy-company records; satellite imagery; contemporaneous Russian Defence Ministry statements and strike claims.

### E08 — War censorship criminalized independent reporting and anti-war speech

- **Fact and significance:** On 4 March, parliament rapidly adopted and Putin signed laws criminalizing information about the armed forces that departed from official accounts and penalizing “discrediting” them, with potential imprisonment of up to 15 years. These laws sharply reduced media freedom and the reliability of domestic reporting.
- **Strongest opened source:** “World Report 2023: Russian Federation,” Human Rights Watch, 2023, [direct link](https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2023/country-chapters/russian-federation).
- **Stable support:** “Freedom of Expression,” especially the passage beginning “On March 4.”
- **Time:** 2022.
- **Attribution:** **Very strong.** Putin signed the laws; the legislature, prosecutors, Roskomnadzor, police, and courts implemented them.
- **Complication:** Authorities claimed the laws protected the armed forces and society from deliberately false wartime information. Genuine wartime misinformation existed on all sides, but the laws’ dependence on official Defence Ministry information and broad “discrediting” offense went far beyond narrow correction of false statements.
- **Caution:** HRW is an advocacy organization and relies partly on Russian rights monitors whose activities were themselves restricted.
- **Independent support:** Amnesty International; RSF; Russian court records; Roskomnadzor blocking orders; prosecutions of Aleksei Gorinov, Ilya Yashin, and others.

### E09 — Anti-war and mobilization protests met mass detention and prosecution

- **Fact and significance:** Authorities arrested more than 15,000 anti-war protesters in the first month after the invasion, used police force, and opened thousands of administrative and hundreds of criminal cases. September mobilization protests, including in Dagestan, were also forcibly dispersed. This indicates limited tolerance for peaceful opposition and helps explain the absence of visible public constraint on Putin.
- **Strongest opened source:** “World Report 2023: Russian Federation,” Human Rights Watch, 2023, [direct link](https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2023/country-chapters/russian-federation).
- **Stable support:** “Freedom of Assembly.”
- **Time:** 2022.
- **Attribution:** **Strong at policy level; shared operationally.** Repression was carried out by police, Rosgvardia, prosecutors, and courts within a presidentially dominated system.
- **Complication:** Protest participation was real but did not represent a reliable census of public opinion. Fear, censorship, emigration, and legal risk suppressed participation, while some citizens supported the war.
- **Caution:** The arrest count originates substantially from civil-society monitoring and may contain duplicates or incomplete case resolution.
- **Independent support:** OVD-Info; Amnesty International’s report on suppression of protest monitoring; videos and court dockets.

### E10 — Independent media were blocked or driven into exile

- **Fact and significance:** Independent outlets closed, suspended domestic operations, or relocated after blocking orders and the new criminal laws. A later RSF-sponsored study estimated that 1,500–1,800 Russian journalists fled abroad after the invasion. This substantially worsened the information available about war losses, dissent, corruption, and policy performance.
- **Strongest opened source:** “RSF urges European governments to strengthen their support for independent Russian journalism,” Reporters Without Borders, 2023, [direct link](https://rsf.org/en/rsf-urges-european-governments-strengthen-their-support-independent-russian-journalism-resilient).
- **Stable support:** Passages reporting the 1,500–1,800 estimate and describing exile newsrooms’ continued Russian reporting.
- **Time:** **Later retrospective finding about the post-February 2022 exodus.**
- **Attribution:** **Strong to state policy; high indirect attribution to Putin.**
- **Complication:** Exile media remained active through websites, mirrors, YouTube, Telegram, and Russian sources; censorship reduced but did not eliminate independent reporting. Exile can also weaken local access and introduce selection effects.
- **Caution:** The estimate comes from an RSF-linked media-support study and is a range rather than a registration count.
- **Independent support:** HRW records blocking and closure of outlets; Amnesty documents journalists’ departure; individual newsroom announcements.

### E11 — Political competition and judicial independence were minimal

- **Fact and significance:** The governing system offered no credible institutional route for parliament, courts, opposition parties, or regional governments to reverse Putin’s war policy. Courts endorsed annexation and prosecuted anti-war speakers; opposition networks had already been excluded or criminalized.
- **Strongest opened source:** “Russia: Nations in Transit 2023 Country Report,” Freedom House, 2023, [direct link](https://freedomhouse.org/country/russia/nations-transit/2023).
- **Stable support:** Sections on national governance, electoral process, judiciary, and local governance.
- **Time:** 2022, substantially inherited.
- **Attribution:** **Strong.** The institutional structure developed over Putin’s long tenure, though many actors participated in maintaining it.
- **Complication:** The Communist Party, other “systemic” opposition parties, governors, bureaucracies, and courts were not literally controlled in every low-level matter. Their limited bargaining role should not be confused with an effective veto over core presidential policy.
- **Caution:** Freedom House’s scoring is expert-coded and partly judgment-based.
- **Independent support:** HRW’s accounts of politically significant prosecutions; the State Duma’s rapid passage of censorship laws; the Constitutional Court’s annexation decisions.

### E12 — The economy contracted much less than initially expected

- **Fact and significance:** The World Bank estimated that Russian GDP contracted by 2.1% in 2022 despite unusually severe sanctions. There was no banking collapse or mass unemployment. High energy receipts, capital controls, monetary tightening, trade redirection, import substitution, and fiscal measures helped contain the shock. This was a significant short-run implementation success.
- **Strongest opened source:** “Macro Poverty Outlook for Russian Federation: April 2023,” World Bank, April 2023, [document page](https://documents.worldbank.org/en/publication/documents-reports/documentdetail/099725104112326740).
- **Stable support:** Country summary: “Russia’s economy contracted by only 2.1 percent in 2022.”
- **Time:** **Later retrospective estimate of 2022.**
- **Attribution:** **Medium.** Putin set strategic policy, but the central bank, finance ministry, energy exporters, high commodity prices, continuing foreign demand, and sanction design were major independent causes.
- **Complication:** A smaller-than-feared contraction does not mean sanctions had no cost. Lost investment, technology access, human-capital flight, military spending, and reduced statistical transparency may create longer-term losses not visible in 2022 GDP.
- **Caution:** Russian national accounts were the principal underlying source; wartime restrictions and subsequent revisions complicate measurement. Aggregate GDP also says little about household distribution or service quality.
- **Independent support:** IMF World Economic Outlook; Russian central-bank decisions; reporting on the ruble, employment, and consumer availability.

### E13 — Military deaths were substantially underreported in official public information

- **Fact and significance:** A Mediazona–Meduza investigation using named deaths, the national probate registry, and excess male mortality estimated approximately 24,000–25,000 Russian military deaths by the end of 2022, compared with 14,119 names then identified in open sources. The discrepancy illustrates both the war’s human cost and the weakness of official transparency.
- **Strongest opened source:** “At least 47,000 Russian soldiers killed in Ukraine: A data investigation,” Mediazona and Meduza, 10 July 2023, [direct link](https://en.zona.media/article/2023/07/10/stats-trl).
- **Stable support:** Methodology section and paragraphs stating that 14,119 named deaths represented about 58% of an estimated 24,000–25,000 excess deaths by year-end 2022.
- **Time:** **Later retrospective estimate of 2022.**
- **Attribution:** **High for initiating and continuing the war; medium for individual losses; strong for the broader secrecy environment.**
- **Complication:** The estimate is not a battlefield count and excludes some categories, while excess mortality can include non-war causes. Conversely, named lists necessarily miss unpublicized deaths.
- **Caution:** Mediazona and Meduza are independent/exile outlets opposed to censorship; the method depends on assumptions linking probate cases and excess deaths to military fatalities. The authors disclose these assumptions and validate them against Rosstat data.
- **Independent support:** BBC Russian’s joint named-death project and cemetery work; local obituaries; probate records; later demographic studies.

### E14 — Accountability and elite conflicts of interest remained weak

- **Fact and significance:** Putin governed through a network linking senior state officials, security leaders, state firms, favored business figures, and family members. In 2022, meaningful domestic investigation of elite wealth or conflicts of interest was inhibited by captured institutions and repression of investigative media. This is evidence of weak accountability, not by itself proof that every sanctioned person committed corruption.
- **Strongest opened source:** “U.S. Treasury Escalates Sanctions on Russia for Its Atrocities in Ukraine,” U.S. Department of the Treasury, 6 April 2022, [direct link](https://home.treasury.gov/news/press-releases/jy0705).
- **Stable support:** Sections identifying Putin’s family, Security Council members, major banks, and the Security Council’s role; the page describes the council as chaired by Putin and composed of his appointees.
- **Time:** 2022, with inherited networks.
- **Attribution:** **Strong for appointments and accountability structure; variable for individual financial conduct.**
- **Complication:** Sanctions designations are administrative foreign-policy actions, not criminal judgments. Family relationship, political proximity, or sanctions exposure does not independently establish corruption or beneficial ownership.
- **Caution:** Treasury represented a government actively supporting Ukraine and imposing economic pressure on Russia. Its allegations require confirmation from property registries, court records, corporate filings, or independent investigations.
- **Independent support:** OCCRP, ICIJ offshore data, Anti-Corruption Foundation investigations, and European property and company registries—several of which remain leads below rather than fully extracted evidence here.

### E15 — Health, education, welfare, and essential-service evidence is comparatively weak

- **Fact and significance:** Available international data show Russia retained nationwide health, education, pension, and utility systems in 2022; there is no strong evidence in the opened sources of a general domestic service collapse. However, war mobilization, emigration, repression, propaganda in schools, and shifting expenditure created material risks to human capital and service quality.
- **Strongest opened source:** “Russian Federation,” WHO Global Health Observatory, current country page containing historical estimates, [direct link](https://www.who.int/data/gho/data/countries/country-details/GHO/russian-federation).
- **Stable support:** Country data and methodology notices; the page warns that changes in registration systems may affect mortality trends.
- **Time:** Mostly inherited conditions; some indicators lag 2022.
- **Attribution:** **Low to medium.** National outcomes reflect long-term policy, regional administration, the pandemic, demographics, and household behavior, not only Putin’s 2022 decisions.
- **Complication:** Freedom House reports that education became more politicized through war propaganda and that educators and students opposing the invasion were targeted. Russia also avoided the social-system collapse feared immediately after sanctions.
- **Caution:** The WHO page is data-oriented and does not provide a sufficiently specific 2022 service-performance assessment. This domain remains under-researched.
- **Independent support:** World Bank WDI, UNDP, Russian budget execution, Rosstat mortality and income tables, and regional service statistics should be examined at source.

## 3. Information environment and evidentiary limitations

Reliable information was unusually difficult to obtain.

- The March censorship laws made deviation from official military information punishable by long prison terms. Roskomnadzor blocked outlets and platforms, while journalists, activists, lawyers, and monitors faced prosecution or exile.
- The state’s description of the invasion as a “special military operation” and restrictions on reporting distorted both language and public knowledge.
- Russia published very limited military casualty information. Independent estimates must combine obituaries, cemetery visits, probate cases, demographic statistics, and foreign intelligence.
- Russian economic and social statistics did not disappear, but wartime publication restrictions, classification, revisions, import-price problems, and incentives to present resilience make fine-grained conclusions less secure.
- Ukrainian authorities, Western governments, and advocacy groups also had strategic or normative interests. Their claims require incident-level corroboration rather than acceptance based on alignment.
- UN investigators had much better access to Ukrainian-controlled areas than to Russia or many occupied territories. Confirmed civilian-harm counts are therefore minima and geographically uneven.
- Opinion polling was affected by fear, nonresponse, emigration, question wording, propaganda, and uncertainty over whether respondents could safely criticize the war.
- Global attention was heavily concentrated on Ukraine. Domestic Russian health, education, ordinary crime, regional welfare, and non-war public administration received much less independent investigation.
- The supplied national indicators are useful orientation, but they do not establish Putin’s individual responsibility and should not substitute for policy-specific evidence.

## 4. Questions for deeper research

1. What documentary or testimonial evidence exists about Putin’s personal invasion-planning process and the advice he received from intelligence and military officials?
2. Which original operational aims can be established beyond Putin’s public rhetoric, and when were they revised?
3. How did the central bank, finance ministry, energy revenues, capital controls, and sanctions loopholes each contribute to 2022 economic stabilization?
4. What happened to real household income, regional poverty, medicines, hospital staffing, school quality, pensions, and municipal utilities?
5. How unequal were mobilization and military deaths by ethnicity, region, income, prison recruitment, and regular-versus-contract status?
6. What orders, reporting chains, investigations, or disciplinary failures connect senior Russian officials to atrocities and attacks on civilian infrastructure?
7. Which assets or benefits can be reliably tied to Putin or immediate family through registries and ownership documents, rather than sanctions assertions?
8. How competitive were the September 2022 regional and municipal elections, including candidate exclusion, coercion, and fraud?
9. What polling methods can distinguish genuine war support from preference falsification and nonresponse?
10. How much authority did military leaders, the FSB, Rosgvardia, Ramzan Kadyrov, Yevgeny Prigozhin, governors, and state firms retain in their respective areas?

## 6. Promising leads still needing full inspection

These were located but not sufficiently opened or extracted to support a final evidence record:

- The full Russian Constitution as amended in 2020, especially Articles 80, 83, 87, 90, and 110: [English text](https://constitution.garant.ru/english/).
- Full Kremlin transcripts of Putin’s 21 February, 24 February, 21 September, and 30 September 2022 addresses.
- UN General Assembly resolutions ES‑11/1 on the invasion and ES‑11/4 on the attempted annexations, including vote records and explanatory statements.
- The complete September 2022 OSCE Moscow Mechanism report on repression in Russia; the document opened in this session was a later OSCE statement referring back to it, not the underlying report.
- RUSI’s operational studies of the Kyiv campaign, Russian force planning, command failures, and Ukrainian adaptation.
- IISS *Military Balance* and *Armed Conflict Survey* material for force structure, territorial control, and loss estimates.
- Russian Central Bank emergency decisions, inflation reports, and financial-stability review for the economic-policy response.
- Rosstat’s original 2022 GDP, household-income, mortality, poverty, and regional data releases, including revision histories.
- Russian federal budget execution for defense, health, education, pensions, and regional transfers.
- Levada Center’s 2022 polling questionnaires, response rates, mode changes, and experiments concerning war support and preference falsification.
- OCCRP, ICIJ Offshore Leaks, European land and corporate registries, and Anti-Corruption Foundation investigations concerning Putin-linked assets and elite patronage.
- Russian election commission protocols, Golos monitoring, and candidate-registration records for the September 2022 subnational elections.
- Original court judgments under Criminal Code Articles 207.3 and 280.3, rather than secondary summaries.
- Regional mobilization orders, complaints, casualty payments, and demographic breakdowns, particularly for Dagestan, Buryatia, Tuva, Bashkortostan, and occupied Ukrainian territories.
