# Evaluation

Scores assess the research artifacts only. “Underlying-source inspection” receives credit when the memo extracts operative text, page/paragraph locations, methodology, or evidentiary details—not merely when it names a promising source.

**Better memo: X, by a modest but meaningful margin.**

### Memo X strengths

- It discovers substantially more score-relevant material: invasion ownership, mobilization, strategic failure, atrocities, energy attacks, domestic repression, media exile, economic resilience, casualty opacity, elite accountability, and weakly covered social services.
- Attribution is unusually careful. For example, it distinguishes Putin’s direct responsibility for signing the mobilization decree from military implementation, and state-force responsibility for atrocities from the unproven elements of Putin’s individual criminal responsibility.
- It does not reduce the year to adverse evidence. The smaller-than-expected economic contraction, continued access through exile media, military stabilization after mobilization, and Russia’s retained territory are meaningful complications.
- Its information-environment analysis is strong and operationally useful: censorship, casualty opacity, restricted statistics, unequal UN access, polling distortions, and war-driven attention bias are treated separately.
- Several records show genuine underlying inspection. E04 identifies an operative paragraph in Decree No. 647; E05 gives documented and pending Bucha counts; E07 isolates the mixed 2022–January 2023 reporting window; E13 explains the probate/excess-mortality method.
- The deeper-research questions are specific enough to guide chapter work, especially those concerning the invasion-planning process, economic stabilization mechanisms, unequal mobilization burdens, command chains, and ownership records.

### Memo X defects

- Some evidence is repeated across records. Freedom House and HRW findings about concentrated power, courts, censorship, and repression recur in E01, E08, E09, and E11 without always adding a distinct underlying fact.
- Several “independent support” entries are prospective source lists, not inspected corroboration. RUSI, IISS, ISW, OCCRP, ICIJ, registries, and Russian budget records should not add much confidence until extracted.
- E02 is weaker than its presentation suggests: the direct Kremlin page reportedly failed, yet the memo says the transcript was examined through an indexed result. That is less secure than opening and locating the actual transcript.
- E03 relies principally on Freedom House for battlefield and territorial outcomes even though the memo itself recognizes that Freedom House is not a specialist military source.
- E14 provides limited material evidence about corruption or personal integrity. A U.S. Treasury sanctions release establishes networks and official allegations, but not ownership, corruption, or misconduct.
- E15 is largely a gap record. It is useful for scoping, but it should not receive the same evidentiary status as records based on specific 2022 findings.
- The dossier is somewhat overextended: the long corroboration and leads sections improve handoff value, but obscure which facts are actually established.

### Memo Y strengths

- Its accepted-unit structure is highly reusable. Canonical fact keys, dispositions, chapter mappings, locators, period fit, attribution limits, and contrary evidence make later deduplication and ledger ingestion easier.
- It distinguishes sources from facts better than X and avoids presenting uninspected leads as evidence units.
- Several records have good direct-source selection: the Constitution, Putin’s address, the mobilization decree, the UN General Assembly resolution, and UN investigations.
- Attribution limits are consistently explicit. The memo repeatedly refuses to convert state- or force-level findings into proof of Putin’s personal orders or criminal responsibility.
- It delivers a credible research core with much less apparent volume.

### Memo Y defects

- It is materially thinner. It omits X’s strongest additional findings on energy-infrastructure attacks, final economic outturn, military-death opacity, media exile, annexation and territorial reversals, and the limited state of social-service evidence.
- Balance is mostly implemented through short “contrary evidence” fields rather than through researched favorable or mitigating records. Economic resilience is represented by a contemporaneous forecast rather than the later 2022 outturn.
- Some locators are fragile or vague: “lines 609–613,” “lines 60–62,” and “article text…lines 7 and 12–18” appear tied to a particular extraction interface rather than stable document structure.
- The Time article is a weaker foundation for elite constraints and Kremlin information flow than official records, specialist studies, or multiple independently sourced investigations.
- The opening summary says UN findings strongly establish “state and force-level conduct,” but the individual records need more incident-level and command-structure differentiation before supporting the broader state-level formulation.
- Wildcard methodology mappings such as `1B.*` are less useful than mapping evidence to exact applicable lenses.
- Its information-environment analysis is correct but compressed; it misses X’s treatment of statistical restrictions, geographic access bias, casualty reconstruction, polling limitations, and unequal topical attention.

### Prompt decision

The difference is large enough to favor X’s prompt if the goal is reconnaissance that maximizes useful material for later deep research. It is not large enough to discard Y’s approach wholesale: Y is superior in efficiency and ledger-ready organization.

Features from Y that should be preserved:

- canonical fact keys and dispositions;
- one accepted unit per underlying fact;
- explicit chapter and question mappings;
- compact period-fit, attribution-limit, and contrary-evidence fields;
- strict separation between accepted evidence and unresolved gaps.

## Félix Tshisekedi, 2022

**Better memo: X, by a clear but not overwhelming margin.**

### Memo X strengths

- It captures the central analytical contradiction: Tshisekedi had consolidated national political authority while remaining constrained by coalition bargaining, weak institutions, fragmented security forces, foreign intervention, armed groups, donors, and limited territorial control.
- The evidence is unusually broad without being purely enumerative. It covers the Sacred Union, institutional checks, the state of siege, repression, M23 and Rwanda, regional diplomacy, civilian protection, displacement, media, elections, macroeconomic management, education, health, corruption auditing, and Indigenous rights.
- It contains meaningful favorable evidence: macroeconomic stabilization, ending central-bank financing, free-primary-schooling continuity, election scheduling, audit activity, regional diplomacy, and Indigenous-rights legislation.
- It handles ruler attribution particularly well. Examples include distinguishing Tshisekedi’s direct responsibility for maintaining the state of siege from individual arrests, and separating responsibility for government responses from the external and inherited causes of eastern violence.
- Its strongest source work is authoritative and well inspected: Amnesty page references, MONUSCO paragraph references, the UN Group of Experts report and annex structure, IMF appraisal material, and the specific warning that the Gécamines audit did not date the disputed transfers.
- The memo repeatedly separates enactment or program adoption from implementation and outcomes. ER11, ER13, ER14, and ER16 are especially disciplined on this point.
- Its bias analysis is tailored to the DRC rather than generic: inaccessible conflict zones, repeated displacement, overlapping armed actors, incomplete state records, pluralistic but coercive media, Rwanda–DRC narrative conflict, and donor/Kinshasa-centered coverage.
- The final research priorities correctly move away from more country summaries toward Congolese budgets, audits, court records, parliamentary votes, CENI records, and implementation data.

### Memo X defects

- A few records use weak or indirect sources where primary material should eventually replace them. The election record relies on a 2023 CENI audit notice; the Indigenous-rights law relies on HRW; the Gécamines finding relies on a Reuters account of an unpublished audit.
- ER14’s strongest source is a World Bank announcement about intended investments and priorities. It cannot substantiate the broad health-system claims without the WHO and UNICEF reports that remain uninspected.
- ER13 relies on a World Bank project blog, which is useful but institutionally interested and not an impact evaluation.
- Some exact locator descriptions are questionable or interface-dependent, such as Reuters “paragraphs 426–442” and Amnesty “paragraphs 428–442.” Page and section references are more durable.
- The memo sometimes combines distinct facts into one record. ER05 combines M23’s military resurgence, civilian abuses, Rwandan support, and implications for Tshisekedi’s authority; these should be separate canonical facts downstream.
- The evidence base remains concentrated in UN, donor, and international advocacy institutions. The memo recognizes the absence of Congolese civil-society and administrative records, but does not solve it.
- The length is mostly productive, though some inherited context and repeated cautions could be compressed.

### Memo Y strengths

- As with the Putin memo, the unitized format is efficient and well suited to a cumulative evidence ledger.
- Ruler-attribution discipline is excellent. It explicitly states that no personal-integrity nexus was established and does not transform Kamerhe’s case, institutional corruption, or associates’ conduct into findings against Tshisekedi.
- The World Bank governance-operation records add useful material that X does not extract as clearly: prior actions, debt disclosure, mining royalties, telecom reform, SOE transparency, forest governance, and the limitations of those reforms.
- The oil-and-gas auction record is a credible additional policy fact absent from X and provides a useful adverse counterpart to the Indigenous-rights law.
- The CPJ record usefully distinguishes threats from M23 from threats attributable to the state, improving information-environment attribution.
- The memo is highly efficient and has comparatively strong duplication control.

### Memo Y defects

- It under-researches the year’s principal security and foreign-policy issues. It lacks extracted UN Group of Experts evidence on Rwandan support and FARDC cooperation with armed groups, detailed MONUSCO reporting, regional diplomacy, foreign-force deployments, and the implementation of the state of siege.
- The source-family claim is broader than the visible evidence. It says UN Security Council, RSF, Congo Research Group/Ebuteli, and other sources were opened, but no substantive accepted units from several of those families appear.
- Eastern-conflict evidence relies heavily on a single HRW country chapter, sometimes with HRW itself relaying UN data. That is less authoritative and less diverse than X’s use of MONUSCO and UN expert reports.
- It provides little material on education, health, electoral preparations, media pluralism, coalition operation during 2022, or anti-corruption auditing.
- Its information-environment discussion is sound but short. It lacks X’s detailed analysis of inaccessible territory, repeated displacement, weak national statistics, coercion of witnesses, donor measurement bias, and topic-specific undercoverage.
- Some locators again appear tied to an extraction interface rather than stable documents.
- The methodology mappings are more precise than Putin Y’s wildcards, but several units are mapped very broadly across chapters without demonstrating that each mapping has a distinct evidentiary use.

### Prompt decision

The difference is large enough to justify changing toward X’s prompt for future reconnaissance. X produces a materially more complete foundation for later chapter researchers, especially on conflict, institutional authority, social policy, and evidence bias.

The following Y features should nevertheless be preserved:

- ledger-ready canonical units;
- the explicit “no personal-integrity nexus” safeguard;
- bounded government-level attribution for donor-program reforms;
- extraction of specific governance prior actions and debt-disclosure measures;
- the oil-and-gas auction as contrary environmental evidence;
- differentiation between state and non-state threats to journalists.

# Overall recommendation

Adopt the prompt behind Memo X as the research-content baseline, but require Memo Y’s structured evidence-unit output.

The best combined prompt would require:

1. Broad but relevance-controlled discovery across all major governing domains.
2. One canonical fact per accepted record.
3. Stable page, paragraph, section, article, or operative-clause locators.
4. Explicit separation of opened evidence, corroboration, and uninspected leads.
5. Period fit, ruler nexus, authority limits, contrary evidence, and source bias for every record.
6. A compact deduplicated ledger plus a short domain-gap and information-environment analysis.
7. A stopping rule once new searching repeats existing facts.

Across both rulers, X found more evidence that later researchers could actually use. Y organized a smaller body of evidence more efficiently. The prompt should change toward X’s investigative depth while preserving Y’s canonicalization, compactness, and attribution discipline.
