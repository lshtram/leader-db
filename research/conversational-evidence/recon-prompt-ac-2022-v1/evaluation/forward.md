Scores assess the research artifacts as reconnaissance inputs. They do not score the rulers, prose quality, or raw quantity of material.

### Memo X

Concrete strengths:

- It has excellent fact-level discipline. Each accepted unit has a canonical fact key, source, locator, period-fit statement, attribution limit, and contrary evidence.
- It distinguishes direct presidential conduct from system-level outcomes particularly well. Examples include:
  - Putin’s invasion announcement establishes ownership of the announcement and stated rationale, but not the truth of that rationale.
  - The mobilization decree establishes a personal legal act, but not the quality of every regional call-up.
  - UN findings establish conduct by Russian forces without claiming proof of Putin’s personal orders, knowledge, or criminal responsibility.
- It relies on a strong core of primary or institutionally authoritative materials: the Constitution, Kremlin address, official mobilization decree, UN resolution, UN investigations, and World Bank reporting.
- Duplication control is unusually good. The UN Commission’s general war-crimes finding and OHCHR’s northern-Ukraine killing evidence are retained as distinct facts rather than presented as two independent confirmations of an identical proposition.
- The chapter-gap list is concise and operational. It correctly identifies personal integrity, decision process, mobilization administration, distributional effects, and final economic outcomes as unresolved.
- It delivers substantial reusable evidence with little excess volume.

Concrete defects:

- Favorable or competence-related evidence is thin. The only substantial favorable qualification is that the economic contraction was smaller than forecast. There is little extraction concerning financial stabilization, military adaptation, retained territorial control, state-service continuity, or policies that worked on their own terms.
- Some “contrary evidence” fields contain official rationalizations rather than contrary empirical evidence. For example, the authorities’ characterization of repression as enforcement of public-order or false-information laws does not independently contradict evidence of repression.
- Several locators use browser-specific “lines” without durable section or paragraph identifiers. These are weaker than decree provisions, report paragraphs, PDF pages, or formal document symbols.
- The constitutional source is described as amended through 2014 even though 2020 amendments were relevant to the 2022 institutional framework. The cited provisions may remain applicable, but the version issue should have been resolved.
- The Time analysis adds useful constraint context, but is appreciably weaker than the official and UN materials and rests partly on expert interpretation and intelligence claims.
- Domestic repression evidence is concentrated in organizations with overlapping rights-monitoring perspectives—HRW, Freedom House, OVD-Info, and Memorial. They are useful, but not wholly independent measurements of separate facts.
- Economic, social-service, institutional, and military-effectiveness coverage remains too narrow for all eight chapters.

### Memo Y

Concrete strengths:

- It discovers substantially more material evidence, especially:
  - the failed initial campaign and later territorial reversals;
  - systematic torture, sexual violence, deportations, and child transfers;
  - attacks on Ukraine’s energy infrastructure;
  - journalist exile and censorship effects;
  - the final 2022 GDP estimate and mechanisms of stabilization;
  - an independently modeled military-death estimate;
  - the absence of evidence for a general domestic service collapse.
- It handles balance better than X. The dossier credits Russia’s better-than-expected macroeconomic resilience, absence of banking collapse or mass unemployment, continued state services, retained territory, ability to sustain the war, and mobilization’s contribution to stabilizing parts of the front.
- Its information-environment section is strong and specific. It addresses censorship, casualty secrecy, economic-statistical restrictions, polling distortions, unequal UN access, strategic incentives of Ukrainian and Western sources, and the relative neglect of ordinary domestic administration.
- It usually calibrates attribution carefully. The distinction between strong attribution for strategic decisions and shared responsibility for military implementation is particularly useful.
- Retrospective evidence is explicitly segregated. This prevents 2023 reports from being mistaken for contemporaneous 2022 knowledge.
- The deeper-research questions are well targeted toward decision process, operational aims, policy mechanisms, distribution, command responsibility, polling validity, and documented ownership.
- It candidly identifies uninspected leads instead of silently treating discovery as evidence.

Concrete defects:

- Some records overstate inspection quality:
  - E02 calls the Kremlin address the strongest opened source even though the official page produced a retrieval error and the text was examined through an indexed transcript.
  - E03 relies chiefly on Freedom House for battlefield aims and reversals while listing specialist operational studies only as future leads.
  - E06 relies on a graphic summary rather than the Commission’s complete legal report.
- Locator quality is inconsistent. Phrases such as “sections concerning,” “country summary,” “methodology section,” and “passages reporting” are much less durable than X’s document provisions and page-specific locators.
- A few claims exceed the cited source:
  - The assertion that the coordinated energy campaign was “unlikely” without top authorization is a reasonable inference, but not a documented personal nexus.
  - The account of stabilization mechanisms in E12 appears broader than the quoted World Bank country-summary support.
  - E14’s wide patronage-network characterization is not established by a Treasury sanctions release alone.
- Several records depend repeatedly on Freedom House or HRW. Separate reports from the same organization, or reports that reproduce the same monitoring inputs, should not be counted as fully independent confirmation.
- E15 is a weak evidence record. A current WHO data page with lagged indicators does not substantiate a specific assessment of Russian health and education performance in 2022.
- The source and lead lists are useful, but their volume partially dilutes the compact research core. A downstream researcher must distinguish extracted evidence, corroboration, and merely promising material carefully.
- The dossier contains more interpretive synthesis than X. Much of it is sound, but that increases the risk that later researchers inherit conclusions whose exact documentary basis is less precise than the surrounding format suggests.

### Verdict

**Better memo: Memo Y, narrowly.**

The difference is real but not large. Y is more useful for full-spectrum downstream research because it adds meaningful favorable evidence, later-but-relevant outcome evidence, military-performance material, casualty transparency, energy attacks, and a substantially stronger bias analysis. X remains better at atomic evidence control, durable reuse, attribution restraint, and efficiency.

**Is the difference large enough to justify changing the prompt?** Yes, if the prompt change produced Y’s broader balancing, explicit retrospective-evidence segregation, and deeper information-environment analysis. The improvement is modest, so it does not justify adopting Y’s full verbosity or looser locator standard wholesale.

Features from X that should be preserved:

- canonical fact keys and atomic ledger units;
- explicit disposition fields;
- precise period-fit and attribution-limit fields;
- separation of related UN findings into distinct underlying facts;
- short chapter-specific gap inventory;
- compactness and low repetition;
- refusal to turn state or force-level findings into personal criminal attribution.

### Memo X

Concrete strengths:

- It establishes the key authority frame efficiently: Tshisekedi’s power had increased after formation of the Sacred Union, while coalition heterogeneity, weak institutions, foreign intervention, armed groups, and limited state reach continued to constrain him.
- Attribution is consistently careful:
  - conflict fatalities are not assigned personally to Tshisekedi;
  - the IMF’s macroeconomic findings are credited to the authorities collectively;
  - World Bank prior actions are not treated as proof of implementation or presidential integrity;
  - the Kamerhe case is retained as institutional context without inferring presidential interference;
  - threats by M23 are correctly separated from government conduct.
- It finds a useful mix of adverse and favorable material: emergency-rule abuses and security deterioration alongside macroeconomic stabilization, debt disclosure, governance prior actions, and the Indigenous-rights law.
- Its extraction from the World Bank governance-financing document is especially useful. The memo distinguishes completed prior actions from weak baselines and downstream results.
- It correctly warns that eastern-conflict figures combine numerous perpetrators and that Rwanda’s denial must be retained alongside UN and independent findings.
- The Chapter 7B restraint is exemplary: no personal-integrity finding is claimed without a direct personal nexus.
- The gap list is concise, chapter-specific, and actionable.

Concrete defects:

- It under-researches several major domains that Y covers materially:
  - the precise scale and mechanics of Sacred Union dominance;
  - the number and scrutiny of state-of-siege extensions;
  - M23 and Rwandan support through the UN Group of Experts;
  - regional diplomacy and foreign-force choices;
  - national democratic-space violations outside the emergency provinces;
  - election preparation;
  - primary education;
  - health and social-policy capacity;
  - the Gécamines audit.
- Source diversity is adequate but somewhat narrow. HRW supplies six of the twelve records or major parts of them, including several distinct topics from the same country chapter.
- The displacement and conflict records partly reuse the same HRW underlying source. Their separation is logically valid, but they should not be counted as independent corroboration.
- Some locators again rely on extraction-interface line numbers. The World Bank PDF page locators are much stronger than “lines 60–68” on a changing web page.
- The information-environment analysis is directionally strong but brief. It does not examine weak government records, delayed statistics, donor dependence, witness retaliation, repeated displacement, or the unequal visibility of mining and conflict compared with routine services.
- The presidency biography confirms officeholding but is not the most useful source for the precise constitutional allocation of presidential powers.
- The oil-and-gas record is credible policy evidence, but its environmental implications remain prospective; it contributes less to immediate 2022 ruler evaluation than several omitted governance and service-delivery topics.

### Memo Y

Concrete strengths:

- It adds several high-value underlying sources rather than merely more commentary:
  - Amnesty’s detailed state-of-siege investigation;
  - the UN Group of Experts’ report on M23 and Rwandan involvement;
  - two specific MONUSCO/Secretary-General reports;
  - CENI’s official election-process record;
  - the IMF Article IV report;
  - a Reuters report based on an unreleased IGF audit.
- Underlying-source inspection is generally strong. Report symbols, pages, paragraphs, annexes, interview counts, methodology cautions, and documentary limitations are frequently supplied.
- It distinguishes inherited weakness, direct presidential decisions, operational responsibility, and external constraints exceptionally well.
- Its treatment of emergency rule is especially valuable: it connects the original presidential decision, repeated extensions, military replacement of civilian authorities, weak parliamentary scrutiny, documented abuses, and limited security effectiveness.
- It provides genuine favorable and mixed evidence:
  - regional diplomacy and negotiating channels;
  - limited security coordination improvements;
  - pockets of accountability through convictions;
  - movement toward the election calendar;
  - macroeconomic discipline;
  - continued free primary education;
  - outbreak response and social-policy ambitions;
  - the Indigenous-rights law;
  - stronger auditing activity, even though it exposed severe opacity.
- It treats discovery of suspected corruption as both evidence of institutional failure and evidence of oversight, avoiding a one-sided inference.
- Its information-environment analysis is excellent. It addresses access constraints, witness danger, weak records, donor-produced statistics, pluralistic but coercive media, competing narratives about Rwanda and the FDLR, and topic-selection bias.
- The priority questions direct later researchers toward original Congolese materials: official gazettes, parliamentary records, audits, budget execution, court records, CENI documentation, education outcomes, and health data.

Concrete defects:

- Several evidence records are not equally strong:
  - ER11 uses a May 2023 CENI document as retrospective support for late-2022 electoral steps. It is useful, but the original 2022 calendar and registration instruments would be preferable.
  - ER13 relies on a World Bank project blog rather than an independent evaluation.
  - ER14 relies on a World Bank partnership announcement describing needs and intended investments, not achieved 2022 health outcomes.
  - ER15 relies on Reuters’ access to an unreleased audit, so underlying inspection remains incomplete.
- Source repetition is substantial. Amnesty, Freedom House, HRW, MONUSCO, and the World Bank each support multiple records. The memo usually distinguishes the facts, but independent-source diversity is somewhat lower than the record count suggests.
- Some overview claims—such as influence over electoral, judicial, military, intelligence, and state-enterprise appointments or the exact number of recently removed governors—are not tied as clearly to evidence records as the main findings.
- The free-education record is important, but phrases such as “expanded access” need a precise 2022 enrollment or household-cost measure. The source itself is institutionally interested in the supported program.
- The anti-corruption record concerns older or undated underlying transfers. Its 2022 relevance lies primarily in the audit and accountability response, not necessarily in when the suspected losses occurred.
- Favorable social-policy material sometimes records goals, continuation, or financing rather than demonstrated outcomes. The memo usually warns about this, but later researchers must not convert those records into performance findings.
- The length is not inherently a defect, but some records could be consolidated. ER03 and ER04, for example, concern the same emergency-policy mechanism and principal Amnesty investigation; ER07 and ER08 similarly split closely related conflict consequences.
- The “independent support” lines sometimes name reports or bodies without showing that their relevant passages were fully extracted. These should remain corroboration pointers rather than additional evidence weight.

### Verdict

**Better memo: Memo Y.**

The advantage is larger than in the Putin comparison. Y supplies materially better coverage of emergency governance, M23 and Rwanda, regional diplomacy, democratic space, election preparation, education, health, auditing, and implementation constraints. It also uses more authoritative underlying UN material and offers a much stronger roadmap for original Congolese records.

**Is the difference large enough to justify changing the prompt?** Yes. The increase in research usefulness is substantial enough to favor the prompt that produced Y, provided it is tightened to require stronger evidence-record thresholds and more compact consolidation. This is not merely a gain in length: several of Y’s additions change what later researchers would know to investigate.

Features from X that should be preserved:

- atomic, machine-reusable evidence units and canonical fact keys;
- compact chapter mapping;
- explicit disposition and integrity caveats;
- concise differentiation of state, foreign, and non-state perpetrators;
- World Bank governance-reform and debt-disclosure extraction;
- the environmental and resource-policy records;
- strict refusal to infer Tshisekedi’s personal corruption from associates, institutional weakness, or unresolved cases;
- superior research-to-volume efficiency.

# Overall recommendation

Adopt the **Memo Y prompt as the base**, because it consistently produces broader material coverage, better favorable/adverse balance, stronger information-environment analysis, and more useful deep-research questions. The improvement is especially clear for Tshisekedi.

However, incorporate Memo X’s evidence-control requirements into that prompt:

1. Require atomic canonical facts with explicit disposition, period fit, ruler nexus, and attribution limits.
2. Require durable page, paragraph, section, provision, or document-symbol locators; browser line numbers alone should not suffice.
3. Separate fully inspected evidence from corroboration and merely discovered leads.
4. Do not count multiple reports from one organization—or multiple summaries of the same underlying monitoring data—as independent evidence.
5. Require interpretive inferences, such as presumed top-level authorization, to be labeled explicitly.
6. Preserve Y’s retrospective-evidence section and balanced treatment of successful, failed, inherited, and externally constrained outcomes.
7. Impose a consolidation rule so breadth does not produce redundant records or weak records based only on program announcements, blogs, or current data pages.

In short: **use Y’s research ambition and bias awareness, with X’s ledger discipline and efficiency.**
