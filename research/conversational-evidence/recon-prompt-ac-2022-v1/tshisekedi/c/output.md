## 1. Ruler and period overview

Félix Antoine Tshisekedi Tshilombo was president and head of state of the Democratic Republic of the Congo throughout 2022. He had taken office on 24 January 2019 after a disputed election and remained constitutionally responsible for national strategy, foreign affairs, senior security appointments, and appointing the prime minister and government.

By 2022, Tshisekedi was substantially more powerful than at the beginning of his presidency. He had broken with former president Joseph Kabila’s Common Front for Congo and assembled the Sacred Union, which claimed 391 of 500 National Assembly members. It supported Prime Minister Jean-Michel Sama Lukonde and generally approved presidential priorities. The presidency also had considerable influence over senior judicial, electoral, military, intelligence, and state-enterprise appointments.

His power was nevertheless uneven rather than total:

- Much of the eastern DRC remained beyond effective government control, with approximately 120 armed groups active and M23 capturing territory in 2022.
- Rwanda’s military support to M23, Ugandan and Burundian deployments, MONUSCO, and regional diplomacy sharply constrained Kinshasa’s options.
- The army, police, intelligence service, courts, and provincial governments were fragmented, poorly resourced, and vulnerable to patronage and corruption.
- The Sacred Union was a broad alliance of political entrepreneurs, not a cohesive presidential party; Tshisekedi depended on coalition figures and provincial networks.
- Parliament and courts possessed formal checking powers, but the governing majority, political interference, and weak judicial capacity greatly reduced their practical independence.
- Provincial assemblies could remove governors, while irregular transfers from the central government limited provincial performance. Twelve governors and two vice-governors had recently been dismissed amid national interference in provincial politics.
- The IMF, World Bank, foreign donors, and mining investors constrained economic choices through financing conditions and the DRC’s dependence on mineral exports.

The year’s central contradiction was that Tshisekedi had consolidated national political power while the state’s territorial reach, administrative capacity, public accountability, and civilian protection remained exceptionally weak.

## 2. Evidence records

### ER01 — Tshisekedi held the presidency with a dominant governing coalition

**Fact and significance.** Tshisekedi was president throughout 2022 and governed through the Sacred Union, which claimed 391 of 500 National Assembly seats. This gave him broad influence over legislation, the cabinet, emergency-rule extensions, and oversight, although the coalition’s ideological and organizational cohesion was weak.

**Strongest opened source.** *DRC: Justice and Freedoms Under Siege in North-Kivu and Ituri*, Amnesty International, May 2022, [direct PDF](https://www.amnesty.org/ar/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/AFR6254952022ENGLISH.pdf).

**Stable support.** Page 9, paragraphs accompanying notes 52–53: Amnesty identifies the Sacred Union as Tshisekedi’s ruling coalition, records its claimed 391 seats, and describes parliamentary approval of successive state-of-siege extensions as largely formal.

**Time status.** Governing condition in 2022, formed in 2020–21.

**Ruler connection.** **Very strong.** Tshisekedi created the coalition specifically to replace the Kabila-aligned parliamentary majority and provide his presidency with a governing base.

**Complication.** A claimed coalition majority is not the same as disciplined control. Congolese parties were highly personalistic, weakly financed, and prone to switching alliances.

**Caution.** Amnesty’s concern is emergency rule, not a comprehensive measurement of coalition discipline.

**Independent support.** BTI’s country report describes Tshisekedi’s creation of the Sacred Union and the personalistic, clientelist party system; Freedom House likewise records the replacement of the Kabila power-sharing government.

### ER02 — Formal checks existed, but institutional independence was weak

**Fact and significance.** Parliament, the Constitutional Court, ordinary courts, provincial governments, and the electoral commission formally limited presidential authority, but political manipulation, corruption, patronage, and low capacity weakened these checks. Courts were widely perceived as biased against opposition and civil-society actors, while government allies often enjoyed impunity.

**Strongest opened source.** *Freedom in the World 2023: Democratic Republic of the Congo*, Freedom House, 2023, covering 2022, [country report](https://freedomhouse.org/country/democratic-republic-congo/freedom-world/2023).

**Stable support.** “Rule of Law,” F1–F2: the judiciary is described as corrupt and subject to political manipulation; arbitrary arrest and prolonged pretrial detention remained common.

**Time status.** 2022, with deeply inherited institutional weaknesses.

**Ruler connection.** **Moderate to strong.** Tshisekedi did not create these structural weaknesses, but presidential appointments, the Sacred Union majority, and security agencies under the executive increased his influence over them.

**Complication.** Courts did sometimes prosecute soldiers, police, armed-group members, and senior political figures. Formal constitutional mechanisms remained available and opposition parties and civil society continued operating.

**Caution.** Freedom House is an expert assessment rather than a court-by-court empirical study.

**Independent support.** BTI 2022, pp. 11–13; MONUSCO report S/2022/892, paragraph 31, documents convictions of state personnel and armed-group members, showing limited pockets of accountability.

### ER03 — The state of siege concentrated presidential and military authority without restoring security

**Fact and significance.** The state of siege imposed by Tshisekedi in North Kivu and Ituri replaced civilian authorities with military officials and was extended repeatedly in 2022. By April it had received 22 consecutive extensions, without detailed public justification or clear security success. Parliament’s large pro-presidential majority supplied little meaningful scrutiny.

**Strongest opened source.** *DRC: Justice and Freedoms Under Siege in North-Kivu and Ituri*, Amnesty International, May 2022, [direct PDF](https://www.amnesty.org/ar/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/AFR6254952022ENGLISH.pdf).

**Stable support.** Page 9, paragraphs 428–442; pages 10–11, discussion of blocked parliamentary questions and assessments left unanswered.

**Time status.** Inherited from Tshisekedi’s May 2021 decision, continued throughout 2022.

**Ruler connection.** **Very strong.** Tshisekedi declared and defended the measure; his government requested extensions and appointed military authorities, although Parliament formally approved them.

**Complication.** The government faced genuine mass-casualty threats from ADF, CODECO, M23, and many smaller groups. MONUSCO reported reinforcements, joint operations, and improved operational coordination in some areas.

**Caution.** Amnesty conducted 44 mostly remote interviews; insecurity impeded site access, and some government security information was confidential.

**Independent support.** UN Secretary-General report S/2022/252, paragraphs 6 and annex paragraphs 1.2; Human Rights Watch’s 2023 country chapter; Library of Congress legal summary.

### ER04 — Emergency rule was used to suppress critics

**Fact and significance.** Military and security authorities used emergency powers against activists, journalists, protesters, and legislators criticizing the state of siege. Examples included arrested provincial deputies, blocked parliamentary interventions, lethal force against protester Mumbere Ushindi, and nine months’ detention of 13 Lucha activists.

**Strongest opened source.** *DRC: Justice and Freedoms Under Siege in North-Kivu and Ituri*, Amnesty International, May 2022, [direct PDF](https://www.amnesty.org/ar/wp-content/uploads/2022/05/AFR6254952022ENGLISH.pdf).

**Stable support.** Pages 9–10, particularly paragraphs 437–442 and 464–498; note 63 records Jean-Paul Ngahangondi’s February 2022 arrest after criticism of military rule.

**Time status.** Partly inherited from 2021; continued directly in 2022.

**Ruler connection.** **Strong at policy level; moderate for individual abuses.** Tshisekedi maintained the legal regime and appointed military authorities, but evidence does not establish that he ordered each arrest or assault.

**Complication.** Some demonstrations occurred in an extremely violent environment, and authorities characterized certain restrictions as security measures. That does not explain arrests based solely on peaceful criticism.

**Caution.** Several individual accounts depend on interviews with affected politicians and activists, though Amnesty cross-checked them with legal and documentary material.

**Independent support.** Human Rights Watch, *World Report 2023*, “Freedom of Expression, Peaceful Assembly, and Media”; Freedom House, sections D4 and E1–E2.

### ER05 — M23’s resurgence became the leading war and foreign-policy crisis

**Fact and significance.** M23 launched its largest offensive in a decade, defeated or displaced government forces, occupied parts of North Kivu, and killed civilians. UN investigators found substantial evidence of direct Rwandan military support. The crisis exposed serious limits on Tshisekedi’s military control and shifted his agenda toward regional diplomacy and foreign deployments.

**Strongest opened source.** *Midterm Report of the Group of Experts on the Democratic Republic of the Congo*, UN Security Council, S/2022/967, 16 December 2022, [direct UN PDF](https://digitallibrary.un.org/record/3998599/files/S_2022_967-EN.pdf).

**Stable support.** Sections on M23/ARC and Rwandan Defence Force operations; supporting annexes distinguish interviews, imagery, documents, weapons evidence, and other material.

**Time status.** Directly concerns 2022.

**Ruler connection.** **Strong for government response, limited for conflict causation.** Tshisekedi controlled diplomacy and senior military policy but did not control M23 or Rwanda; the conflict had older regional roots.

**Complication.** Rwanda denied supporting M23 and cited the threat posed by the FDLR. UN experts and other reporting also identified cooperation between elements of the Congolese forces and armed groups, including FDLR elements.

**Caution.** Security sources sometimes have partisan incentives; parts of the experts’ evidentiary base must remain confidential to protect witnesses.

**Independent support.** UN Secretary-General report S/2022/892, paragraphs 20–24 and 28; Human Rights Watch, *World Report 2023*, paragraphs 78–85; HRW’s later witness investigation of M23 atrocities.

---

### ER06 — Regional diplomacy and foreign forces produced activity, but no decisive settlement

**Fact and significance.** Tshisekedi combined military operations with regional initiatives: the DRC joined the East African Community, accepted an EAC regional force, participated in Nairobi talks with armed groups, pursued the Luanda process with Rwanda, and continued joint FARDC–Ugandan operations against ADF. These measures did not end M23 advances or ADF attacks during 2022.

**Strongest opened source.** *Report of the Secretary-General on MONUSCO*, UN Security Council, S/2022/892, 1 December 2022, [direct PDF](https://monusco.unmissions.org/sites/default/files/sg_report_-_english_s-2022-892.pdf).

**Stable support.** Political and security sections; paragraphs 20–24 on M23 and regional initiatives; paragraph 32 onward on the humanitarian consequences.

**Time status.** Directly concerns 2022.

**Ruler connection.** **Very strong for diplomatic choices; moderate for results.** Presidential authority centrally shaped foreign and security policy, but foreign governments, armed groups, and regional institutions determined much of the outcome.

**Complication.** Regional coordination may have prevented further deterioration and created negotiating channels. MONUSCO also reported improved coordination among FARDC, Ugandan forces, and peacekeepers in some areas.

**Caution.** A UN peacekeeping report reflects MONUSCO’s institutional perspective and cannot fully evaluate confidential military decisions.

**Independent support.** Human Rights Watch, *World Report 2023*, paragraphs 80, 84–85; UN report S/2022/252, paragraphs 8–10 and annex 1.2.

### ER07 — Civilian protection deteriorated across the east

**Fact and significance.** Armed actors killed at least 2,446 civilians in Ituri, North Kivu, and South Kivu between January and late October 2022; the estimate included at least 155 civilians killed by Congolese security forces. Approximately 120 armed groups operated across four eastern provinces.

**Strongest opened source.** *World Report 2023: Democratic Republic of Congo*, Human Rights Watch, 2023, [country chapter](https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2023/country-chapters/democratic-republic-congo).

**Stable support.** “Attacks on Civilians by Armed Groups and Government Forces,” paragraphs 78–81, drawing principally on the Kivu Security Tracker.

**Time status.** Directly concerns 2022.

**Ruler connection.** **Moderate.** Civilian protection and security-force discipline fell within Tshisekedi’s authority, but most killings were committed by autonomous or foreign-backed armed groups amid long-standing state collapse.

**Complication.** The figures are documented minimums, not a national total. Some perpetrators were unidentified, and conditions made attribution difficult.

**Caution.** Kivu Security Tracker depends on local reporting networks and cannot observe every remote incident. UCDP event coding may classify the conflict differently; the supplied national indicator showing zero “internationalized conflict events” should not be read as proof that foreign forces were absent.

**Independent support.** MONUSCO S/2022/892, paragraphs 28–31; UN Group of Experts reports; Freedom House’s security discussion.

### ER08 — Conflict caused massive displacement and disrupted essential services

**Fact and significance.** The DRC had approximately 5.7 million internally displaced people by late 2022; about 1.5 million were displaced during the year, more than 80 percent by armed attacks and clashes. M23’s renewed fighting displaced about 280,000 people in Rutshuru after 20 October, including roughly 128,000 sheltering in schools, churches, hospitals, homes, and improvised sites.

**Strongest opened source.** *Report of the Secretary-General on MONUSCO*, S/2022/892, 1 December 2022, [direct PDF](https://monusco.unmissions.org/sites/default/files/sg_report_-_english_s-2022-892.pdf).

**Stable support.** Page 5, paragraphs 32–33.

**Time status.** 2022, atop a large inherited displacement crisis.

**Ruler connection.** **Moderate.** Protection, relief access, and military policy were governmental responsibilities, but foreign-backed and autonomous armed groups caused most new displacement.

**Complication.** Government, UN agencies, and humanitarian organizations provided assistance under severe access and funding constraints.

**Caution.** Displacement totals are estimates and can include repeated displacement of the same household.

**Independent support.** UNICEF’s eastern DRC emergency material; Freedom House, section D3; HRW’s M23 reporting.

### ER09 — Repression extended beyond the eastern emergency zones

**Fact and significance.** Security forces used excessive force against peaceful or predominantly peaceful political and labor protests in Kinshasa, including an April opposition sit-in concerning the electoral law and a September medical-union demonstration. UN monitoring later found a 49 percent increase in documented democratic-space violations over the preceding reporting period and an 84 percent increase in alleged violations by the intelligence agency.

**Strongest opened source.** *Report of the Secretary-General on MONUSCO*, S/2022/892, 1 December 2022, [direct PDF](https://monusco.unmissions.org/sites/default/files/sg_report_-_english_s-2022-892.pdf).

**Stable support.** Page 5, paragraph 30: protest-related deaths, mistreatment and arrests, and alleged illegal detention of more than 43 people by the Agence nationale de renseignements.

**Time status.** Directly concerns late 2022.

**Ruler connection.** **Moderate to strong.** National police and intelligence agencies fell within the executive security structure, but direct presidential orders have not been established.

**Complication.** Some anti-MONUSCO demonstrations became violent and attacked UN premises and personnel. Those incidents should not be conflated with peaceful opposition or labor demonstrations.

**Caution.** Percentage changes cover a MONUSCO reporting interval rather than the entire calendar year.

**Independent support.** HRW, *World Report 2023*, paragraphs 69–75; Freedom House, sections E1–E2.

---

### ER10 — Media pluralism survived, but intimidation and attacks increased

**Fact and significance.** The DRC retained numerous private and community outlets and open political discussion, but journalists faced arrests, threats, physical attacks, criminal defamation, and poor nationwide access. Congolese watchdog Journaliste en Danger reported 124 press-freedom violations through November 2022, 13 percent more than in 2021, and one journalist killed.

**Strongest opened source.** *Freedom in the World 2023: Democratic Republic of the Congo*, Freedom House, 2023, [country report](https://freedomhouse.org/country/democratic-republic-congo/freedom-world/2023).

**Stable support.** Media section, paragraphs 163–164.

**Time status.** Directly concerns 2022.

**Ruler connection.** **Moderate.** National security bodies and regulators operated under institutions influenced by the presidency, but perpetrators also included provincial officials and armed groups.

**Complication.** The media environment was genuinely diverse; citizens openly discussed corruption and insecurity, especially on social media. Radio Okapi provided a relatively independent nationwide service.

**Caution.** Freedom House relies here on JED’s monitoring; definitions of a “violation” and attribution should be checked in JED’s underlying annual report.

**Independent support.** HRW’s 2023 country chapter; MONUSCO S/2022/892, paragraph 30; Amnesty’s interviews with eastern journalists.

### ER11 — The government launched the 2023 election timetable, amid doubts about institutional independence

**Fact and significance.** On 26 November 2022, CENI scheduled presidential, National Assembly, provincial, and municipal elections for 20 December 2023. Voter registration began on 24 December 2022. This showed movement toward constitutionally scheduled elections, but disputes over CENI appointments, electoral-law consensus, funding, registration, and political influence left credibility concerns unresolved.

**Strongest opened source.** *External Audit of the Voter Register 2022–2023: Terms of Reference*, Independent National Electoral Commission (CENI), 10 May 2023, [official record](https://www.ceni.cd/communique-de-presse/2023/05/10/urgent-external-audit-voter-register-2022-2023-notice-call).

**Stable support.** Paragraphs 2–5: registration dates, election date and offices, audit requirement, and pooled government resources.

**Time status.** Official retrospective description of steps initiated in late 2022.

**Ruler connection.** **Moderate.** CENI was legally independent, but government financing, presidential appointments in the wider institutional system, and the Sacred Union’s dominance affected the political environment.

**Complication.** Publishing the calendar and starting registration were genuine implementation milestones. Some later election problems cannot automatically be projected backward onto every 2022 decision.

**Caution.** CENI was describing its own process and had an institutional interest in presenting it as lawful and technically coherent.

**Independent support.** Tshisekedi’s December 2022 state-of-the-nation summary; UN reports on the electoral roadmap; Freedom House’s assessment of electoral manipulation.

### ER12 — Macroeconomic management improved, but growth remained mineral-dependent and noninclusive

**Fact and significance.** The IMF found an improved macroeconomic environment, prudent policy, and an end to central-bank financing of government. Mineral production supported strong growth and reserves, but inflation, war spending, commodity dependence, weak governance, and poor infrastructure limited benefits to ordinary households.

**Strongest opened source.** *Democratic Republic of the Congo: 2022 Article IV Consultation and Second ECF Review*, IMF Country Report 22/210, 5 July 2022, [report page and PDF](https://www.imf.org/en/publications/cr/issues/2022/07/05/democratic-republic-of-the-congo-staff-report-for-the-2022-article-iv-consultation-second-520400).

**Stable support.** Report summary and staff appraisal; summary paragraphs identify the halt to central-bank government financing and the need for governance, natural-resource-management, business-climate, and diversification reforms.

**Time status.** Primarily 2021–mid-2022 results and 2022 projections.

**Ruler connection.** **Strong for national fiscal and reform policy; limited for mineral-price effects.** The presidency and Sama Lukonde government supported the IMF program, but global copper and cobalt demand supplied much of the growth.

**Complication.** GDP expansion should not be equated with broad welfare improvement. The supplied local background places nominal GDP per capita at about $688, while the World Bank reported that three in four residents lived in poverty.

**Caution.** IMF reviews emphasize program compliance and macroeconomic aggregates; household conditions, informal activity, and conflict areas are measured poorly.

**Independent support.** World Bank 2022–26 Country Partnership Framework; Tshisekedi’s state-of-the-nation account of revenue and growth.

### ER13 — Free primary education remained a major, partly successful presidential policy

**Fact and significance.** Tshisekedi’s flagship abolition of public-primary-school fees, begun in 2019, remained in operation in 2022 and expanded access for children whose families previously paid fees. Its sustainability and quality were undermined by hidden charges, teacher strikes, payroll weaknesses, fraud, overcrowding, insecurity, and inadequate teaching resources.

**Strongest opened source.** Jean-Christophe Carret and Scherezad Latif, *Free Primary Schooling in the DRC: Progress Despite the Perils*, World Bank, 27 June 2022, [article](https://blogs.worldbank.org/en/nasikiliza/free-primary-schooling-drc-progress-despite-perils).

**Stable support.** Paragraphs 43–49: continued implementation, household benefit, hidden costs, teacher-pay problems, education-sector fraud, and an $800 million system-strengthening project.

**Time status.** Policy inherited from Tshisekedi’s 2019 initiative and active in 2022.

**Ruler connection.** **Very strong for policy adoption and continuation; moderate for operational outcomes.**

**Complication.** Enrollment or fee removal does not establish learning gains. Conflict closed or repurposed schools, and system expansion may have reduced quality.

**Caution.** This is a World Bank project blog, not an independent impact evaluation; it has a favorable institutional perspective.

**Independent support.** World Bank Country Partnership Framework, February 2022; Freedom House’s account of conflict-related school disruption; later World Bank project data reporting substantially higher public-primary enrollment.

### ER14 — Health and social-policy goals were ambitious, but service capacity remained extremely weak

**Fact and significance.** Universal health coverage and stronger social protection were declared government priorities, but in 2022 the country faced Ebola outbreaks, cholera, measles, polio and other epidemics alongside malnutrition, displacement, low service coverage, and severe regional inequality. International agencies continued providing a large share of emergency services.

**Strongest opened source.** *World Bank Deepens Commitment to Building Human Capital and Inclusive Development in the Democratic Republic of Congo*, World Bank, 22 February 2022, [official release](https://www.worldbank.org/en/news/press-release/2022/02/22/world-bank-deepens-commitment-to-building-human-capital-and-inclusive-development-in-the-democratic-republic-of-the-congo).

**Stable support.** Paragraphs 146–152: poverty, weak governance, fragile access to water and electricity, social-protection expansion, education, reproductive health, and women’s economic inclusion.

**Time status.** Direct 2022 development baseline and forward program.

**Ruler connection.** **Strong for setting policy priorities; moderate to weak for short-term outcomes**, many of which depended on provincial administration and foreign funding.

**Complication.** Government and partners successfully contained some outbreaks and continued education and vaccination services despite extreme conditions.

**Caution.** The World Bank release describes an approved partnership and intended investments, not completed government results. WHO and UNICEF country reports should be extracted before making precise national outcome claims.

**Independent support.** WHO, *Annual Report 2022*, health-emergency section; UNICEF DRC Country Office Annual Report 2022; UNICEF’s September 2022 Sankuru cholera response.

### ER15 — Anti-corruption auditing exposed large losses but did not establish systemic accountability

**Fact and significance.** A May 2022 Inspectorate General of Finance audit found that, of more than $591 million in advances and loans Gécamines said it had paid to the state, only $178 million could be traced in Treasury accounts. The audit presumed that approximately $413 million had been misappropriated; a further $175 million signing bonus was also untraceable. This demonstrated stronger audit activity but also continuing opacity in the mining-state nexus.

**Strongest opened source.** Reuters, *Congo auditor says $400 million went missing from state mining company*, 4 June 2022, [republication of opened Reuters report](https://www.investing.com/news/stock-market-news/congo-auditor-says-400-million-went-missing-from-state-mining-company-2833518).

**Stable support.** Paragraphs 426–442, especially 432–437.

**Time status.** Audit issued in 2022; the underlying transfers were not dated in the leaked report and may span earlier governments.

**Ruler connection.** **Mixed.** Tshisekedi had replaced Gécamines leaders associated with earlier allegations and empowered an inspectorate reporting to the presidency. The missing funds cannot be attributed personally to him, and Reuters noted that the audit did not specify when the payments occurred.

**Complication.** Gécamines denied longstanding corruption allegations; the audit investigation was continuing. Discovery of suspected losses is evidence of oversight as well as evidence of institutional failure.

**Caution.** The underlying IGF report was not publicly released and was seen by Reuters; researchers should obtain the full audit and Treasury response before assigning responsibility.

**Independent support.** IMF 2022 Article IV’s demands for better natural-resource governance; Freedom House’s finding that corruption remained endemic.

### ER16 — A meaningful Indigenous-rights reform was enacted

**Fact and significance.** In November 2022, Tshisekedi signed legislation protecting and promoting the rights of Indigenous Pygmy peoples. This was a concrete rights-related achievement in a year otherwise dominated by insecurity and repression.

**Strongest opened source.** *World Report 2023: Democratic Republic of Congo*, Human Rights Watch, 2023, [country chapter](https://www.hrw.org/world-report/2023/country-chapters/democratic-republic-congo).

**Stable support.** Country chapter’s reform discussion records presidential signature of the law in November 2022.

**Time status.** Direct 2022 enactment.

**Ruler connection.** **Strong for enactment**, shared with Parliament; implementation depended on ministries, courts, provinces, and future budgets.

**Complication.** Enactment alone does not demonstrate land restitution, equal treatment, or effective enforcement.

**Caution.** The record should be supplemented with the official gazette text, legislative history, and Indigenous organizations’ assessment.

**Independent support.** Tshisekedi’s state-of-the-nation presentation; later UN and rights-organization references to the law.

## Later retrospective evidence

These findings help interpret 2022 but should not be treated as contemporaneous proof:

- The Carter Center’s final report on the December 2023 elections found serious transparency, tabulation, procedural, and incumbency-advantage problems. This strengthens concerns about institutions prepared during 2022, but it does not prove that every 2022 registration or scheduling decision was improper. [Carter Center final report](https://www.cartercenter.org/resources/pdfs/news/peace_publications/election_reports/drc/drc-final-report-2023.pdf)
- Later World Bank project reporting suggests that free-primary-schooling reforms substantially increased public-school enrollment in selected provinces. These are useful outcome measures, but researchers must verify baselines, geographic coverage, and whether reported enrollment represented regular attendance and learning.
- Later investigations documented further M23 atrocities and continuing Rwandan support. They corroborate the trajectory visible in 2022 but should remain dated to their investigative periods.
- Tshisekedi’s 2023 decision to begin easing the state of siege supports the conclusion that it remained politically controversial and ineffective, but it does not substitute for 2022 evidence.

## 3. Information environment and evidentiary limitations

Reliable research on the DRC in 2022 is unusually difficult.

Conflict zones were inaccessible, telecommunications were weak, populations were repeatedly displaced, and witnesses faced retaliation from armed groups and state authorities. Fatality and displacement figures are therefore documented minimums or modeled estimates. Perpetrator attribution is especially difficult where FARDC units, foreign troops, local militias, and shifting coalitions operated in the same area.

Government records were incomplete and sometimes unavailable. The Gécamines audit illustrates the problem: even auditors could not reconcile company and Treasury records, while the full report was not public. National statistics are often delayed, donor-produced, or unable to measure informal and conflict-affected areas.

The information environment was pluralistic but coercive. Private and community media existed, Radio Okapi reached much of the country, and citizens openly debated corruption and insecurity. At the same time, journalists, activists, legislators, victims, and opposition supporters risked threats, detention, violence, or criminal accusations. Emergency rule particularly distorted reporting from North Kivu and Ituri.

Political polarization also affects evidence. The presidency characterized M23 as a Rwandan proxy and emphasized foreign aggression; Rwanda denied support and emphasized FDLR threats and Congolese cooperation with hostile armed groups. Both dimensions require investigation. Opposition claims about political courts or electoral capture can be well founded without every prosecution being fabricated.

International attention was highly unequal: M23 and strategic minerals received substantially more reporting than rural policing, health-system performance, provincial budgeting, gender-based violence outside prominent conflicts, or routine service delivery.

The evidence base is strongest for the eastern conflict, emergency rule, and macroeconomic policy; it is weaker for household welfare, health outcomes, provincial variation, nepotism, procurement, presidential finances, police violence outside major cities, and actual implementation of social reforms.

## 4. Priority questions for deeper research

1. Obtain the official gazette, parliamentary votes, and executive decrees for every 2022 state-of-siege extension.
2. Establish the presidential chain of command for FARDC, intelligence, and police abuses documented in 2022.
3. Extract the full UN Group of Experts findings on both Rwandan support to M23 and FARDC cooperation with FDLR or other armed groups.
4. Obtain the complete IGF Gécamines audit, Treasury reconciliation, named responsible officials, and subsequent prosecutions or recoveries.
5. Map Sacred Union membership, cabinet allocation, defections, and coalition bargaining as of each quarter of 2022.
6. Examine appointments involving Tshisekedi family members, close associates, and UDPS financiers; distinguish nepotism allegations from documented conflicts of interest.
7. Compare free-schooling enrollment, attendance, learning, class size, teacher pay, and household charges across provinces.
8. Extract 2022 health-budget execution, vaccination, maternal-health, outbreak, and universal-health-coverage data.
9. Reconstruct CENI appointments, procurement, government transfers, electoral-law amendments, and objections made during 2022.
10. Assess the 145-territory local development program: 2022 disbursement, procurement, completed infrastructure, and audit findings.
11. Compare declared budget priorities with actual 2022 expenditure, particularly defense, elections, education, health, and presidential institutions.
12. Obtain Congolese civil-society, women’s-rights, Indigenous, trade-union, provincial, and victim-group assessments to offset Kinshasa- and donor-centered reporting.
