## Evidence-source continuity

“Observable” describes the claim being examined, not a class of privileged sources.
The researcher continues to use the full source ecology from the earlier process:

- books, biographies, histories, and other long-form syntheses;
- peer-reviewed and other credible academic work;
- NGO, civil-society, international-organization, commission, and think-tank reports;
- investigative, specialist, national, local, and international journalism;
- expert analysis and contemporaneous archives;
- legislation, budgets, administrative records, appointments, transcripts, courts,
  audits, inquiries, statistics, and structured datasets.

Secondary and synthesis sources are often the best starting point because they identify
which acts mattered, reveal informal behavior, interpret technical records, connect
events, and challenge official accounts. Primary records are not automatically more
truthful, independent, complete, or relevant.

## Proportional collection rule

The researcher does not review every law or attempt a census of all observable acts.
For each chapter it should:

1. use strong overviews and syntheses to identify the consequential decisions,
   controversies, programs, institutions, and outcomes;
2. reuse already accepted evidence and determine the material unresolved themes;
3. inspect specific primary records when they establish a consequential claim, resolve
   disagreement, improve ruler attribution, or test implementation;
4. seek important favorable, adverse, contrary, and underrepresented perspectives;
5. stop when additional searching mainly repeats the established account and is
   unlikely to change material findings or confidence.

Sampling must remain balanced. A representative record should include major conduct,
important exceptions, and evidence capable of disproving the initial account. Missing
minor acts are not a failure; omission of a consequential contrary case is.
